The Last Pot of Lemon Balm

By half past four, the rain had softened everything beyond the greenhouse glass. The hills were still there, Neville knew, but they had withdrawn into a pleasant grey distance. Water gathered on the sloping panes and slipped down in crooked lines. Somewhere beneath his workbench, a terracotta saucer received a slow, dependable drip.

There were six pots of lemon balm on the bench.

There should, according to the list beside them, have been five.

Neville counted them again, although counting had never improved a situation involving an extra plant. One for the hospital wing, one for the library, one for the staffroom, one for the little room where visiting lecturers stayed, and one for the entrance hall, where the draughts troubled almost everything except lemon balm.

The sixth had no destination.

He turned its label over. On the back, in his own handwriting, were the words: Find a window.

“Oh, very helpful,” Neville told his earlier self.

The plant gave no answer. It was a good specimen, round and healthy, with pale new leaves at its centre. When he brushed one with his thumb, the clean scent rose immediately, bright without being sharp.

He remembered taking the cuttings in June. A surprising number had rooted, and he had decided that Hogwarts might benefit from a few plants which did nothing more demanding than smell nice.

Most of the things he grew had duties. They were ingredients, antidotes, teaching specimens, or examples of what happened when someone ignored instructions. Lemon balm asked only for light, water and an occasional trim. Neville had liked the idea of putting it where somebody might appreciate it without needing to do anything useful with it.

Now it was Friday, the last lessons were over, and the plants had spent three days waiting to be delivered.

He had promised himself he would finish before supper.

Neville rinsed his hands in the small stone sink and dried them on a towel that had once been yellow. Then he drew his wand and gave it a careful little turn.

Five pots rose from the bench.

The sixth remained where it was.

Neville looked at it.

“Yes, all right. You too.”

The last pot lifted, settled into line behind the others, and followed him towards the door.



The entrance hall smelled of wet wool, stone and something buttery from the kitchens.

Students came in from the grounds in cheerful, dripping groups. Neville kept the plants close to the wall as two second-years passed with a chessboard between them, the pieces arguing about whether the journey counted as part of the game.

He put the first pot on a broad windowsill beside the doors.

It looked rather small.

Neville moved it six inches to the left, then back again.

Professor McGonagall, coming down the marble staircase with a sheaf of parchment, watched this adjustment without comment.

“Good afternoon, Longbottom,” she said at last.

Neville straightened. Even after several years of teaching, he still sometimes felt that he ought to explain why he was standing in a corridor.

“Professor. I’m bringing these round.”

“I had gathered that,” McGonagall said. She examined the pot. “Lemon balm?”

“Yes. It should manage the draught.”

“A useful qualification for anything living in this hall.”

Neville smiled and tucked the label so that it faced the room.

McGonagall glanced at the five pots hovering patiently behind his shoulder.

“Have you included the library?”

“And the hospital wing, the staffroom and the visitors’ room,” Neville replied. “Then there’s one left.”

“An unexpected abundance,” McGonagall observed.

“I thought I’d find somewhere for it.”

She shifted the parchments under her arm and touched a leaf with one finger. Her expression changed very slightly as the scent reached her.

“My mother used to grow this beside the back step,” McGonagall said. “She maintained that it improved a wet afternoon.”

“Did it?”

McGonagall considered the blurred windows.

“I believe it helped.”

A student called a question from the foot of the stairs. McGonagall answered it, gave Neville a small nod, and went on towards her office.

Neville stayed beside the sill for a moment.

Already, a passing girl had slowed to read the label. She touched a leaf, smelled her fingers and held them out to her friend.

That seemed a reasonable beginning.



By the time Neville reached the hospital wing, the rain had become a quiet tapping behind the curtains.

The matron was busy at the far end of the room, so he left the second pot in the place they had agreed upon: a sunny sill near the cupboard of clean cups. He turned it until its fullest side faced the beds.

Then he put his watering instructions beneath the saucer.

When the soil feels dry. Please do not allow anyone to improve it with Pepperup Potion.

He had underlined the second sentence.

In the library, the next plant received a less uncomplicated welcome.

Neville set it down on the returns desk while he moved a pile of books out of the way. Before he could retrieve it, Hermione Granger looked up from the table beyond.

“Neville?”

She had three open books, two closed ones and a Ministry folder arranged around her. Her hair was escaping from its clip in the manner it always did when she had forgotten about it.

“Hermione,” Neville said. “I didn’t know you were here.”

“I came up to look at some old school records. There are references to educational grants that never made it into the Ministry archive.” Hermione closed one book with a finger marking her place. “I meant to finish an hour ago.”

Neville glanced at the remaining volumes.

“Going well, then?”

“Annoyingly well,” Hermione replied. “Every time I find what I need, it refers me to something else.”

She noticed the pot on the desk.

“Is that for the library?”

“Provided nobody objects.”

Hermione reached across and gently rubbed a leaf.

“Oh, that’s lovely,” she said. “My parents had some in the garden. It spread everywhere.”

“It does like to take an interest in neighbouring territory.”

“That sounds familiar.” Hermione looked down at the open books and smiled. “Where are you putting it?”

Neville indicated a tall window near the shelves of school histories.

Hermione followed his glance.

“Would it get more light by the other one?”

They carried the plant between them, though neither of them needed help with it. At the first window, an oak tree outside intercepted most of the afternoon. At the second, the glass was clear and the sill broad.

There was also a chair.

It had a rubbed wooden frame and a green cushion that had sunk comfortably in the middle.

“That’s better,” Neville said.

Hermione shifted a stack of periodicals to make space.

“People sit here while they’re waiting for books to be found,” she explained. “I sat here this morning. It was quite nice until I remembered what I was supposed to be doing.”

Neville placed the pot beside the chair.

He had imagined the library plant as something seen from a desk. Here, a person could rest a hand on the sill and find it close enough to touch.

Hermione tested the chair again, settling into it with a small, surprised sigh.

“I should have stayed here,” she said.

“You wouldn’t have reached the books.”

“That might have been an advantage.”

Neville looked back at her table. A cup stood among the papers, its tea long past steaming.

“Have you had anything since lunch?”

Hermione followed his gaze.

“I had most of that.”

“That wasn’t quite what I meant.”

“I know,” Hermione admitted.

Neville found himself laughing. It was pleasant to be able to ask her such a thing now. At school, he had generally assumed that Hermione had already considered every sensible question and was busy answering a more difficult one.

She rose and took her coat from the back of the chair.

“Ten minutes,” Hermione decided. “I can spare ten minutes. Are you going towards the staffroom?”

“Yes. With three plants.”

“Then I’ll come.”



They walked along a corridor where the windows reflected little gold flames from the lamps.

Hermione asked about the greenhouses, and Neville told her about a first-year class which had successfully repotted twenty-four seedlings and accidentally repotted one pupil’s shoe.

“Was anyone wearing it?” Hermione asked.

“Not by the end.”

“Then I’d call that a successful lesson.”

“He did ask whether he could leave it there until it grew a matching one.”

Hermione laughed, and the sound travelled ahead of them into the quiet passage.

She told him that Ron was spending the evening with George and had assured her that she needn’t hurry home. Neville asked whether she believed this had been entirely generous.

“I believe there was a new delivery at the shop,” Hermione replied. “And I believe I wasn’t meant to ask any further questions.”

They reached the staffroom just as a log settled in the fire.

Only McGonagall was there. She had exchanged her working parchments for a cup of tea, though a small stack of papers waited beside her chair.

“Miss Granger,” she said. “Have you escaped the library?”

“Temporarily, Professor.”

“A wise precaution.”

Neville placed the fourth pot on a low table near the south-facing window. Hermione sat down, accepted a cup and warmed both hands around it.

McGonagall watched Neville check the distance between the plant and the fire.

“Would a larger saucer be preferable?” she asked.

“This one should do. I’ve charmed the underside so it won’t mark the wood.”

“Excellent. The table has expressed its displeasure on that subject before.”

Neville glanced at a pale ring beside the saucer.

“It actually complained?”

“Only when Professor Flitwick put his cup down. A very small voice, but persistent.”

Hermione smiled into her tea.

Neville stood for another moment, wondering whether the pot needed turning. Then McGonagall pointed towards a chair.

“Sit down, Longbottom. The plant can be trusted to remain upright without supervision.”

He sat.

A cup floated over to him. The tea smelled faintly of bergamot, and someone had left a plate of shortbread within reach of all three chairs.

For a while, they talked about ordinary school matters. A window latch in the west corridor. The surprising popularity of the new porridge topping. Whether the suits of armour ought to be permitted to rehearse carols before December.

“No,” McGonagall said firmly.

“They’ve only got as far as humming,” Neville offered.

“That is how it begins.”

Hermione broke a piece of shortbread in half.

“How many plants have you got left?”

“Two,” Neville said. “One for your room, if you’re staying.”

“I am. I thought I’d start again in the morning, while the books are still out.”

“And one without a home.”

McGonagall looked towards the window.

“Have you tried your own rooms?”

Neville paused with his cup halfway to its saucer.

“I’ve got plants.”

“I should certainly hope so,” McGonagall replied.

“Quite a few, actually.”

“Then you are experienced in accommodating them.”

Hermione’s mouth curved slightly, but she said nothing.

Neville thought of the narrow sitting room adjoining his bedroom. There were essays on the table, seed catalogues beside the hearth and a pair of muddy boots underneath a chair. On the windowsill stood a tray of experimental seedlings which needed recording twice a day.

“I was thinking of somewhere people could enjoy it,” he said.

McGonagall reached for her tea.

“Yes,” she replied. “So was I.”



Hermione’s room was in a quiet passage overlooking the courtyard.

A fire had been laid, and her overnight bag stood on a low stool. Neville put the fifth plant by the window while Hermione drew the curtains halfway, leaving the wet courtyard visible between them.

The rain had stopped. Water gleamed on the paving, and the sky above the roofs was beginning to clear.

“This is perfect,” Hermione said.

Neville put a small instruction card beneath the saucer.

Hermione picked it up.

“‘Touch a leaf whenever required,’” she read. “That’s very straightforward.”

“I thought you might prefer it to a watering schedule.”

“I do know how to water a plant.”

“I know. But you might make a chart.”

Hermione considered protesting, then laughed.

“I might.”

Neville stood back. The pot filled an awkward space between the curtain and a little brass candlestick. In the fading light, its leaves looked softer than they had in the greenhouse.

Hermione brushed one as she passed.

“I’ll notice it when I open the curtains,” she said. “That’s a nice thought.”

Neville nodded. He had been thinking about how each plant would look when he left it, rather than what somebody might do beside it tomorrow.

Hermione unfastened her bag and took out a woollen cardigan.

“Will the last one be all right while you decide?”

“Oh, yes. It’s tougher than it looks.”

“I meant you carrying it about.”

Neville glanced at the remaining pot, which hovered at a patient angle beside the door.

“I ought to take it back before supper.”

“Will you come down to the Great Hall?”

“In a bit.”

Hermione folded her coat over the stool.

“I’ll save you a place.”



Outside, the air had the freshly washed smell that came after autumn rain.

Neville took the covered path towards the greenhouses. The last pot travelled beside him at elbow height. Without the other five, it looked less like part of a task and more like company.

He passed the dark kitchen garden, where broad leaves held shallow pools of water. Beyond it, the greenhouse roofs caught the first pale streak of evening.

He had been tired when he set out. He could feel it properly now: a heaviness across his shoulders, an ache in the hand he used for pruning, the particular weariness of having spoken clearly and encouragingly for several hours.

It had been a good day. That made the tiredness easier to overlook.

In the greenhouse, the saucer under the bench had nearly filled.

Neville emptied it, checked the latch on a high window and returned the lemon balm to the place where it had stood.

The gap beside it was surprisingly large.

He tidied the loose labels. He rinsed a pair of scissors. He put away a bag of grit which had been open since Tuesday.

Then he found the notebook.

It lay under a folded square of sacking, its green cover freckled with soil. Neville opened it at the page where he had recorded the June cuttings.

Six strong roots. Three weaker. Pot on separately.

Beside this, he had drawn a small square to remind himself of the parent plant’s position.

It had come from his grandmother’s garden.

Not a special plant. Not one she had planted for him. It had grown beside the old outside tap for as long as he could remember, surviving neglect, frost and an unfortunate attempt to scour the paving.

He had taken a cutting when he visited in spring because his sleeve had brushed it and brought the smell indoors.

His grandmother had noticed him sniffing his cuff.

“Take some if you want it, Neville,” she had said. “There’s enough of it.”

He had brought it back wrapped in damp newspaper.

Neville turned another page. There, between notes about compost and a reminder to order gloves, was a sentence he had forgotten writing.

One for the window at home.

He looked at the pot.

Then he sat down on the stool beside the bench.

He could remember what he had meant. He had been in his rooms on a bright June evening, making notes for the following year. The window had been open. Somewhere below, students had been crossing the lawn, talking too loudly to hear individual words.

He had thought that a little lemon balm would be pleasant there.

By the time the plants were ready, he had filled the sill with experiments.

Neville rested his hands on his knees. For a minute he listened to the greenhouse: the faint settling of pipes, a drop of water falling from a leaf, the almost inaudible movement of something growing in the far bed.

Then he closed the notebook.

“Well,” he said to the plant. “That makes things easier.”



He brought a small tray from the potting cupboard and fitted the saucer neatly into it.

The lemon balm needed no further preparation, but Neville removed one damaged leaf and loosened the paper label. On its clean side he wrote the date.

He considered adding instructions.

Instead, he put the pencil away.

Before leaving, he made his usual round. He checked the tender cuttings beneath their glass covers, lowered the warming charm over a bed of young seedlings and secured the door to the store cupboard.

At the last bench, he paused over the tray of experiments destined for his sitting-room window. They were ready to spend their nights in the greenhouse now. He had kept them upstairs because it had been convenient to check them after supper.

Neville cleared a place for them near the thermometer.

Then he took the lemon balm to the castle.

The windows of the Great Hall shone warmly across the grounds. Through the open doors, he could hear the comfortable noise of supper: cutlery, conversation, a burst of laughter that was quickly swallowed by the room.

For once, he carried the pot in his hands.



Hermione had saved him a place near the end of the staff table.

“How did you get on?” she asked as Neville sat down.

“Found a window.”

“Good.”

She passed him the potatoes without asking for more detail, which he appreciated.

The lemon balm sat on the floor beside his chair, safely out of the way. Neville ate roast vegetables, chicken and rather more bread than he had intended. As the warmth of the food reached him, he realised how hungry he had been.

Hermione described one of the records she had found: a fund established to help pupils buy winter cloaks, maintained for years by donations whose sources had never been properly entered.

Neville listened while she explained the difficulties of tracing it.

“Does it matter who gave the money?” he asked.

“For the report, probably. For the children who got the cloaks, I don’t suppose it did.”

“Perhaps there’s something in the governors’ minutes.”

Hermione looked at him.

“There might be.”

Neville recognised the expression.

“Tomorrow,” he suggested.

Hermione glanced towards the doors, then at her half-finished supper.

“Tomorrow,” she agreed.

They stayed until the candles had burned a little lower. Neither hurried over pudding. Neville found that, once he stopped thinking about the things waiting upstairs, it was possible to enjoy a bowl of stewed apple simply as a bowl of stewed apple.

At the staircase, Hermione touched the rim of the pot he carried.

“I hope it likes the window,” she said.

“It should. East-facing. Morning light.”

“Very well chosen, then.”

“Apparently I chose it in June.”

Hermione gave him a questioning look.

“I found a note,” Neville explained. “I’d meant to keep one.”

“I’m glad you remembered.”

Hermione wished him goodnight and turned towards the visitors’ passage. Neville watched her go, then climbed the last flight to his rooms.



His sitting room looked exactly as he had expected.

The boots were under the chair. The essays occupied most of the table. A gardening periodical lay open on the floor, where it had slipped several evenings earlier.

Neville set down the lemon balm and lit the lamps.

There was nothing particularly wrong with the room. It was warm, private and pleasantly quiet. He liked the old rug and the deep chair by the hearth. He had simply allowed every surface to become somewhere to put the next thing.

He carried the experimental seedlings to the door and sent them, with a carefully paced charm, down the empty staircase towards the greenhouses. Then he followed to make certain they reached their new bench.

When he returned, the windowsill was bare except for a fine scattering of soil.

Neville wiped it clean.

He brought the lemon balm across and placed it at the right-hand end, where he would see it from his chair. Its reflection appeared in the dark glass, a second little plant standing against the night.

He moved the essays into one pile. The periodical went beside the chair. His boots could stay where they were until morning.

That was enough.

Neville opened the window an inch.

Cool air entered, carrying the smell of wet grass. The clouds had separated above the grounds, and a few stars showed between them. Down by the greenhouses, the warming lights made small amber squares.

He touched a lemon balm leaf.

The scent reminded him of a tap dripping into a stone trough, a patch of gravel, his grandmother’s kitchen door standing open in spring.

He had been thinking of visiting on Sunday. He would write in the morning.

For now, he drew the curtains around the plant, leaving its leaves clear of the fabric, and put the kettle on.



The kettle had a habit of beginning to whistle before the water boiled. Neville had never quite corrected it. Tonight, he took it off the heat at its second, more convincing attempt and made an ordinary cup of tea.

He carried it to the chair.

For a little while, he read the gardening periodical. There was an article about pruning which he disagreed with in several minor respects, but he found himself too comfortable to compose objections.

The fire settled.

A clock ticked on the mantelpiece.

Neville lowered the periodical onto his knee and looked towards the curtains. He could see the round edge of the saucer beneath them.

He pictured the other pots in their places: one beside the hospital cups, one by the library chair, one waiting for Hermione to open her curtains. By Monday, somebody would probably have moved the staffroom plant to make room for a plate.

He would find out then.

His tea cooled to a comfortable warmth. He finished it, put the cup on the hearth and stood with a small stretch.

In the bedroom, he turned back the covers and opened the top drawer for his pyjamas. The sheets were cool when he first lay down, then gradually warmed around his feet.

Neville rested his head on the pillow.

The last pot was where he had wanted it.
