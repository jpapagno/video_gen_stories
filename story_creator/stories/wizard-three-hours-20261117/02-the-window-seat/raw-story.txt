The Window Seat

Hermione found the window seat because she had taken the longer way to breakfast.

She was staying at Hogwarts for three nights, in the spring of her twenty-sixth year, to help Professor McGonagall sort through several proposals for the school library. The work was agreeable and rather more complicated than the phrase “a few papers” had suggested. By Saturday morning, Hermione had decided that she ought to see something beyond the guest room, the headmistress’s study and the stretch of corridor connecting them.

A staircase brought her to the second floor instead of the first. She followed the smell of toast as far as a narrow gallery, where morning light lay across the flagstones.

Halfway along stood a deep window recess overlooking the inner courtyard. There was a wooden seat beneath it, wide enough for two people, with a thin cushion that had slipped sideways and curtains gathered into stiff, dusty bundles. On the sill sat a small brass candleholder, empty except for a grey curl of wick.

Hermione stopped.

Someone had left a book on the seat, face down.

She picked it up before the weight of its pages could do further damage to the spine. It was a library copy of *A Hundred Useful Household Charms*, open at a chapter about drying washing indoors. A narrow piece of blue wool marked the reader’s place.

“Ah,” Professor McGonagall said behind her. “You’ve discovered it.”

Hermione turned, keeping one finger between the pages.

“Is this someone’s usual place?” Hermione asked.

“Not that I’m aware of,” McGonagall replied. She regarded the cushion with the expression she generally reserved for an avoidable piece of untidiness. “Although I have been meaning to do something about it.”

“The seat?”

“The whole recess,” McGonagall said. “It gets excellent light, and very little passing traffic. At present, it also gets a draught from the left-hand corner.”

Hermione glanced at the faded cushion. Its filling had retreated into two hard ridges.

“It could be lovely,” she said.

“Yes,” McGonagall replied. “That is precisely what I keep thinking as I pass it on my way to something else.”

She took the book from Hermione, closed it carefully over the wool and set it on the sill.

“I had thought of asking you for an opinion,” McGonagall continued. “Though I ought to warn you that choosing a cushion may prove more troublesome than reviewing library regulations.”

Hermione smiled. “Who is the seat for?”

McGonagall looked at the empty place beneath the window.

“I haven’t decided,” she said.

***

They went to breakfast first.

The Great Hall was quieter on a Saturday, with students arriving in leisurely groups and the enchanted ceiling showing a pale sky streaked with white cloud. Hermione and McGonagall sat at one end of the staff table, where a pot of tea stood beneath a small knitted cosy.

McGonagall put marmalade on her toast.

“The recess used to contain a display of commemorative plates,” she said. “They were removed when one of them began giving unsolicited advice about posture.”

“Did anyone ask it to stop?” Hermione asked.

“Repeatedly,” McGonagall said. “It considered the requests evidence that its work was necessary.”

Hermione laughed into her tea.

Beyond the tall windows, a breeze stirred the tops of the trees. She had expected her visit to feel strange. Instead, Hogwarts had so far offered her a succession of small, practical matters: a sticking drawer, a misplaced report, an argument between two portraits over the ownership of a footstool. She found herself enjoying the manageable size of these problems.

“What did you have in mind for the window?” Hermione asked.

“A proper cushion, for a start,” McGonagall replied. “Curtains that can be closed without requiring considerable upper-body strength. Possibly a lamp.”

“And somewhere for a book,” Hermione added.

“There is a sill,” McGonagall said.

“For a book and a cup of tea?”

McGonagall considered this. “A small table, then.”

By the time they finished breakfast, Hermione had written a short list on the back of an old envelope.

McGonagall glanced at it as they rose.

“You have allotted a separate line to measuring,” she observed.

“It saves time later,” Hermione said.

“I am sure it does,” McGonagall replied, with enough dryness to make Hermione look up and smile.

They agreed to meet at the recess after McGonagall had dealt with her morning correspondence. Hermione went upstairs to collect her wand, a tape measure and the cardigan she had left over the back of her guest-room chair.

Before leaving, she put the library proposals into a neat pile.

For once, she felt no inclination to take them with her.

***

In full morning light, the recess looked both shabbier and more promising.

The window had three tall panes. Through the middle one Hermione could see the courtyard paving, a stone bench and a square of garden where pale green leaves were opening along the stems of a climbing rose. The left pane showed a drainpipe and a generous strip of sky. Through the right, a sloping roof led the eye towards the hills.

She measured the seat, making the tape hold itself straight across the wood.

The recess was deeper than she had realised. A person could sit facing the courtyard, or turn sideways with their feet tucked up. There was room at one end for a small table without obstructing the corridor.

Hermione knelt to examine the join beneath the left-hand window.

The draught touched her fingers immediately.

“Found it?” McGonagall asked, arriving with a large flat book beneath her arm.

“A gap in the frame,” Hermione replied. “The wood seems sound.”

“Good,” McGonagall said. “I should prefer not to begin a building project before lunch.”

Between them, they repaired the narrow opening and tested it with a feather from McGonagall’s pocket. The feather rested motionless on Hermione’s palm.

McGonagall then laid the book on the seat.

It had green cloth covers, rounded corners and a brass clasp. Across the front, in worn gold lettering, were the words *Furnishing Samples: North Rooms, Galleries and Landings*.

“I found this in a cupboard adjoining the staff room,” McGonagall said. “It appears to have been retained because nobody could decide which department it belonged to.”

Hermione released the clasp.

Inside were squares of fabric fixed to thick paper leaves. Some were scarcely larger than postage stamps; others unfolded into broad panels. Each had a handwritten label, and many had notes squeezed around the edges.

There were woollens, linens, velvets and things Hermione could not immediately identify. A purple square gave a faint shimmer when she touched it.

“Do be careful with page forty-three,” McGonagall said. “It sheds.”

“Lint?” Hermione asked.

“Opinions,” McGonagall replied.

Hermione turned a page.

A blue fabric waved gently beneath her fingers, although the air was still. Beside it someone had written, *Suitable for sea-facing rooms. Discouraging beside breakfast.*

She bent closer to read the next label.

The ordinary task of choosing a cushion had suddenly acquired possibilities.

***

They began with comfort.

McGonagall removed the old cushion with a careful Levitation Charm. Beneath it, the seat was made from broad oak boards, rubbed smooth in the middle and dusty at the edges. Hermione cleaned the wood while McGonagall examined the cushion’s seams.

“The cover can be repaired,” McGonagall said. “The filling has reached the end of its usefulness.”

“Could we use it elsewhere?” Hermione asked.

“In a draught stopper, perhaps.”

Hermione added that to her envelope.

They found several small things down the back of the seat: a broken quill, a sweet wrapper folded into a tiny square, and a green button with four holes. Hermione put the button on the sill in case its owner returned. The quill and wrapper went into a rubbish basket McGonagall summoned from an adjoining classroom.

Under the far corner of the cushion lay a narrow wooden ruler.

One side bore a neat set of inches. On the other, someone had carved, very faintly, *Return to library*.

Hermione held it up.

“I suppose this has been here for years.”

“Quite possibly,” McGonagall said. “The library has a remarkable ability to survive without the things people borrow from it.”

Hermione slipped it into her cardigan pocket.

They sat on the bare wood to test the height. McGonagall sat upright, hands folded on her lap. Hermione tried sitting normally, then turned sideways.

“A cushion needs to be thick enough to soften the boards,” Hermione said, “but not so thick that your feet hang above the floor.”

“And sufficiently firm that one can get up again,” McGonagall added.

They returned to the sample book.

Hermione favoured a moss-green wool with a close weave. McGonagall preferred a russet cloth that looked warm even before it was touched.

They laid both on the seat.

Against the oak, the russet became almost orange. The green went dull in the shadow beneath the sill.

“That’s disappointing,” Hermione said.

“Better to discover it now,” McGonagall replied.

They moved the samples into the sunlight, then back again.

Neither improved.

Hermione looked through the remaining pages with greater care. She had expected to choose a colour she liked and be done. Instead, the room seemed to have its own views, expressed through light and stone and the old honey-coloured wood.

Near the back of the book, she found a square of grey-blue wool.

At first it appeared unremarkable. Then she laid it on the seat.

The colour held.

***

A note beneath the grey-blue square read, *West landing. Wears well. Does not attract moths.*

“That seems encouraging,” Hermione said.

“Particularly the last point,” McGonagall replied.

Hermione unfolded the sample. It opened once, then again, becoming a piece large enough to cover half the seat. Fine darker threads ran through it, giving the blue a depth she had not noticed on the page.

She pressed her palm against it.

It was soft, but the weave felt sturdy.

“Would there be any of it left?” Hermione asked.

“The book claims to have an inventory,” McGonagall said.

They turned to the back.

There, in a pocket that had nearly come away from its stitching, lay a folded sheet. The ink had faded to brown, and several entries had been crossed out. Hermione found the grey-blue wool halfway down the list.

*Two lengths. Linen room, upper east passage. Shelf six.*

McGonagall glanced towards the corridor.

“I believe I know the room,” she said.

The upper east passage was warmer than the gallery, and smelled faintly of beeswax. Its linen-room door stood beside a portrait of a witch mending a sleeve.

McGonagall unlocked it.

Inside, shelves rose nearly to the ceiling. Folded blankets occupied one wall, while another held curtains arranged in stacks tied with cotton tape. There were bolster covers, bed hangings, bundles of braid and a large basket of wooden curtain rings.

Hermione stood just inside the doorway, delighted.

“There must be enough here to furnish half the castle.”

“There was once enough to furnish the whole of it,” McGonagall said. “I suspect some of these curtains remember doing so.”

They found shelf six behind a folding screen.

The blue wool was there, wrapped in plain linen. Hermione untied the tape and held a corner towards the door.

In the passage light, it looked exactly as it had beside the window.

Beneath it lay a roll of warm cream fabric, light enough to fall in soft folds. A label tied to the end read, *Curtaining. Washable. No enchantments.*

“That sounds restful,” Hermione said.

McGonagall touched the fabric between finger and thumb.

“Let us see it at the window,” she replied.

They carried the rolls back together, Hermione holding the blue and McGonagall the cream. For several minutes, neither spoke. Their footsteps and the quiet rustle of cloth were enough.

***

Neville found them shortly before lunch.

He came along the gallery carrying a shallow wooden box of young plants. There was a little soil on one cuff and a pencil tucked behind his ear.

“Morning, Professor,” Neville said to McGonagall. Then his face brightened. “Hermione. I thought you’d be buried in paperwork.”

“I was,” Hermione replied. “We’re making somewhere to sit.”

Neville shifted his box and looked into the recess.

“Oh, good,” he said.

Hermione glanced up from the cream fabric she was holding against the curtain rail.

“Do people use it?”

“I’ve seen a few,” Neville replied. “Usually when the library’s busy. Someone was revising here on Wednesday.”

“Comfortably?” McGonagall asked.

Neville’s eyes travelled to the old cushion, which was now leaning against the wall.

“They kept changing position,” he admitted.

Hermione lowered the fabric.

“We were trying to decide who it should be for.”

Neville looked puzzled for a moment.

“Wouldn’t it be for whoever’s sitting there?” he asked.

McGonagall’s mouth moved slightly at one corner.

“That is certainly one approach.”

Neville bent to put the plant box down. It held several pots of small, round leaves, each leaf edged in pale silver.

“Those are lovely,” Hermione said.

“Moonmint,” Neville replied. “For the greenhouse benches. It likes the cooler light.”

“Would it like this window?” Hermione asked.

Neville examined the direction of the light, then held his hand near the glass.

“Yes,” he said. “As long as nobody waters it every time they feel helpful.”

He selected a pot from the box and set it at the far end of the sill.

The little plant made the space look occupied already.

“Would you mind?” Hermione asked.

“Not at all,” Neville replied. “I have plenty.”

McGonagall regarded the pot.

“Nothing with a tendency to sing, I hope.”

“Quite silent,” Neville assured her. “It smells a bit like mint if you brush the leaves. That’s all.”

Hermione touched one leaf with her fingertip. A cool, clean scent rose briefly.

Neville picked up his box.

“If you’re still here after lunch, I’ll bring a saucer,” he said. “That one leaks.”

After he had gone, Hermione looked at the seat again.

Whoever was sitting there.

It was a sensible answer. She was not entirely sure why she had expected a more elaborate one.

***

They ate lunch in McGonagall’s study.

A small table had been cleared beside the window, and there were sandwiches, a dish of sliced pears and a pot of tea. Hermione had brought the sample book, which lay open on a chair like a guest waiting to be included.

“The cream will let the light through,” Hermione said.

“And soften it in the afternoon,” McGonagall replied.

“Do we need curtains that close all the way?”

McGonagall considered the question over her cup.

“Yes,” she said. “Even if they are seldom used that way. It is pleasant to have the choice.”

Hermione nodded.

She had been thinking about warmth, colour, measurements. McGonagall had thought of the feeling of sitting beside a window on a dark afternoon and being able to draw the curtains.

“There’s a note in the book about linings,” Hermione said. “We could add something light.”

“Provided we can still lift the result,” McGonagall replied.

Hermione smiled and reached for a sandwich.

The headmistress’s study was unusually still. A silver instrument on a shelf turned once, gave a soft click and stopped. Several portraits were asleep. Somewhere beyond the door, a clock struck one.

“I used to think,” Hermione said, “that making a room useful meant making sure it had everything anyone might need.”

McGonagall set down her cup.

“And now?”

“I’m beginning to suspect that leaves very little room for the person.”

McGonagall glanced at the sample book on the chair.

“A window seat should probably retain enough space for someone to sit on it,” she said.

Hermione laughed.

They finished lunch without returning to the library proposals.

When they rose, McGonagall took a small sewing box from a drawer. It was plain walnut, polished by long use.

“I could summon a larger one,” she said, “but this has very good scissors.”

Hermione lifted the sample book from the chair.

For the first time since her arrival, she had no idea what time she would finish what she was doing, and found that she did not mind.

***

The afternoon work required more judgement than Hermione had expected.

McGonagall could have transformed the old cushion outright, but she wanted the new cover to last, and Hermione liked the thought of using the wool they had found.

They measured, marked and cut. McGonagall guided the scissors with her wand while Hermione held the material steady. The blades travelled in a clean line, making a soft, crisp sound.

For the filling, McGonagall brought a folded wool blanket from the linen room and a bag of clean upholstery flock. Together they made a firm inner pad, then worked the blue cover over it.

The first fitting was too tight.

Hermione sat down and felt the cushion bunch beneath her knees.

“We allowed for the width,” she said, getting up, “but not quite enough for the depth once it was filled.”

McGonagall removed her spectacles and examined the seam.

“Fortunately,” she said, “we have not yet finished the ends.”

They loosened the cover and let it settle.

The second fitting was better.

Hermione sat for a full minute, looking out over the courtyard. A small cloud moved across the sun. The flagstones changed from silver to grey.

“Well?” McGonagall asked.

“I think you should try it,” Hermione replied.

McGonagall sat, adjusted her position once and rested her hands in her lap.

“Yes,” she said.

It was a modest word, but Hermione recognised approval when she heard it.

The curtains took longer. Their hems had to fall clear of the seat, and the folds needed room to move. They replaced two cracked rings from the basket in the linen room and rubbed a little wax along the wooden rail.

When McGonagall drew the cream fabric across, it moved with a quiet sliding sound.

Sunlight came through in a pale wash.

Hermione drew it back again. The cloth settled against the stone in easy folds.

They stood together in the corridor and looked.

The recess was beginning to belong to the gallery. Nothing demanded attention. The blue took up the colour of the distant hills, while the cream caught the warmth in the oak.

Only the empty candleholder remained rather forlorn.

“A lamp,” Hermione said.

McGonagall nodded. “A small one.”

***

They found the table before they found the lamp.

It was tucked beneath a larger desk in an unused classroom: a little round table with three legs and a water mark shaped vaguely like a pear. Hermione cleaned it and tightened the join at the top of one leg.

It fitted beside the seat with room to spare.

The lamp came from a cupboard near McGonagall’s study. Its shade was plain parchment, its base dark green glass. McGonagall lit it with a touch of her wand.

A gentle pool of light fell across the table.

“Too low?” Hermione asked.

“For a tall reader, perhaps,” McGonagall replied.

Hermione tried it with the household charms book. The light reached the page without shining into her eyes.

McGonagall moved the lamp an inch nearer the wall.

“There,” she said.

They returned the brass candleholder to the linen-room shelf, where it could wait for somewhere that needed it. Hermione put the green button in a small dish by the gallery door, beside a lost hair ribbon and a single cufflink.

Then she took the wooden ruler to the library.

The short walk gave her a chance to notice how pleasantly tired she had become. Her shoulders had loosened. She had spent hours concentrating, but the concentration had travelled through her hands: lifting cloth, smoothing a seam, holding a curtain straight.

On her way back, she paused outside the recess.

A younger pupil was sitting on the new cushion.

Hermione could not see the pupil’s face clearly, only a bent head, dark robes and a pair of shoes planted firmly on the floor. The household charms book was open across the reader’s knees.

McGonagall stood a little further along the corridor, sorting the remaining samples into their book.

Hermione approached quietly.

“Shall we come back?” she asked in a low voice.

“We can finish here,” McGonagall replied, equally softly.

They gathered their tools at the other end of the gallery.

The pupil turned a page.

Nothing else happened, and Hermione found herself listening with unexpected satisfaction to that small, ordinary sound.

***

Neville returned with a saucer and a narrow-necked watering bottle.

The pupil had gone by then. The book lay closed on the table, its blue wool marker projecting neatly from the top.

Neville put the saucer beneath the moonmint.

“Someone’s been using it already,” he said.

“Yes,” Hermione replied.

“Good,” Neville said. He looked at the cushion, then at the curtains. “It looks as though it’s always been like that.”

Hermione glanced towards McGonagall, pleased.

“I hope you mean that kindly.”

“I do,” Neville said. “Some things look new in a way that makes you afraid to touch them.”

He sat down at McGonagall’s invitation, placing the watering bottle on the table.

The seat took his weight without creaking.

“This is comfortable,” Neville said.

“Firm enough?” McGonagall asked.

Neville shifted back and considered. “Yes. Though you’d want something behind you if you were sitting sideways.”

Hermione looked at the scraps of blue wool folded beside the sample book.

“A small cushion,” she said.

“Only a small one,” McGonagall replied.

They made it while Neville told them about a batch of seedlings that had finally put out roots after several weeks of apparent indecision. He spoke without hurrying, occasionally pausing to turn the moonmint so its leaves faced the light.

Hermione sewed the last short opening by hand. It would have been quicker with magic, but the stitches were easy and there were only a few.

When she had finished, she placed the little cushion against the side of the recess.

Neville tried leaning against it.

“That’s it,” he said.

McGonagall collected the scissors and closed the walnut sewing box.

The task appeared to be finished.

Hermione looked at the table, the lamp and the book.

“We ought to leave some sort of notice,” she said.

Neville looked up. “About what?”

“That anyone can sit here,” Hermione replied.

McGonagall let her gaze rest on the seat for a moment.

“Let us see whether anyone needs telling,” she said.

***

They put everything away before tea.

The unused cream fabric returned to the linen room, wrapped against dust. The remaining wool went beside it, with a new label giving the date and the amount. Hermione made sure the sample book’s inventory reflected what they had used.

She repaired the loose pocket at the back with a small charm.

McGonagall watched her smooth it flat.

“That book has been waiting a considerable time for someone to do that,” she said.

“It was only a little tear,” Hermione replied.

“They often are,” McGonagall said.

In the staff room, they had tea and thick slices of seed cake. Neville stayed long enough to finish one cup before returning to the greenhouses.

Hermione held her warm cup in both hands.

There were still things she could have added to the recess. A shelf beneath the window, perhaps. A footstool. A collection of books chosen especially for the place.

She imagined each of them in turn.

The shelf would catch people’s knees. The footstool would drift into the corridor. A special collection would need arranging, and returning, and deciding upon.

The household charms book had found its way there perfectly well.

McGonagall cut another thin slice of cake.

“You are considering something,” she said.

“I was wondering whether it needed anything else,” Hermione admitted.

“And does it?”

Hermione took a sip of tea before answering.

“I don’t think so.”

McGonagall passed her the plate.

“Then have some cake while that remains your conclusion.”

Outside the staff-room window, the afternoon had begun to turn gold. Hermione accepted the cake and sat back in her chair.

They spoke for a while about her work in London, then about the proposals she had come to review. There was one small alteration McGonagall wanted to make to the library opening hours.

Hermione understood it more readily now.

They made a note to discuss it in the morning.

***

After supper, Hermione took another walk through the castle.

She had meant to go straight to her room, but her feet led her towards the gallery. The corridors were settling into their evening quiet. Doors closed gently. Somewhere below, a piano was being played slowly, one phrase repeated until its ending came right.

The lamp beside the window seat was lit.

McGonagall sat beneath it with a slim book open in her hands.

Hermione stopped at the entrance to the gallery.

The headmistress looked up.

“I thought I ought to test it properly,” McGonagall said.

“Of course,” Hermione replied.

“The curtains work very well.”

Beyond the glass, the courtyard had become a pattern of pale stone and soft shadow. One narrow strip of sky remained blue above the opposite roof.

Hermione came closer.

“What are you reading?”

“Essays on Highland gardens,” McGonagall replied. She turned the book slightly so Hermione could see its cover. “I was given it at Christmas.”

“Have you only just started it?”

McGonagall marked her place with a finger.

“I have started it several times.”

There was room beside her. Hermione sat down, leaving the small cushion where it supported McGonagall’s elbow.

For a little while they watched the courtyard.

The moonmint gave no scent until Hermione’s sleeve brushed it. Then a faint freshness rose between them.

“I think Neville answered my question,” Hermione said eventually.

“Which question?” McGonagall asked.

“Who the seat was for.”

McGonagall looked at the book in her lap.

“Yes,” she said. “I believe he did.”

Hermione leaned back against the wood. Across the courtyard, a light appeared in an upper window.

She had spent much of her life finding a place for things. Books belonged on particular shelves. Papers needed headings. Work was easier when its purpose could be stated clearly.

This small space had required care, but apparently it had no need of a category.

A pupil had read here. Neville had rested between errands. Now McGonagall had brought a book she had been meaning to enjoy for months.

Hermione would have been sorry to see a notice hanging above them.

McGonagall opened her book again.

“Do stay if you wish,” she said.

Hermione settled more comfortably on the cushion.

“Just for a few minutes,” she replied.

***

The few minutes lengthened.

McGonagall read. Hermione watched the last colour leave the courtyard and listened to the soft movement of pages beside her.

A pair of pupils passed through the gallery, speaking quietly. One glanced at the recess, then continued on. Somewhere far below, a door shut with a mellow wooden sound.

The lamp made a warm circle around the book and McGonagall’s hands. Beyond it, the cream curtains rested against the stone.

Hermione let her eyes close briefly.

She was aware of the cushion beneath her, firm and comfortable, and of the familiar castle holding its evening around them. There was no report in her lap. Nothing needed measuring.

When she opened her eyes, McGonagall had reached the end of a chapter.

“I shall go up,” Hermione said.

“A sensible decision,” McGonagall replied. “You have done quite enough for one day.”

Hermione stood and smoothed the place where she had been sitting.

“Will you be here much longer?”

“Another few pages,” McGonagall said.

Hermione smiled at the answer.

“Goodnight, Professor.”

“Goodnight, Hermione.”

At the end of the gallery, Hermione looked back once.

McGonagall had returned to her book. The seat, which had seemed that morning to be waiting for someone to explain its purpose, was quietly in use.

Hermione went upstairs.

***

Her guest room was warm when she opened the door.

Someone had drawn the bed curtains halfway back and left a jug of water on the small chest beside the bed. Her slippers stood where she had put them that morning. The library papers waited in their tidy pile.

Hermione took the old envelope from her cardigan pocket.

Measuring, cushion, curtains, lamp, table.

Each item had a small tick beside it. Underneath, in handwriting made slightly uneven by the wall she had written against, were the words *Draught stopper*.

She remembered the old cushion filling.

For a moment she considered finding a fresh sheet of paper and making a note about it for the linen room. Then she folded the envelope and laid it on top of the proposals. Morning would do perfectly well.

She washed, changed into her nightclothes and hung the cardigan over the chair.

At the window, she paused to look out.

Her room faced a different part of the grounds. The lawn sloped away beneath a clear night sky, and the windows reflected a little of the lamplight behind her. She could see the edge of the lake, still and dark, with a pale gleam where the moon touched it.

Hermione drew her curtains.

Their rings made the same quiet sliding sound as the ones in the gallery.

She got into bed and settled beneath the blankets. The pillow was cool against her cheek. From somewhere in the wall came a soft tick as the room lost the last warmth of the day.

For a while she thought of the sample book: the little squares of cloth, their patient labels, the blue wool folded away for years until it was needed.

Then she pictured the gallery lamp and McGonagall turning another page.

Tomorrow, someone else would find the seat. They might read a chapter, mend a quill or sit for five minutes watching the courtyard. Perhaps they would leave the curtains open. Perhaps they would draw them against the afternoon sun.

There was nothing further Hermione needed to arrange.

She reached for her wand and lowered the lamp to a soft glow, then let it go out.

The bed curtains enclosed a gentle darkness. Hermione shifted once, finding a comfortable place for her shoulder, and drew the blanket a little higher.

Her last clear thought was of the book waiting on the small round table, its place kept by a piece of blue wool.

Then the thought loosened, the quiet deepened, and Hermione slept.
