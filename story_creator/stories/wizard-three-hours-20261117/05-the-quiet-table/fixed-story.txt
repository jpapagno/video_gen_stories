The Quiet Table

Ron first noticed the little table because someone had put a folded blanket beneath one of its legs.

It stood near the window of a sitting room on the third floor of Hogwarts, where the November afternoon had gathered into a soft grey light. There were five chairs around it, none of them alike. One had a high, straight back; another was upholstered in faded green; a third looked as though it had spent most of its life beside a kitchen fire. The remaining two had been pushed so close together that their arms overlapped.

Professor McGonagall stood beside the hearth with a small sheaf of parchment.

“I was assured this room would be suitable for an informal supper,” McGonagall said. “I should perhaps have been more specific about the furniture.”

Hermione set her overnight bag beside the sofa.

“It’s a lovely room,” Hermione said.

It was. A fire had been laid, the shelves held books that appeared to have been read, and the window seat was deep enough for two people who knew each other well. Beyond the glass, the lawns sloped towards the lake. Ron could see a gardener’s wheelbarrow standing beneath a bare tree.

“The room’s fine,” Ron said. He touched the table with two fingers. It tipped towards him, then settled back on its blanket. “The question is whether we can all eat without wearing most of it.”

“That,” McGonagall replied, “is what I hoped you might help me establish.”

Ron had come prepared for a weekend of being fed. He considered the table again.

“How long have we got?”

“Neville and Luna will join us at half past six,” McGonagall said. “It is now a quarter to four.”

“Plenty of time,” Ron said.

Hermione looked at him with the affectionate caution she generally reserved for those words.

Their invitation had arrived a fortnight earlier: one quiet Saturday at Hogwarts, supper with a few old friends, and a guest room for the night. Ron and Hermione’s children were staying with his parents, who had accepted the arrangement before either child had finished explaining it. Hermione had brought a book. Ron had brought a second pair of socks and a firm intention not to be useful until Monday.

Yet there was something agreeable about this particular problem. It required no journey, no committee and, so far as he could see, no one telling him that his answer must be submitted in duplicate.

McGonagall laid her parchment on the mantelpiece.

“The larger rooms are in use,” McGonagall explained. “I thought we might enjoy somewhere less formal. Neville suggested this one, since it is close to the guest rooms.”

“Very considerate of him,” Ron said, examining the two chairs whose arms had become entangled. “Especially if he knew about these.”

Hermione drew one chair away. The other came with it.

For a moment they both stood holding the furniture.

“Perhaps,” Hermione said, “we should begin by separating them.”

Once apart, the chairs proved to be a narrow wooden armchair and a broad one covered in blue wool. The broad chair had surprisingly short legs. Ron sat down in it and found the table edge level with his chest.

“No,” Ron said.

McGonagall’s mouth moved at one corner.

“An unusually decisive contribution, Mister Weasley.”

“Ron,” Hermione said gently, as she had several times since they had arrived.

“I am aware,” McGonagall replied. “Old habits are persistent.”

Ron got up again. He had thought returning to Hogwarts as an adult would make the castle feel smaller. Instead, certain things remained precisely the same size: the windows, the staircases, and Professor McGonagall’s capacity to make him stand a little straighter.

He took off his coat.

“Right,” Ron said. “Let’s see what we’ve actually got.”

The table was made of dark wood, with a narrow drawer at one end and a leaf folded down on either side. Opened fully, it might accommodate five people, though not if they wanted much supper. One corner was rounded from years of use. A small pale circle marked the place where a hot cup had once stood.

Hermione knelt beside the leg with the blanket beneath it.

“The floor slopes,” Hermione said.

“So does the table,” Ron replied.

“We should find out how much of each.”

McGonagall watched Hermione remove the blanket, which proved to be a rather good travelling rug folded six times. The table dropped noticeably.

Ron caught the teapot before it slid.

There was a short silence.

“I had not realised there was a teapot on it,” McGonagall said.

“Neither had I,” Ron replied, placing it safely on the mantelpiece. “That’s something we’ve learnt.”

The teapot was empty. It had a chipped lid and a little knitted cover shaped like an ordinary brown hen. Hermione turned it once in her hands before setting it down.

“Shall we move the table away from the window?” Hermione suggested. “The boards may be more even.”

They shifted it towards the hearth. Ron lifted one end while McGonagall guided the other with her wand. Hermione watched the legs. A faint rectangular outline emerged on the carpet where the table had stood.

In its new position it rocked less, but one foot still hovered above the floor.

Ron crouched.

“There’s something missing here.”

The short leg ended in a small brass socket. The other legs had wooden feet, each screwed into a similar fitting. One foot had been turned higher than the others.

Hermione leaned down beside him.

“They’re adjustable,” Hermione said.

Ron tried the nearest foot. It moved with a dry little squeak.

“Well,” Ron said, “that’s clever.”

“Until someone loses one,” McGonagall observed.

The drawer seemed the obvious place to look.

Inside, Ron found three candle ends, a bundle of plain string, a folded square of green baize and a paper packet labelled FELT. There was also a drawer handle, which was puzzling, since the drawer already possessed one.

There was no wooden foot.

Hermione lifted out the baize and shook it gently. A brass screw rolled into Ron’s palm.

“Promising,” Hermione said.

Ron looked from the screw to the table.

“Only if we’re planning to grow the rest.”

McGonagall took the packet of felt.

“These will at least prevent us from scratching the floor while we experiment,” McGonagall said.

They put a felt square under each leg and set the travelling rug across the sofa. Then Hermione stood back, her wand held loosely at her side.

“We could repair the missing foot,” Hermione said. “If we had it.”

“We could make another,” Ron replied.

“Yes. Although I’d rather understand how the table is meant to stand before we alter it.”

Ron approved of that. He had spent enough time helping George with shop fittings to know that a charm could leave a cupboard looking beautifully straight while every drawer inside it remained impossible to open.

He lifted one of the folded leaves.

A wooden support swung out underneath, stopped halfway and refused to go any further.

“Oh,” Ron said. “There’s another bit.”

McGonagall set her parchment down again.

“I shall ask for tea,” McGonagall said.

By the time the tea arrived, they had discovered that one leaf was supported properly and the other was not. The obstructed support had been turned the wrong way on its hinge. It had probably happened when the table was folded.

Ron eased it back, lifted the leaf slightly, and swung the support underneath.

The wood settled with a sound of quiet agreement.

Hermione placed a cup on the opened leaf. It stayed where it was.

“That’s much better,” Hermione said.

Ron sat in the kitchen chair and tested the space beneath.

His knee struck the support.

He shifted sideways.

His other knee struck a crosspiece.

“Better for the cup,” Ron said.

They drank their tea from the sofa and the window seat, leaving the table unoccupied in the middle of the room.

Outside, the light had thinned. A few students crossed the lawn with their scarves pulled up, taking the diagonal route towards the entrance. Somewhere below, a door closed and a burst of laughter travelled briefly along the corridor.

Ron held his cup between both hands.

“How many chairs does Hogwarts have?” Ron asked.

“I have never commissioned a count,” McGonagall replied.

“Thousands, surely.”

“Very likely.”

“And we’ve managed to find the five that don’t go together.”

Hermione looked over her cup.

“We don’t necessarily need them to match.”

“I’d settle for them all reaching the table.”

McGonagall stirred her tea once.

“I could have a suitable set brought up,” McGonagall said. “This need not become an undertaking.”

Ron glanced at the little table. With one leaf raised and the other hanging, it looked halfway through getting dressed.

“No, it’s all right,” Ron said. “I want to see if we can make it work.”

Hermione’s expression softened. She knew that particular inclination in him: a minor difficulty, a little time, and suddenly he would be unable to leave the thing alone.

“Then perhaps we should think about the people,” Hermione said.

Ron nodded towards the blue chair.

“That one’s for someone with much shorter legs.”

“Or,” Hermione replied, “it belongs somewhere other than a dining table.”

They carried the blue chair to the fire. It looked immediately at home there.

The narrow armchair went beside it. Without the broad chair pressing against its side, it seemed quite a decent chair.

“That leaves three,” Ron said.

“And two people who haven’t arrived,” Hermione replied.

McGonagall set down her cup.

“There is a small furniture store at the end of this corridor,” McGonagall said. “We might inspect it before we resort to transfiguring the coal scuttle.”

The store was less a room than a deep cupboard, lit by a window no wider than Ron’s hand. Several framed landscapes leaned against the wall. A folding screen stood behind a rolled carpet. There were two hat stands, a washstand, a chest of draughts pieces and three chairs.

Ron brightened.

Then he saw that one chair had no seat.

“Useful if we invite a ghost,” Ron said.

Hermione examined the other two. One was a plain dining chair with a loose cushion. The second was a small stool, its surface polished smooth at the edges.

McGonagall rested a hand on the dining chair.

“This should do.”

Ron picked up the stool.

Something clicked inside it.

He stopped.

Hermione glanced over.

“What was that?”

“Either it’s meant to do that,” Ron said, “or I’ve just made it worse.”

The stool had a shallow compartment beneath its seat. A wooden catch, nearly flush with the edge, held it closed. Ron pressed it and lifted the lid.

Inside lay a little dusting brush and a dark wooden foot with a brass screw at one end.

Ron held it up.

Hermione laughed, a brief, pleased sound that made the narrow cupboard seem warmer.

“Well done,” Hermione said.

“I haven’t done anything yet,” Ron replied. “It might belong to a completely different troublesome table.”

But the grain matched, as did the brass thread, and the foot fitted neatly into the empty socket.

Ron screwed it into place while Hermione steadied the table. McGonagall turned the other feet until all four stood firmly on their felt squares.

No blanket was required.

Ron put both palms on the top and leaned a little.

The table remained still.

“There,” Ron said.

McGonagall regarded it with satisfaction.

“A considerable improvement.”

They brought in the dining chair and left the stool by the sofa, where it could support a tray. There were now four chairs around the table, and two comfortable ones by the fire.

For a moment, the room appeared finished.

Then Hermione laid five folded napkins on the tabletop.

Ron studied the available space.

“Ah.”

The fifth person would have to sit at the end with the drawer. Unfortunately, the drawer projected far enough beneath the top to make that position uncomfortable. The opposite end had a crossbar. With both leaves raised, there was room for plates, but very little room for the people who intended to use them.

Hermione put down the last napkin.

“We’ve made a stable table,” Hermione said. “We haven’t made a supper table.”

Ron pulled out the kitchen chair again and sat down. He tried turning his knees to the side.

“No,” Ron said. “We haven’t.”

At five o’clock, Neville arrived carrying a shallow basket covered with a clean cloth.

“I thought these might be useful,” Neville said.

He lifted the cloth to reveal several small pots of thyme, each tucked into a paper sleeve.

“For the table?” Hermione asked.

“If there’s room,” Neville said. Then he looked properly at the table. “Perhaps the windowsill.”

“That’s becoming the difficulty,” Ron replied.

Neville put the basket down and listened while they explained. He tried the high-backed chair, stood up, and shifted it six inches to the left.

“The leg’s in the way there,” Neville said.

“It’s in the way almost everywhere,” Ron replied.

Neville considered the table without any sign of urgency. His jumper had a little soil on one cuff, and he brushed it away with his thumb.

“I use one rather like this in the greenhouse office,” Neville said. “Only one side’s open.”

“For eating?” Ron asked.

“For putting things down while I’m working.”

Ron looked at the folded leaf, then at the window seat.

Hermione was already moving the napkins.

“What if we only opened one side?” Hermione said.

“Then it’s smaller,” Ron replied.

“Yes,” Hermione said, “but we might be able to sit around the open side without the chairs colliding.”

They tried it. McGonagall folded down the leaf nearest the hearth, and Neville helped Ron turn the table. The flat edge went towards the window seat, with the raised leaf facing into the room.

It looked possible.

Ron sat on the window seat.

The table was too far away.

They moved it closer.

Now he could reach, but he could not get out.

Hermione folded her arms and studied him.

“You could remain there until breakfast,” Hermione said.

“As long as someone brings it.”

Neville laughed, then glanced at the broad blue chair by the fire.

“Could we eat from our laps?”

McGonagall looked mildly doubtful.

“I had imagined plates.”

“Plates can go on laps,” Ron said. “It’s one of their lesser-known qualities.”

Hermione sat beside him on the window seat, testing the distance.

“Soup would be awkward,” Hermione said.

They all looked at McGonagall.

“There is no soup,” McGonagall said.

Ron felt they had made more progress in the last thirty seconds than in the previous hour.

Supper, it turned out, was to be a pie with mushrooms and leeks, roast potatoes, carrots, bread and a warm apple pudding.

“All very manageable,” Ron said.

“With gravy,” McGonagall added.

“Still manageable.”

Hermione raised an eyebrow.

“Mostly,” Ron conceded.

Luna arrived while they were trying to arrange the chairs in a loose circle.

She had come from Hogsmeade on foot, and a fine sprinkling of rain glittered on the shoulders of her coat. Her hair was fastened with a blue ribbon. She carried a paper bag, which she handed to Hermione.

“Pears,” Luna said. “The woman in the shop said they would be good tomorrow, but I tried one and it was already good.”

“Thank you,” Hermione said. “We can have them with breakfast.”

Luna took in the room, the open leaf and the scattering of chairs.

“Are you choosing places?” Luna asked.

“We’re trying to,” Ron replied. “It’s proving surprisingly technical.”

Luna removed her coat and hung it beside the door.

“May I sit in that one?” Luna asked, pointing to the green upholstered chair.

“Please do,” McGonagall said.

Luna settled into it. She put both feet on the floor and rested her hands on the arms.

“It’s comfortable,” Luna said. “Except that I can’t see Neville.”

Neville moved the high-backed chair a little.

“Now?” Neville asked.

“Now I can see your elbow,” Luna replied.

Ron stepped back and looked at their arrangement.

They had placed the largest backs towards the middle of the room. Anyone sitting in them would have to turn to speak to anyone else. The window seat was half hidden. The blue chair faced the door.

He had been thinking about how to fit chairs around a table. He had barely considered where anyone would be looking.

“Hang on,” Ron said.

He moved the high-backed chair to one side of the fireplace, angled towards the window. The green chair went opposite it, with enough space between them to see across. He put the plain dining chair nearer the window seat and drew the kitchen chair back towards the hearth.

Luna watched with interest.

“That’s nicer,” Luna said.

Hermione sat on the window seat again. This time there was nothing in front of her knees.

“Yes,” Hermione said. “It is.”

The table stood slightly apart from everyone, one leaf raised, looking useful at last.

Ron folded his arms.

“What if we stop trying to sit at it?”

McGonagall considered the suggestion.

“A serving table?”

“Put the food there,” Ron said. “Pass the plates round. Everyone sits where they’re comfortable.”

Neville glanced at the basket on the windowsill.

“And there’ll be room for one of these.”

“There will,” Hermione agreed.

They tried the seats properly before deciding.

McGonagall preferred the high-backed chair, which supported her shoulders and allowed her to reach the small side table by the hearth. Luna kept the green chair. Neville took the kitchen chair after discovering that the plain dining chair was slightly too low for him. Hermione chose the window seat, leaving room for Ron beside her.

The remaining dining chair could hold a tray of used dishes, and the blue armchair waited near the fire for anyone who wished to move later.

“It’s a great deal of furniture for five people,” Neville said.

“We’ve used almost all of it,” Ron replied proudly.

There was still the matter of the serving table’s position. Too near the window seat, it blocked Hermione’s way. Too near the hearth, it would require everyone to reach across the hot dishes.

Ron and Neville carried it to the wall between the bookcase and the window. With the leaf open, it offered ample room for supper; with the other folded, it left a clear path to every chair.

Hermione opened the little drawer. It slid out without meeting anyone’s knees.

“Napkins,” Hermione said, putting them inside.

McGonagall placed the teapot at one end.

Luna chose a pot of thyme and set it at the other.

Ron took the travelling rug from the sofa and folded it over the back of the blue chair.

The room was ready.

They had forty minutes until supper.

For the first time that afternoon, no one appeared inclined to improve anything.

Neville lit the fire with a quiet movement of his wand. The kindling caught, then the logs, and a light warmth began to reach across the carpet. Hermione drew the curtains when the window became more reflective than transparent, leaving a narrow gap through which the first lights on the grounds could be seen.

Ron sat beside her with his empty hands resting on his knees.

He had expected to feel hungry by now. He did feel hungry, but beneath that was the pleasant satisfaction of having finished something small enough to see all at once.

The table stood level. The chairs faced one another. Nobody would have to apologise whenever they wanted to get up.

Hermione touched his sleeve.

“You enjoyed that,” Hermione said quietly.

Ron looked towards the table.

“More than I expected.”

“I noticed.”

“It’s nice when something can be sorted out before dinner.”

Hermione smiled.

“Yes,” Hermione said. “It is.”

McGonagall was asking Neville about a classroom windowsill that had become crowded with seedlings. Neville explained that the students were supposed to take their plants away on Fridays, but several had begun leaving notes requesting that he keep an eye on them over the weekend.

“One note included a watering schedule,” Neville said.

“Was it correct?” McGonagall asked.

“Very nearly.”

“Then I should be inclined to reward the planning.”

Luna leaned forward in her green chair.

“Perhaps the plants enjoy staying together,” Luna said.

Neville considered this with the seriousness he gave most questions about plants.

“They do seem to be doing well,” Neville replied.

Supper arrived beneath silver covers, accompanied by a stack of warmed plates. McGonagall arranged the dishes while Ron fetched the napkins from the drawer.

He opened it, stopped, and then laughed.

Hermione turned towards him.

“What is it?”

“There’s plenty of room for the napkins,” Ron said. “We spent ages trying to put someone’s legs here.”

Hermione accepted hers with a small smile.

“We needed somewhere to begin.”

Ron passed a plate to Luna, then to McGonagall. Neville took charge of the potatoes. Hermione held the bread basket while everyone helped themselves.

There was one brief difficulty with the gravy boat, whose handle faced the wall. Ron turned it round.

After that, everything went quite easily.

The pie had a crisp, golden top and a filling fragrant with thyme. Steam rose when McGonagall cut into it. The potatoes broke softly beneath their browned edges, and the bread was still warm enough to soften butter.

Ron balanced his plate on a folded napkin and took a careful mouthful.

“Well?” Hermione asked.

Ron swallowed.

“I think the furniture was worth it.”

From the high-backed chair, McGonagall gave him a dry look.

“I shall pass your assessment to the kitchens.”

Conversation moved in the easy, uneven way it did when nobody needed to catch a train. Neville described a student who had tried to keep a weekly height record for a particularly fast-growing vine and had run out of wall before Wednesday. Hermione told them about a woman at work whose filing charm had begun sorting everything according to how urgently she wished to avoid it.

“Sounds useful,” Ron said.

“It put every document in the same cupboard,” Hermione replied.

“Still sounds useful.”

Luna had been travelling along the coast and spoke about a village where the harbour lights were covered each autumn to keep migrating birds from circling them.

“You could still see the boats,” Luna said. “Only the light stayed low, close to the water.”

Neville asked about the birds. McGonagall asked whether the harbour had a lighthouse. Ron listened while he finished his pie, the fire warm against one side of his face.

No one had to raise their voice. From the window seat, he could see everyone.

When Hermione wanted more carrots, Ron could reach the dish without standing in anybody’s way. When Neville went to fetch water, his chair moved freely. Luna rested her empty plate on the stool, which had been placed conveniently beside her.

The table bore its dishes without a tremor.

Later, as they passed round the apple pudding, McGonagall glanced towards it.

“I believe we may leave the room arranged this way,” McGonagall said.

“For the next gathering?” Hermione asked.

“For anyone who wants to use it.”

Ron looked at the chairs, each occupied according to comfort rather than appearance.

“Someone will probably move them all round tomorrow,” Ron said.

“Almost certainly,” McGonagall replied.

The thought did not trouble him. Whoever came tomorrow might want to write letters, or play cards, or sit alone by the window. They could move a chair.

For this evening, everything was where it needed to be.

After supper, Neville helped clear the plates while Hermione wrapped the remaining bread. Luna moved to the blue chair and drew the travelling rug over her knees. McGonagall poured tea from the brown hen teapot, which kept the second cup as warm as the first.

Ron put one of the pears beside the bread for breakfast.

By nine o’clock, the conversation had slowed. There were longer pauses in which the logs settled and someone turned a cup in its saucer. Neville yawned, apologised, and admitted that he had been in the greenhouses before breakfast.

Luna folded the rug carefully before they left together.

“I’m glad we didn’t all sit at the table,” Luna said at the door. “I should have spent the evening looking at the back of that chair.”

“So should Neville,” Ron replied. “And he was sitting in it.”

Neville smiled and wished them goodnight.

McGonagall remained long enough to check the fire.

“Thank you for your assistance,” McGonagall said.

Ron glanced at Hermione.

“We found the foot in a stool,” Ron said. “I’m not sure it counts as expert assistance.”

“It counts as a comfortable evening,” McGonagall replied.

Their guest room was a short walk down the corridor. Hermione carried her book, which she had not opened. Ron carried the paper bag of pears.

The bed had been turned down, and a shaded lamp stood on either side. At the foot lay a thick quilt. Rain touched the window in small, intermittent taps.

Ron placed the pears on the chest of drawers.

“Breakfast is organised,” Ron said.

Hermione sat on the edge of the bed to take off her shoes.

“Very efficient.”

Ron drew the curtains while Hermione washed. When she returned, he went in, leaving his slippers neatly beside the bed without quite intending to.

By the time he came back, Hermione was beneath the quilt, her book open against her knees.

Ron climbed in beside her. The sheets were cool for a moment, then comfortable. He stretched his legs and felt the day loosen through his shoulders.

“Enough room?” Hermione asked.

Ron turned his head on the pillow.

“For my knees? Luxury.”

Hermione laughed softly and returned to her page.

He listened to the rain. Somewhere beyond the room, footsteps crossed the corridor and faded. The castle was settling into its night sounds, familiar enough that he did not need to attend to them.

Ron thought briefly of the little wooden foot turning into its socket, of the drawer opening, of five plates passed from hand to hand. He had meant to tell Hermione that they ought to have Neville and Luna to supper at home before Christmas.

He could tell her in the morning.

Hermione read another page, then looked down. Ron’s eyes were closed, one hand resting loosely on the quilt between them.

She lowered the lamp beside him.

Ron felt the light diminish through his eyelids. He shifted a little closer when Hermione lay down, and her hand found his.

Warm beneath the quilt, with the quiet rain at the window, Ron fell asleep.
