The Last Pot of Lemon Balm

By half past four, the rain had softened everything beyond the greenhouse glass. The hills were still there, Neville knew, but they had withdrawn into a pleasant grey distance. Water gathered on the sloping panes and slipped down in crooked lines. Somewhere beneath his workbench, a terracotta saucer received a slow, dependable drip.

There were six pots of lemon balm on the bench.

There should, according to the list beside them, have been five.

Neville counted them again, although counting had never improved a situation involving an extra plant. One for the hospital wing, one for the library, one for the staffroom, one for the little room where visiting lecturers stayed, and one for the entrance hall, where the draughts troubled almost everything except lemon balm.

The sixth had no destination.

He turned its label over. On the back, in his own handwriting, were the words: Find a window.

“Oh, very helpful,” Neville told his earlier self.

The plant gave no answer. It was a good specimen, round and healthy, with pale new leaves at its centre. When he brushed one with his thumb, the clean scent rose immediately, bright without being sharp.

He remembered taking the cuttings in June. A surprising number had rooted, and he had decided that Hogwarts might benefit from a few plants which did nothing more demanding than smell nice.

Most of the things he grew had duties. They were ingredients, antidotes, teaching specimens, or examples of what happened when someone ignored instructions. Lemon balm asked only for light, water and an occasional trim. Neville had liked the idea of putting it where somebody might appreciate it without needing to do anything useful with it.

Now it was Friday, the last lessons were over, and the plants had spent three days waiting to be delivered.

He had promised himself he would finish before supper.

Neville rinsed his hands in the small stone sink and dried them on a towel that had once been yellow. Then he drew his wand and gave it a careful little turn.

Five pots rose from the bench.

The sixth remained where it was.

Neville looked at it.

“Yes, all right. You too.”

The last pot lifted, settled into line behind the others, and followed him towards the door.



The entrance hall smelled of wet wool, stone and something buttery from the kitchens.

Students came in from the grounds in cheerful, dripping groups. Neville kept the plants close to the wall as two second-years passed with a chessboard between them, the pieces arguing about whether the journey counted as part of the game.

He put the first pot on a broad windowsill beside the doors.

It looked rather small.

Neville moved it six inches to the left, then back again.

Professor McGonagall, coming down the marble staircase with a sheaf of parchment, watched this adjustment without comment.

“Good afternoon, Longbottom,” she said at last.

Neville straightened. Even after several years of teaching, he still sometimes felt that he ought to explain why he was standing in a corridor.

“Professor. I’m bringing these round.”

“I had gathered that,” McGonagall said. She examined the pot. “Lemon balm?”

“Yes. It should manage the draught.”

“A useful qualification for anything living in this hall.”

Neville smiled and tucked the label so that it faced the room.

McGonagall glanced at the five pots hovering patiently behind his shoulder.

“Have you included the library?”

“And the hospital wing, the staffroom and the visitors’ room,” Neville replied. “Then there’s one left.”

“An unexpected abundance,” McGonagall observed.

“I thought I’d find somewhere for it.”

She shifted the parchments under her arm and touched a leaf with one finger. Her expression changed very slightly as the scent reached her.

“My mother used to grow this beside the back step,” McGonagall said. “She maintained that it improved a wet afternoon.”

“Did it?”

McGonagall considered the blurred windows.

“I believe it helped.”

A student called a question from the foot of the stairs. McGonagall answered it, gave Neville a small nod, and went on towards her office.

Neville stayed beside the sill for a moment.

Already, a passing girl had slowed to read the label. She touched a leaf, smelled her fingers and held them out to her friend.

That seemed a reasonable beginning.



By the time Neville reached the hospital wing, the rain had become a quiet tapping behind the curtains.

The matron was busy at the far end of the room, so he left the second pot in the place they had agreed upon: a sunny sill near the cupboard of clean cups. He turned it until its fullest side faced the beds.

Then he put his watering instructions beneath the saucer.

When the soil feels dry. Please do not allow anyone to improve it with Pepperup Potion.

He had underlined the second sentence.

In the library, the next plant received a less uncomplicated welcome.

Neville set it down on the returns desk while he moved a pile of books out of the way. Before he could retrieve it, Hermione Granger looked up from the table beyond.

“Neville?”

She had three open books, two closed ones and a Ministry folder arranged around her. Her hair was escaping from its clip in the manner it always did when she had forgotten about it.

“Hermione,” Neville said. “I didn’t know you were here.”

“I came up to look at some old school records. There are references to educational grants that never made it into the Ministry archive.” Hermione closed one book with a finger marking her place. “I meant to finish an hour ago.”

Neville glanced at the remaining volumes.

“Going well, then?”

“Annoyingly well,” Hermione replied. “Every time I find what I need, it refers me to something else.”

She noticed the pot on the desk.

“Is that for the library?”

“Provided nobody objects.”

Hermione reached across and gently rubbed a leaf.

“Oh, that’s lovely,” she said. “My parents had some in the garden. It spread everywhere.”

“It does like to take an interest in neighbouring territory.”

“That sounds familiar.” Hermione looked down at the open books and smiled. “Where are you putting it?”

Neville indicated a tall window near the shelves of school histories.

Hermione followed his glance.

“Would it get more light by the other one?”

They carried the plant between them, though neither of them needed help with it. At the first window, an oak tree outside intercepted most of the afternoon. At the second, the glass was clear and the sill broad.

There was also a chair.

It had a rubbed wooden frame and a green cushion that had sunk comfortably in the middle.

“That’s better,” Neville said.

Hermione shifted a stack of periodicals to make space.

“People sit here while they’re waiting for books to be found,” she explained. “I sat here this morning. It was quite nice until I remembered what I was supposed to be doing.”

Neville placed the pot beside the chair.

He had imagined the library plant as something seen from a desk. Here, a person could rest a hand on the sill and find it close enough to touch.

Hermione tested the chair again, settling into it with a small, surprised sigh.

“I should have stayed here,” she said.

“You wouldn’t have reached the books.”

“That might have been an advantage.”

Neville looked back at her table. A cup stood among the papers, its tea long past steaming.

“Have you had anything since lunch?”

Hermione followed his gaze.

“I had most of that.”

“That wasn’t quite what I meant.”

“I know,” Hermione admitted.

Neville found himself laughing. It was pleasant to be able to ask her such a thing now. At school, he had generally assumed that Hermione had already considered every sensible question and was busy answering a more difficult one.

She rose and took her coat from the back of the chair.

“Ten minutes,” Hermione decided. “I can spare ten minutes. Are you going towards the staffroom?”

“Yes. With three plants.”

“Then I’ll come.”



They walked along a corridor where the windows reflected little gold flames from the lamps.

Hermione asked about the greenhouses, and Neville told her about a first-year class which had successfully repotted twenty-four seedlings and accidentally repotted one pupil’s shoe.

“Was anyone wearing it?” Hermione asked.

“Not by the end.”

“Then I’d call that a successful lesson.”

“He did ask whether he could leave it there until it grew a matching one.”

Hermione laughed, and the sound travelled ahead of them into the quiet passage.

She told him that Ron was spending the evening with George and had assured her that she needn’t hurry home. Neville asked whether she believed this had been entirely generous.

“I believe there was a new delivery at the shop,” Hermione replied. “And I believe I wasn’t meant to ask any further questions.”

They reached the staffroom just as a log settled in the fire.

Only McGonagall was there. She had exchanged her working parchments for a cup of tea, though a small stack of papers waited beside her chair.

“Miss Granger,” she said. “Have you escaped the library?”

“Temporarily, Professor.”

“A wise precaution.”

Neville placed the fourth pot on a low table near the south-facing window. Hermione sat down, accepted a cup and warmed both hands around it.

McGonagall watched Neville check the distance between the plant and the fire.

“Would a larger saucer be preferable?” she asked.

“This one should do. I’ve charmed the underside so it won’t mark the wood.”

“Excellent. The table has expressed its displeasure on that subject before.”

Neville glanced at a pale ring beside the saucer.

“It actually complained?”

“Only when Professor Flitwick put his cup down. A very small voice, but persistent.”

Hermione smiled into her tea.

Neville stood for another moment, wondering whether the pot needed turning. Then McGonagall pointed towards a chair.

“Sit down, Longbottom. The plant can be trusted to remain upright without supervision.”

He sat.

A cup floated over to him. The tea smelled faintly of bergamot, and someone had left a plate of shortbread within reach of all three chairs.

For a while, they talked about ordinary school matters. A window latch in the west corridor. The surprising popularity of the new porridge topping. Whether the suits of armour ought to be permitted to rehearse carols before December.

“No,” McGonagall said firmly.

“They’ve only got as far as humming,” Neville offered.

“That is how it begins.”

Hermione broke a piece of shortbread in half.

“How many plants have you got left?”

“Two,” Neville said. “One for your room, if you’re staying.”

“I am. I thought I’d start again in the morning, while the books are still out.”

“And one without a home.”

McGonagall looked towards the window.

“Have you tried your own rooms?”

Neville paused with his cup halfway to its saucer.

“I’ve got plants.”

“I should certainly hope so,” McGonagall replied.

“Quite a few, actually.”

“Then you are experienced in accommodating them.”

Hermione’s mouth curved slightly, but she said nothing.

Neville thought of the narrow sitting room adjoining his bedroom. There were essays on the table, seed catalogues beside the hearth and a pair of muddy boots underneath a chair. On the windowsill stood a tray of experimental seedlings which needed recording twice a day.

“I was thinking of somewhere people could enjoy it,” he said.

McGonagall reached for her tea.

“Yes,” she replied. “So was I.”



Hermione’s room was in a quiet passage overlooking the courtyard.

A fire had been laid, and her overnight bag stood on a low stool. Neville put the fifth plant by the window while Hermione drew the curtains halfway, leaving the wet courtyard visible between them.

The rain had stopped. Water gleamed on the paving, and the sky above the roofs was beginning to clear.

“This is perfect,” Hermione said.

Neville put a small instruction card beneath the saucer.

Hermione picked it up.

“‘Touch a leaf whenever required,’” she read. “That’s very straightforward.”

“I thought you might prefer it to a watering schedule.”

“I do know how to water a plant.”

“I know. But you might make a chart.”

Hermione considered protesting, then laughed.

“I might.”

Neville stood back. The pot filled an awkward space between the curtain and a little brass candlestick. In the fading light, its leaves looked softer than they had in the greenhouse.

Hermione brushed one as she passed.

“I’ll notice it when I open the curtains,” she said. “That’s a nice thought.”

Neville nodded. He had been thinking about how each plant would look when he left it, rather than what somebody might do beside it tomorrow.

Hermione unfastened her bag and took out a woollen cardigan.

“Will the last one be all right while you decide?”

“Oh, yes. It’s tougher than it looks.”

“I meant you carrying it about.”

Neville glanced at the remaining pot, which hovered at a patient angle beside the door.

“I ought to take it back before supper.”

“Will you come down to the Great Hall?”

“In a bit.”

Hermione folded her coat over the stool.

“I’ll save you a place.”



Outside, the air had the freshly washed smell that came after autumn rain.

Neville took the covered path towards the greenhouses. The last pot travelled beside him at elbow height. Without the other five, it looked less like part of a task and more like company.

He passed the dark kitchen garden, where broad leaves held shallow pools of water. Beyond it, the greenhouse roofs caught the first pale streak of evening.

He had been tired when he set out. He could feel it properly now: a heaviness across his shoulders, an ache in the hand he used for pruning, the particular weariness of having spoken clearly and encouragingly for several hours.

It had been a good day. That made the tiredness easier to overlook.

In the greenhouse, the saucer under the bench had nearly filled.

Neville emptied it, checked the latch on a high window and returned the lemon balm to the place where it had stood.

The gap beside it was surprisingly large.

He tidied the loose labels. He rinsed a pair of scissors. He put away a bag of grit which had been open since Tuesday.

Then he found the notebook.

It lay under a folded square of sacking, its green cover freckled with soil. Neville opened it at the page where he had recorded the June cuttings.

Six strong roots. Three weaker. Pot on separately.

Beside this, he had drawn a small square to remind himself of the parent plant’s position.

It had come from his grandmother’s garden.

Not a special plant. Not one she had planted for him. It had grown beside the old outside tap for as long as he could remember, surviving neglect, frost and an unfortunate attempt to scour the paving.

He had taken a cutting when he visited in spring because his sleeve had brushed it and brought the smell indoors.

His grandmother had noticed him sniffing his cuff.

“Take some if you want it, Neville,” she had said. “There’s enough of it.”

He had brought it back wrapped in damp newspaper.

Neville turned another page. There, between notes about compost and a reminder to order gloves, was a sentence he had forgotten writing.

One for the window at home.

He looked at the pot.

Then he sat down on the stool beside the bench.

He could remember what he had meant. He had been in his rooms on a bright June evening, making notes for the following year. The window had been open. Somewhere below, students had been crossing the lawn, talking too loudly to hear individual words.

He had thought that a little lemon balm would be pleasant there.

By the time the plants were ready, he had filled the sill with experiments.

Neville rested his hands on his knees. For a minute he listened to the greenhouse: the faint settling of pipes, a drop of water falling from a leaf, the almost inaudible movement of something growing in the far bed.

Then he closed the notebook.

“Well,” he said to the plant. “That makes things easier.”



He brought a small tray from the potting cupboard and fitted the saucer neatly into it.

The lemon balm needed no further preparation, but Neville removed one damaged leaf and loosened the paper label. On its clean side he wrote the date.

He considered adding instructions.

Instead, he put the pencil away.

Before leaving, he made his usual round. He checked the tender cuttings beneath their glass covers, lowered the warming charm over a bed of young seedlings and secured the door to the store cupboard.

At the last bench, he paused over the tray of experiments destined for his sitting-room window. They were ready to spend their nights in the greenhouse now. He had kept them upstairs because it had been convenient to check them after supper.

Neville cleared a place for them near the thermometer.

Then he took the lemon balm to the castle.

The windows of the Great Hall shone warmly across the grounds. Through the open doors, he could hear the comfortable noise of supper: cutlery, conversation, a burst of laughter that was quickly swallowed by the room.

For once, he carried the pot in his hands.



Hermione had saved him a place near the end of the staff table.

“How did you get on?” she asked as Neville sat down.

“Found a window.”

“Good.”

She passed him the potatoes without asking for more detail, which he appreciated.

The lemon balm sat on the floor beside his chair, safely out of the way. Neville ate roast vegetables, chicken and rather more bread than he had intended. As the warmth of the food reached him, he realised how hungry he had been.

Hermione described one of the records she had found: a fund established to help pupils buy winter cloaks, maintained for years by donations whose sources had never been properly entered.

Neville listened while she explained the difficulties of tracing it.

“Does it matter who gave the money?” he asked.

“For the report, probably. For the children who got the cloaks, I don’t suppose it did.”

“Perhaps there’s something in the governors’ minutes.”

Hermione looked at him.

“There might be.”

Neville recognised the expression.

“Tomorrow,” he suggested.

Hermione glanced towards the doors, then at her half-finished supper.

“Tomorrow,” she agreed.

They stayed until the candles had burned a little lower. Neither hurried over pudding. Neville found that, once he stopped thinking about the things waiting upstairs, it was possible to enjoy a bowl of stewed apple simply as a bowl of stewed apple.

At the staircase, Hermione touched the rim of the pot he carried.

“I hope it likes the window,” she said.

“It should. East-facing. Morning light.”

“Very well chosen, then.”

“Apparently I chose it in June.”

Hermione gave him a questioning look.

“I found a note,” Neville explained. “I’d meant to keep one.”

“I’m glad you remembered.”

Hermione wished him goodnight and turned towards the visitors’ passage. Neville watched her go, then climbed the last flight to his rooms.



His sitting room looked exactly as he had expected.

The boots were under the chair. The essays occupied most of the table. A gardening periodical lay open on the floor, where it had slipped several evenings earlier.

Neville set down the lemon balm and lit the lamps.

There was nothing particularly wrong with the room. It was warm, private and pleasantly quiet. He liked the old rug and the deep chair by the hearth. He had simply allowed every surface to become somewhere to put the next thing.

He carried the experimental seedlings to the door and sent them, with a carefully paced charm, down the empty staircase towards the greenhouses. Then he followed to make certain they reached their new bench.

When he returned, the windowsill was bare except for a fine scattering of soil.

Neville wiped it clean.

He brought the lemon balm across and placed it at the right-hand end, where he would see it from his chair. Its reflection appeared in the dark glass, a second little plant standing against the night.

He moved the essays into one pile. The periodical went beside the chair. His boots could stay where they were until morning.

That was enough.

Neville opened the window an inch.

Cool air entered, carrying the smell of wet grass. The clouds had separated above the grounds, and a few stars showed between them. Down by the greenhouses, the warming lights made small amber squares.

He touched a lemon balm leaf.

The scent reminded him of a tap dripping into a stone trough, a patch of gravel, his grandmother’s kitchen door standing open in spring.

He had been thinking of visiting on Sunday. He would write in the morning.

For now, he drew the curtains around the plant, leaving its leaves clear of the fabric, and put the kettle on.



The kettle had a habit of beginning to whistle before the water boiled. Neville had never quite corrected it. Tonight, he took it off the heat at its second, more convincing attempt and made an ordinary cup of tea.

He carried it to the chair.

For a little while, he read the gardening periodical. There was an article about pruning which he disagreed with in several minor respects, but he found himself too comfortable to compose objections.

The fire settled.

A clock ticked on the mantelpiece.

Neville lowered the periodical onto his knee and looked towards the curtains. He could see the round edge of the saucer beneath them.

He pictured the other pots in their places: one beside the hospital cups, one by the library chair, one waiting for Hermione to open her curtains. By Monday, somebody would probably have moved the staffroom plant to make room for a plate.

He would find out then.

His tea cooled to a comfortable warmth. He finished it, put the cup on the hearth and stood with a small stretch.

In the bedroom, he turned back the covers and opened the top drawer for his pyjamas. The sheets were cool when he first lay down, then gradually warmed around his feet.

Before putting out the lamp, he looked through the open doorway.

A little of the sitting-room firelight reached the windowsill. It caught the edge of one green leaf, making it briefly distinct against the curtain.

Neville rested his head on the pillow.

The last pot was where he had wanted it.

He extinguished the lamp, and listened as the castle quietened beyond his door. Somewhere far below, a latch clicked. The clock continued its steady, familiar ticking.

His shoulders eased into the mattress. Tomorrow there would be watering to do, and a letter to write.

Neville turned onto his side, drew the blanket comfortably beneath his chin, and fell asleep.


The Window Seat

Hermione found the window seat because she had taken the longer way to breakfast.

She was staying at Hogwarts for three nights, in the spring of her twenty-sixth year, to help Professor McGonagall sort through several proposals for the school library. The work was agreeable and rather more complicated than the phrase “a few papers” had suggested. By Saturday morning, Hermione had decided that she ought to see something beyond the guest room, the headmistress’s study and the stretch of corridor connecting them.

A staircase brought her to the second floor instead of the first. She followed the smell of toast as far as a narrow gallery, where morning light lay across the flagstones.

Halfway along stood a deep window recess overlooking the inner courtyard. There was a wooden seat beneath it, wide enough for two people, with a thin cushion that had slipped sideways and curtains gathered into stiff, dusty bundles. On the sill sat a small brass candleholder, empty except for a grey curl of wick.

Hermione stopped.

Someone had left a book on the seat, face down.

She picked it up before the weight of its pages could do further damage to the spine. It was a library copy of A Hundred Useful Household Charms, open at a chapter about drying washing indoors. A narrow piece of blue wool marked the reader’s place.

“Ah,” Professor McGonagall said behind her. “You’ve discovered it.”

Hermione turned, keeping one finger between the pages.

“Is this someone’s usual place?” Hermione asked.

“Not that I’m aware of,” McGonagall replied. She regarded the cushion with the expression she generally reserved for an avoidable piece of untidiness. “Although I have been meaning to do something about it.”

“The seat?”

“The whole recess,” McGonagall said. “It gets excellent light, and very little passing traffic. At present, it also gets a draught from the left-hand corner.”

Hermione glanced at the faded cushion. Its filling had retreated into two hard ridges.

“It could be lovely,” she said.

“Yes,” McGonagall replied. “That is precisely what I keep thinking as I pass it on my way to something else.”

She took the book from Hermione, closed it carefully over the wool and set it on the sill.

“I had thought of asking you for an opinion,” McGonagall continued. “Though I ought to warn you that choosing a cushion may prove more troublesome than reviewing library regulations.”

Hermione smiled. “Who is the seat for?”

McGonagall looked at the empty place beneath the window.

“I haven’t decided,” she said.



They went to breakfast first.

The Great Hall was quieter on a Saturday, with students arriving in leisurely groups and the enchanted ceiling showing a pale sky streaked with white cloud. Hermione and McGonagall sat at one end of the staff table, where a pot of tea stood beneath a small knitted cosy.

McGonagall put marmalade on her toast.

“The recess used to contain a display of commemorative plates,” she said. “They were removed when one of them began giving unsolicited advice about posture.”

“Did anyone ask it to stop?” Hermione asked.

“Repeatedly,” McGonagall said. “It considered the requests evidence that its work was necessary.”

Hermione laughed into her tea.

Beyond the tall windows, a breeze stirred the tops of the trees. She had expected her visit to feel strange. Instead, Hogwarts had so far offered her a succession of small, practical matters: a sticking drawer, a misplaced report, an argument between two portraits over the ownership of a footstool. She found herself enjoying the manageable size of these problems.

“What did you have in mind for the window?” Hermione asked.

“A proper cushion, for a start,” McGonagall replied. “Curtains that can be closed without requiring considerable upper-body strength. Possibly a lamp.”

“And somewhere for a book,” Hermione added.

“There is a sill,” McGonagall said.

“For a book and a cup of tea?”

McGonagall considered this. “A small table, then.”

By the time they finished breakfast, Hermione had written a short list on the back of an old envelope.

McGonagall glanced at it as they rose.

“You have allotted a separate line to measuring,” she observed.

“It saves time later,” Hermione said.

“I am sure it does,” McGonagall replied, with enough dryness to make Hermione look up and smile.

They agreed to meet at the recess after McGonagall had dealt with her morning correspondence. Hermione went upstairs to collect her wand, a tape measure and the cardigan she had left over the back of her guest-room chair.

Before leaving, she put the library proposals into a neat pile.

For once, she felt no inclination to take them with her.



In full morning light, the recess looked both shabbier and more promising.

The window had three tall panes. Through the middle one Hermione could see the courtyard paving, a stone bench and a square of garden where pale green leaves were opening along the stems of a climbing rose. The left pane showed a drainpipe and a generous strip of sky. Through the right, a sloping roof led the eye towards the hills.

She measured the seat, making the tape hold itself straight across the wood.

The recess was deeper than she had realised. A person could sit facing the courtyard, or turn sideways with their feet tucked up. There was room at one end for a small table without obstructing the corridor.

Hermione knelt to examine the join beneath the left-hand window.

The draught touched her fingers immediately.

“Found it?” McGonagall asked, arriving with a large flat book beneath her arm.

“A gap in the frame,” Hermione replied. “The wood seems sound.”

“Good,” McGonagall said. “I should prefer not to begin a building project before lunch.”

Between them, they repaired the narrow opening and tested it with a feather from McGonagall’s pocket. The feather rested motionless on Hermione’s palm.

McGonagall then laid the book on the seat.

It had green cloth covers, rounded corners and a brass clasp. Across the front, in worn gold lettering, were the words Furnishing Samples: North Rooms, Galleries and Landings.

“I found this in a cupboard adjoining the staff room,” McGonagall said. “It appears to have been retained because nobody could decide which department it belonged to.”

Hermione released the clasp.

Inside were squares of fabric fixed to thick paper leaves. Some were scarcely larger than postage stamps; others unfolded into broad panels. Each had a handwritten label, and many had notes squeezed around the edges.

There were woollens, linens, velvets and things Hermione could not immediately identify. A purple square gave a faint shimmer when she touched it.

“Do be careful with page forty-three,” McGonagall said. “It sheds.”

“Lint?” Hermione asked.

“Opinions,” McGonagall replied.

Hermione turned a page.

A blue fabric waved gently beneath her fingers, although the air was still. Beside it someone had written, Suitable for sea-facing rooms. Discouraging beside breakfast.

She bent closer to read the next label.

The ordinary task of choosing a cushion had suddenly acquired possibilities.



They began with comfort.

McGonagall removed the old cushion with a careful Levitation Charm. Beneath it, the seat was made from broad oak boards, rubbed smooth in the middle and dusty at the edges. Hermione cleaned the wood while McGonagall examined the cushion’s seams.

“The cover can be repaired,” McGonagall said. “The filling has reached the end of its usefulness.”

“Could we use it elsewhere?” Hermione asked.

“In a draught stopper, perhaps.”

Hermione added that to her envelope.

They found several small things down the back of the seat: a broken quill, a sweet wrapper folded into a tiny square, and a green button with four holes. Hermione put the button on the sill in case its owner returned. The quill and wrapper went into a rubbish basket McGonagall summoned from an adjoining classroom.

Under the far corner of the cushion lay a narrow wooden ruler.

One side bore a neat set of inches. On the other, someone had carved, very faintly, Return to library.

Hermione held it up.

“I suppose this has been here for years.”

“Quite possibly,” McGonagall said. “The library has a remarkable ability to survive without the things people borrow from it.”

Hermione slipped it into her cardigan pocket.

They sat on the bare wood to test the height. McGonagall sat upright, hands folded on her lap. Hermione tried sitting normally, then turned sideways.

“A cushion needs to be thick enough to soften the boards,” Hermione said, “but not so thick that your feet hang above the floor.”

“And sufficiently firm that one can get up again,” McGonagall added.

They returned to the sample book.

Hermione favoured a moss-green wool with a close weave. McGonagall preferred a russet cloth that looked warm even before it was touched.

They laid both on the seat.

Against the oak, the russet became almost orange. The green went dull in the shadow beneath the sill.

“That’s disappointing,” Hermione said.

“Better to discover it now,” McGonagall replied.

They moved the samples into the sunlight, then back again.

Neither improved.

Hermione looked through the remaining pages with greater care. She had expected to choose a colour she liked and be done. Instead, the room seemed to have its own views, expressed through light and stone and the old honey-coloured wood.

Near the back of the book, she found a square of grey-blue wool.

At first it appeared unremarkable. Then she laid it on the seat.

The colour held.



A note beneath the grey-blue square read, West landing. Wears well. Does not attract moths.

“That seems encouraging,” Hermione said.

“Particularly the last point,” McGonagall replied.

Hermione unfolded the sample. It opened once, then again, becoming a piece large enough to cover half the seat. Fine darker threads ran through it, giving the blue a depth she had not noticed on the page.

She pressed her palm against it.

It was soft, but the weave felt sturdy.

“Would there be any of it left?” Hermione asked.

“The book claims to have an inventory,” McGonagall said.

They turned to the back.

There, in a pocket that had nearly come away from its stitching, lay a folded sheet. The ink had faded to brown, and several entries had been crossed out. Hermione found the grey-blue wool halfway down the list.

Two lengths. Linen room, upper east passage. Shelf six.

McGonagall glanced towards the corridor.

“I believe I know the room,” she said.

The upper east passage was warmer than the gallery, and smelled faintly of beeswax. Its linen-room door stood beside a portrait of a witch mending a sleeve.

McGonagall unlocked it.

Inside, shelves rose nearly to the ceiling. Folded blankets occupied one wall, while another held curtains arranged in stacks tied with cotton tape. There were bolster covers, bed hangings, bundles of braid and a large basket of wooden curtain rings.

Hermione stood just inside the doorway, delighted.

“There must be enough here to furnish half the castle.”

“There was once enough to furnish the whole of it,” McGonagall said. “I suspect some of these curtains remember doing so.”

They found shelf six behind a folding screen.

The blue wool was there, wrapped in plain linen. Hermione untied the tape and held a corner towards the door.

In the passage light, it looked exactly as it had beside the window.

Beneath it lay a roll of warm cream fabric, light enough to fall in soft folds. A label tied to the end read, Curtaining. Washable. No enchantments.

“That sounds restful,” Hermione said.

McGonagall touched the fabric between finger and thumb.

“Let us see it at the window,” she replied.

They carried the rolls back together, Hermione holding the blue and McGonagall the cream. For several minutes, neither spoke. Their footsteps and the quiet rustle of cloth were enough.



Neville found them shortly before lunch.

He came along the gallery carrying a shallow wooden box of young plants. There was a little soil on one cuff and a pencil tucked behind his ear.

“Morning, Professor,” Neville said to McGonagall. Then his face brightened. “Hermione. I thought you’d be buried in paperwork.”

“I was,” Hermione replied. “We’re making somewhere to sit.”

Neville shifted his box and looked into the recess.

“Oh, good,” he said.

Hermione glanced up from the cream fabric she was holding against the curtain rail.

“Do people use it?”

“I’ve seen a few,” Neville replied. “Usually when the library’s busy. Someone was revising here on Wednesday.”

“Comfortably?” McGonagall asked.

Neville’s eyes travelled to the old cushion, which was now leaning against the wall.

“They kept changing position,” he admitted.

Hermione lowered the fabric.

“We were trying to decide who it should be for.”

Neville looked puzzled for a moment.

“Wouldn’t it be for whoever’s sitting there?” he asked.

McGonagall’s mouth moved slightly at one corner.

“That is certainly one approach.”

Neville bent to put the plant box down. It held several pots of small, round leaves, each leaf edged in pale silver.

“Those are lovely,” Hermione said.

“Moonmint,” Neville replied. “For the greenhouse benches. It likes the cooler light.”

“Would it like this window?” Hermione asked.

Neville examined the direction of the light, then held his hand near the glass.

“Yes,” he said. “As long as nobody waters it every time they feel helpful.”

He selected a pot from the box and set it at the far end of the sill.

The little plant made the space look occupied already.

“Would you mind?” Hermione asked.

“Not at all,” Neville replied. “I have plenty.”

McGonagall regarded the pot.

“Nothing with a tendency to sing, I hope.”

“Quite silent,” Neville assured her. “It smells a bit like mint if you brush the leaves. That’s all.”

Hermione touched one leaf with her fingertip. A cool, clean scent rose briefly.

Neville picked up his box.

“If you’re still here after lunch, I’ll bring a saucer,” he said. “That one leaks.”

After he had gone, Hermione looked at the seat again.

Whoever was sitting there.

It was a sensible answer. She was not entirely sure why she had expected a more elaborate one.



They ate lunch in McGonagall’s study.

A small table had been cleared beside the window, and there were sandwiches, a dish of sliced pears and a pot of tea. Hermione had brought the sample book, which lay open on a chair like a guest waiting to be included.

“The cream will let the light through,” Hermione said.

“And soften it in the afternoon,” McGonagall replied.

“Do we need curtains that close all the way?”

McGonagall considered the question over her cup.

“Yes,” she said. “Even if they are seldom used that way. It is pleasant to have the choice.”

Hermione nodded.

She had been thinking about warmth, colour, measurements. McGonagall had thought of the feeling of sitting beside a window on a dark afternoon and being able to draw the curtains.

“There’s a note in the book about linings,” Hermione said. “We could add something light.”

“Provided we can still lift the result,” McGonagall replied.

Hermione smiled and reached for a sandwich.

The headmistress’s study was unusually still. A silver instrument on a shelf turned once, gave a soft click and stopped. Several portraits were asleep. Somewhere beyond the door, a clock struck one.

“I used to think,” Hermione said, “that making a room useful meant making sure it had everything anyone might need.”

McGonagall set down her cup.

“And now?”

“I’m beginning to suspect that leaves very little room for the person.”

McGonagall glanced at the sample book on the chair.

“A window seat should probably retain enough space for someone to sit on it,” she said.

Hermione laughed.

They finished lunch without returning to the library proposals.

When they rose, McGonagall took a small sewing box from a drawer. It was plain walnut, polished by long use.

“I could summon a larger one,” she said, “but this has very good scissors.”

Hermione lifted the sample book from the chair.

For the first time since her arrival, she had no idea what time she would finish what she was doing, and found that she did not mind.



The afternoon work required more judgement than Hermione had expected.

McGonagall could have transformed the old cushion outright, but she wanted the new cover to last, and Hermione liked the thought of using the wool they had found.

They measured, marked and cut. McGonagall guided the scissors with her wand while Hermione held the material steady. The blades travelled in a clean line, making a soft, crisp sound.

For the filling, McGonagall brought a folded wool blanket from the linen room and a bag of clean upholstery flock. Together they made a firm inner pad, then worked the blue cover over it.

The first fitting was too tight.

Hermione sat down and felt the cushion bunch beneath her knees.

“We allowed for the width,” she said, getting up, “but not quite enough for the depth once it was filled.”

McGonagall removed her spectacles and examined the seam.

“Fortunately,” she said, “we have not yet finished the ends.”

They loosened the cover and let it settle.

The second fitting was better.

Hermione sat for a full minute, looking out over the courtyard. A small cloud moved across the sun. The flagstones changed from silver to grey.

“Well?” McGonagall asked.

“I think you should try it,” Hermione replied.

McGonagall sat, adjusted her position once and rested her hands in her lap.

“Yes,” she said.

It was a modest word, but Hermione recognised approval when she heard it.

The curtains took longer. Their hems had to fall clear of the seat, and the folds needed room to move. They replaced two cracked rings from the basket in the linen room and rubbed a little wax along the wooden rail.

When McGonagall drew the cream fabric across, it moved with a quiet sliding sound.

Sunlight came through in a pale wash.

Hermione drew it back again. The cloth settled against the stone in easy folds.

They stood together in the corridor and looked.

The recess was beginning to belong to the gallery. Nothing demanded attention. The blue took up the colour of the distant hills, while the cream caught the warmth in the oak.

Only the empty candleholder remained rather forlorn.

“A lamp,” Hermione said.

McGonagall nodded. “A small one.”



They found the table before they found the lamp.

It was tucked beneath a larger desk in an unused classroom: a little round table with three legs and a water mark shaped vaguely like a pear. Hermione cleaned it and tightened the join at the top of one leg.

It fitted beside the seat with room to spare.

The lamp came from a cupboard near McGonagall’s study. Its shade was plain parchment, its base dark green glass. McGonagall lit it with a touch of her wand.

A gentle pool of light fell across the table.

“Too low?” Hermione asked.

“For a tall reader, perhaps,” McGonagall replied.

Hermione tried it with the household charms book. The light reached the page without shining into her eyes.

McGonagall moved the lamp an inch nearer the wall.

“There,” she said.

They returned the brass candleholder to the linen-room shelf, where it could wait for somewhere that needed it. Hermione put the green button in a small dish by the gallery door, beside a lost hair ribbon and a single cufflink.

Then she took the wooden ruler to the library.

The short walk gave her a chance to notice how pleasantly tired she had become. Her shoulders had loosened. She had spent hours concentrating, but the concentration had travelled through her hands: lifting cloth, smoothing a seam, holding a curtain straight.

On her way back, she paused outside the recess.

A younger pupil was sitting on the new cushion.

Hermione could not see the pupil’s face clearly, only a bent head, dark robes and a pair of shoes planted firmly on the floor. The household charms book was open across the reader’s knees.

McGonagall stood a little further along the corridor, sorting the remaining samples into their book.

Hermione approached quietly.

“Shall we come back?” she asked in a low voice.

“We can finish here,” McGonagall replied, equally softly.

They gathered their tools at the other end of the gallery.

The pupil turned a page.

Nothing else happened, and Hermione found herself listening with unexpected satisfaction to that small, ordinary sound.



Neville returned with a saucer and a narrow-necked watering bottle.

The pupil had gone by then. The book lay closed on the table, its blue wool marker projecting neatly from the top.

Neville put the saucer beneath the moonmint.

“Someone’s been using it already,” he said.

“Yes,” Hermione replied.

“Good,” Neville said. He looked at the cushion, then at the curtains. “It looks as though it’s always been like that.”

Hermione glanced towards McGonagall, pleased.

“I hope you mean that kindly.”

“I do,” Neville said. “Some things look new in a way that makes you afraid to touch them.”

He sat down at McGonagall’s invitation, placing the watering bottle on the table.

The seat took his weight without creaking.

“This is comfortable,” Neville said.

“Firm enough?” McGonagall asked.

Neville shifted back and considered. “Yes. Though you’d want something behind you if you were sitting sideways.”

Hermione looked at the scraps of blue wool folded beside the sample book.

“A small cushion,” she said.

“Only a small one,” McGonagall replied.

They made it while Neville told them about a batch of seedlings that had finally put out roots after several weeks of apparent indecision. He spoke without hurrying, occasionally pausing to turn the moonmint so its leaves faced the light.

Hermione sewed the last short opening by hand. It would have been quicker with magic, but the stitches were easy and there were only a few.

When she had finished, she placed the little cushion against the side of the recess.

Neville tried leaning against it.

“That’s it,” he said.

McGonagall collected the scissors and closed the walnut sewing box.

The task appeared to be finished.

Hermione looked at the table, the lamp and the book.

“We ought to leave some sort of notice,” she said.

Neville looked up. “About what?”

“That anyone can sit here,” Hermione replied.

McGonagall let her gaze rest on the seat for a moment.

“Let us see whether anyone needs telling,” she said.



They put everything away before tea.

The unused cream fabric returned to the linen room, wrapped against dust. The remaining wool went beside it, with a new label giving the date and the amount. Hermione made sure the sample book’s inventory reflected what they had used.

She repaired the loose pocket at the back with a small charm.

McGonagall watched her smooth it flat.

“That book has been waiting a considerable time for someone to do that,” she said.

“It was only a little tear,” Hermione replied.

“They often are,” McGonagall said.

In the staff room, they had tea and thick slices of seed cake. Neville stayed long enough to finish one cup before returning to the greenhouses.

Hermione held her warm cup in both hands.

There were still things she could have added to the recess. A shelf beneath the window, perhaps. A footstool. A collection of books chosen especially for the place.

She imagined each of them in turn.

The shelf would catch people’s knees. The footstool would drift into the corridor. A special collection would need arranging, and returning, and deciding upon.

The household charms book had found its way there perfectly well.

McGonagall cut another thin slice of cake.

“You are considering something,” she said.

“I was wondering whether it needed anything else,” Hermione admitted.

“And does it?”

Hermione took a sip of tea before answering.

“I don’t think so.”

McGonagall passed her the plate.

“Then have some cake while that remains your conclusion.”

Outside the staff-room window, the afternoon had begun to turn gold. Hermione accepted the cake and sat back in her chair.

They spoke for a while about her work in London, then about the proposals she had come to review. There was one small alteration McGonagall wanted to make to the library opening hours.

Hermione understood it more readily now.

They made a note to discuss it in the morning.



After supper, Hermione took another walk through the castle.

She had meant to go straight to her room, but her feet led her towards the gallery. The corridors were settling into their evening quiet. Doors closed gently. Somewhere below, a piano was being played slowly, one phrase repeated until its ending came right.

The lamp beside the window seat was lit.

McGonagall sat beneath it with a slim book open in her hands.

Hermione stopped at the entrance to the gallery.

The headmistress looked up.

“I thought I ought to test it properly,” McGonagall said.

“Of course,” Hermione replied.

“The curtains work very well.”

Beyond the glass, the courtyard had become a pattern of pale stone and soft shadow. One narrow strip of sky remained blue above the opposite roof.

Hermione came closer.

“What are you reading?”

“Essays on Highland gardens,” McGonagall replied. She turned the book slightly so Hermione could see its cover. “I was given it at Christmas.”

“Have you only just started it?”

McGonagall marked her place with a finger.

“I have started it several times.”

There was room beside her. Hermione sat down, leaving the small cushion where it supported McGonagall’s elbow.

For a little while they watched the courtyard.

The moonmint gave no scent until Hermione’s sleeve brushed it. Then a faint freshness rose between them.

“I think Neville answered my question,” Hermione said eventually.

“Which question?” McGonagall asked.

“Who the seat was for.”

McGonagall looked at the book in her lap.

“Yes,” she said. “I believe he did.”

Hermione leaned back against the wood. Across the courtyard, a light appeared in an upper window.

She had spent much of her life finding a place for things. Books belonged on particular shelves. Papers needed headings. Work was easier when its purpose could be stated clearly.

This small space had required care, but apparently it had no need of a category.

A pupil had read here. Neville had rested between errands. Now McGonagall had brought a book she had been meaning to enjoy for months.

Hermione would have been sorry to see a notice hanging above them.

McGonagall opened her book again.

“Do stay if you wish,” she said.

Hermione settled more comfortably on the cushion.

“Just for a few minutes,” she replied.



The few minutes lengthened.

McGonagall read. Hermione watched the last colour leave the courtyard and listened to the soft movement of pages beside her.

A pair of pupils passed through the gallery, speaking quietly. One glanced at the recess, then continued on. Somewhere far below, a door shut with a mellow wooden sound.

The lamp made a warm circle around the book and McGonagall’s hands. Beyond it, the cream curtains rested against the stone.

Hermione let her eyes close briefly.

She was aware of the cushion beneath her, firm and comfortable, and of the familiar castle holding its evening around them. There was no report in her lap. Nothing needed measuring.

When she opened her eyes, McGonagall had reached the end of a chapter.

“I shall go up,” Hermione said.

“A sensible decision,” McGonagall replied. “You have done quite enough for one day.”

Hermione stood and smoothed the place where she had been sitting.

“Will you be here much longer?”

“Another few pages,” McGonagall said.

Hermione smiled at the answer.

“Goodnight, Professor.”

“Goodnight, Hermione.”

At the end of the gallery, Hermione looked back once.

McGonagall had returned to her book. The seat, which had seemed that morning to be waiting for someone to explain its purpose, was quietly in use.

Hermione went upstairs.



Her guest room was warm when she opened the door.

Someone had drawn the bed curtains halfway back and left a jug of water on the small chest beside the bed. Her slippers stood where she had put them that morning. The library papers waited in their tidy pile.

Hermione took the old envelope from her cardigan pocket.

Measuring, cushion, curtains, lamp, table.

Each item had a small tick beside it. Underneath, in handwriting made slightly uneven by the wall she had written against, were the words Draught stopper.

She remembered the old cushion filling.

For a moment she considered finding a fresh sheet of paper and making a note about it for the linen room. Then she folded the envelope and laid it on top of the proposals. Morning would do perfectly well.

She washed, changed into her nightclothes and hung the cardigan over the chair.

At the window, she paused to look out.

Her room faced a different part of the grounds. The lawn sloped away beneath a clear night sky, and the windows reflected a little of the lamplight behind her. She could see the edge of the lake, still and dark, with a pale gleam where the moon touched it.

Hermione drew her curtains.

Their rings made the same quiet sliding sound as the ones in the gallery.

She got into bed and settled beneath the blankets. The pillow was cool against her cheek. From somewhere in the wall came a soft tick as the room lost the last warmth of the day.

For a while she thought of the sample book: the little squares of cloth, their patient labels, the blue wool folded away for years until it was needed.

Then she pictured the gallery lamp and McGonagall turning another page.

Tomorrow, someone else would find the seat. They might read a chapter, mend a quill or sit for five minutes watching the courtyard. Perhaps they would leave the curtains open. Perhaps they would draw them against the afternoon sun.

There was nothing further Hermione needed to arrange.

She reached for her wand and lowered the lamp to a soft glow, then let it go out.

The bed curtains enclosed a gentle darkness. Hermione shifted once, finding a comfortable place for her shoulder, and drew the blanket a little higher.

Her last clear thought was of the book waiting on the small round table, its place kept by a piece of blue wool.

Then the thought loosened, the quiet deepened, and Hermione slept.


The Evening Post

By the time Harry reached Hogsmeade, the lamps were coming on above the shop doors, and the last of the afternoon lay in pale strips along the wet pavement. Rain had passed through an hour earlier. It had left the village smelling of stone, chimney smoke and the damp wool of people returning home.

Ron was waiting outside the Three Broomsticks with his hands in his coat pockets.

“You’re late,” Ron said, although he sounded pleased to see him.

“Five minutes,” Harry replied.

“I’ve been using them responsibly,” Ron said. “I’ve established that there’s shepherd’s pie.”

Harry had come north for a meeting at Hogwarts the following morning. There was nothing urgent about it: a discussion with McGonagall about arrangements for Ministry visitors, followed by lunch if the discussion remained civil. Ron, who had been delivering an order from the joke shop to a Hogsmeade stockist, had arranged to stay for supper.

For once, neither of them needed to hurry home. Their families knew where they were, Harry had a room upstairs, and the evening stretched ahead without anything written across it.

Then Hagrid came round the corner carrying a brown-paper parcel.

He held it flat on one enormous palm, with the care he might have given a sleeping creature. A length of green string crossed the paper. Tucked beneath it was a small cream card.

“Evenin’,” Hagrid said. “Glad I’ve caught yeh. Wondered if either of you might recognise these.”

He lifted a corner of the wrapping. Inside lay a pair of gardening gloves, their brown leather rubbed dark at the fingertips.

Ron looked from the gloves to Hagrid.

“Have they done something?” Ron asked.

“Been mended,” Hagrid replied. “Very neatly, an’ all. Trouble is, I can’t remember who they’re for.”



They went inside, where the warmth met them at the door.

The Three Broomsticks was comfortably busy. A few villagers occupied the tables nearest the fire, and someone had draped a wet scarf over a chair back. The scarf was turning itself at intervals, presenting each side to the heat.

Madam Rosmerta pointed them towards a table by the window.

“I’ll come over in a moment,” Rosmerta said. “And keep that parcel off the wet bit, Hagrid. Someone’s just upset a glass.”

Hagrid put it safely in the middle of the table and settled onto the broad chair that was kept for him near the hearth.

“Picked up a basket from the mender this afternoon,” Hagrid explained. “There was a strap for one of the school barrows, a buckle, couple of things for the greenhouses. An’ these. I remember takin’ them down, only it was a fortnight ago, and I’ve had a fair few things to remember since.”

Harry drew the cream card out from under the string. In small, precise handwriting, it said: Right thumb reinforced. Cuffs finished. Paid.

There was no name.

“Could the mender tell you?” Harry asked.

“Gone to visit her sister till Monday,” Hagrid said. “Left the basket next door for me. I could keep ’em till she gets back, o’ course. Only she said there was something in it wanted for tomorrow.”

Ron pulled the parcel closer.

“Could be the barrow strap,” Ron said.

“Could be,” Hagrid agreed. “But I’d sooner get everything where it belongs.”

Harry understood that. He had once imagined that a peaceful life would consist chiefly of having very little to do. Increasingly, he found he liked having one small thing to put right, provided it could be done before supper went cold.

“Let’s have a look,” Harry said.

Hagrid undid the string. The gloves were adult-sized, with broad cuffs of faded green cloth. Fresh stitching ran round the right thumb, and a small patch had been fitted so carefully into one palm that Harry noticed it only when he tilted the leather towards the lamp.

“They’re certainly not yours,” Ron told Hagrid.

“Got mine here,” Hagrid said, patting a pocket large enough to contain a loaf of bread.

He brought out one glove. It was roughly the size of the parcel.



Rosmerta arrived with three tankards and the news that there was indeed shepherd’s pie.

“Three, please,” Ron said at once.

“Make mine a large one,” Hagrid added.

Rosmerta glanced at the open parcel.

“Those came out well,” Rosmerta said.

Hagrid brightened. “Yeh know whose they are?”

“I remember seeing them before they went in,” Rosmerta replied. “The cuff was hanging off one. You were sitting just there, trying to keep a bag of bulbs from rolling under the table.”

“That sounds like me,” Hagrid said.

Harry waited while Rosmerta thought.

“It was the afternoon we had all that wind,” Rosmerta continued. “People kept coming in with leaves stuck to them. I couldn’t tell you who brought the gloves, though.”

“Anyone gardening?” Ron asked.

“Half the village, at this time of year,” Rosmerta said. “Getting things in before the ground turns difficult. I’ve still got two empty boxes round the back that I keep meaning to do something with.”

She took away an abandoned glass from the neighbouring table.

“You might ask at the little garden by the lane,” Rosmerta added. “There’s been somebody working there most afternoons.”

When she had gone, Harry turned one glove over. There was a dusting of fine grit in a fold of the cuff, and a small white mark near the wrist.

“Neville?” Harry suggested.

Hagrid stroked his beard.

“Could well be,” Hagrid said. “He brings things down with mine sometimes. But I saw him yesterday, and he didn’t mention ’em.”

“He probably has more than one pair,” Harry said.

“He has more than one of most gardening things,” Ron replied. “I went into his greenhouse once looking for a chair. There were six things that looked like chairs, and apparently none of them were.”

A brief smile moved through Hagrid’s beard.

Harry set the gloves side by side. Whoever owned them had worn the leather soft, then thought them worth repairing. The fingers had kept the slight curve of a hand resting round a tool.

“We could walk down after we’ve eaten,” Harry said. “See if anyone’s there.”

Ron looked towards the kitchen door.

“After,” Ron agreed firmly.



Supper arrived under a faint haze of steam.

For a while, the parcel rested between them while they ate and caught up with one another. Ron described a display stand at the shop which had developed the habit of applauding customers who decided against a purchase.

“George thinks it’s encouraging,” Ron said. “Hermione says it makes people feel watched.”

“What do you think?” Harry asked.

“I think it applauded a man for putting back a nose-biting teacup, and he bought three. So I’m reserving judgement.”

Hagrid had been repairing the fence beside his pumpkin patch. One stretch leaned, though the posts appeared sound.

“Ground settles,” Hagrid said. “Things grow. Yeh can have it straight as anything in spring, an’ by autumn it’s found a way of its own.”

Harry ate slowly, feeling the day loosen its hold on him. He had spent most of the afternoon travelling, with a folder of papers pressing against his knee. Now the folder was upstairs, and rainwater shone on the windows, reflecting the room in soft pieces.

Outside, a witch guided a laden shopping basket past the pub. It floated obediently at elbow height, except when it drifted towards a bakery window.

Ron followed Harry’s glance.

“Mine does that near Honeydukes,” Ron said. “Hermione thinks I’ve charmed it.”

“Have you?” Harry asked.

“No,” Ron replied. “But I admire its judgement.”

Hagrid finished first and sat back, cupping his tankard in both hands.

“Nice having you two here,” Hagrid said.

The simple way he said it quietened them for a moment.

“It’s nice being here,” Harry replied.

He meant the pub, and the village, and this particular table. He meant, too, that he could sit with Ron and Hagrid without waiting for a message to arrive or wondering how soon they would have to leave.

When Rosmerta collected the plates, she noticed Harry gathering up the string.

“If you find the owner, tell them that’s good work,” Rosmerta said. “Most people would have thrown those away.”

“Perhaps they’re comfortable,” Harry replied.

Rosmerta nodded. “That’s usually the reason.”



They stepped out into an evening that had cleared.

Above the roofs, the sky was a deep, even blue. A few stars showed between the chimney pots. Harry could see the warm square of his upstairs window, where Rosmerta had left a lamp burning.

Hagrid carried the parcel, and Ron took the side of the pavement nearest the road, where he could walk without brushing against Hagrid’s coat.

The little garden lay beside a narrow lane behind the shops. Harry had passed it before without giving it much thought. It occupied an awkward triangle of ground, too small for a building, with a low wall along one edge and a wooden bench beneath a rowan tree.

A lantern hung from the tree. Beneath it stood several pots, a folded sack, and a wheelbarrow with one handle wrapped in fresh leather.

“Well, there’s the strap,” Ron said.

Hagrid looked pleased.

“Dropped that off earlier,” Hagrid said. “Least I remembered where that went.”

The garden was empty, but someone had been working there recently. The soil in the nearest bed was newly turned, and a narrow board lay across it to keep boots from pressing it down. A trowel stood in a bucket.

Harry bent to look at a small wooden label. It bore a neat line of writing: White crocuses, three inches apart.

“That looks like Neville’s writing,” Harry said.

Ron peered over his shoulder.

“It’s legible,” Ron replied. “That narrows things down considerably from school.”

Hagrid lifted the lantern a little higher on its hook. Light spread across the bed, showing a scattering of pale bulbs arranged on the soil, ready to go in.

There was something restful about the unfinished work. Nobody had abandoned it in haste. The tools were together, the sack had been folded against the damp, and a cover waited beside the barrow. Whoever had stopped expected to return.

Harry noticed a pair of muddy kneeling pads beneath the bench.

“Perhaps he’s gone for something,” Harry said.

“Or he’s had enough sense to stop for supper,” Ron replied.



They stood for a few minutes, letting their eyes become accustomed to the garden.

A trickle of water moved somewhere behind the wall. The rowan’s remaining leaves stirred, making a dry, papery sound above them. Harry could smell the newly opened soil.

Hagrid examined the board across the bed.

“Good way of doing it,” Hagrid said. “Keeps the earth loose. Professor Sprout always used to tell me off for standing where she’d just dug.”

“You do make rather a lot of footprint,” Ron observed.

“Can’t help that,” Hagrid replied mildly.

Harry walked to the far end of the triangle. Three shallow stone bowls had been set beside the path, each filled with gravel and water. A label explained that they were for birds, and asked visitors to leave the stones in place.

Beside them, an old terracotta pot had been repaired with three shining bands.

It reminded Harry of the gloves. The garden seemed to contain a number of things that had been made useful again: the pot, the barrow, even the bench, whose newest slat was still lighter than the others.

He put his hand on the bench back. The wood was smooth and cold.

“Rosmerta said people had been working here most afternoons,” Harry said. “Does Neville come down often?”

“Once or twice a week, lately,” Hagrid replied. “Said he wanted somewhere folk could try a bit o’ gardening without worrying about getting everything wrong.”

Ron looked at the carefully spaced bulbs.

“I’d manage to put one in upside down,” Ron said.

“Neville says most of ’em work it out,” Hagrid replied.

Harry smiled. It sounded like something Neville would say with complete conviction.

From the mouth of the lane came the sound of a door closing. A figure crossed the lamplight at the far end, carrying a crate.

Harry recognised the familiar, slightly forward-leaning walk before he saw the face.

“Neville,” Harry called.



Neville turned, then came towards them with a broad smile.

“Harry! Ron! I didn’t know you were up here.”

“Neither did we, quite,” Ron said. “It’s become an investigation.”

Hagrid held out the parcel.

“Brought these back from the mender,” Hagrid explained. “Can’t place who gave ’em to me.”

Neville set his crate down by the bench and looked inside the paper.

“Oh, those,” Neville said warmly. “I wondered whether they’d be ready.”

Hagrid’s shoulders relaxed.

“Yours, then?”

“They used to be,” Neville replied. “I was having them done for someone else.”

Harry laughed softly.

“We’d got as far as your handwriting,” Harry said.

Neville glanced at the bulb label.

“I’m afraid I’ve labelled rather a lot,” Neville admitted. “People kept asking where they could stand.”

“That’s useful,” Ron said. “I generally discover where I shouldn’t have stood afterwards.”

Neville picked up the right glove, flexing the repaired thumb between his fingers.

“That’s much better,” Neville said. “It had started catching whenever you closed your hand. I asked for the cuffs to be made a little shorter as well.”

“Who are they for?” Harry asked.

“Madam Rosmerta,” Neville replied. “She’s going to help with the planters tomorrow morning.”

The answer was so close to where they had started that all four of them were quiet for a moment.

Then Ron grinned.

“She admired the stitching,” Ron said. “I think that’s promising.”



Neville sat on the edge of the bench and explained.

Rosmerta had mentioned her empty planting boxes one afternoon while Neville was having lunch. He had offered to help her fill them. That had led to a conversation about the little garden, and then to Rosmerta saying that she had never really known where to begin.

“She said she could keep a pub running through a Saturday in December,” Neville told them, “but a tray of seedlings made her feel she ought to fetch someone qualified.”

Harry could imagine her saying it.

“So you gave her your gloves,” Harry said.

“I was going to,” Neville replied. “She tried them on, and they fitted across the hand, but the cuffs got in the way. Then the thumb tore. They’d been doing that for ages.”

Neville turned the glove palm upwards.

“She liked how soft they were,” Neville continued. “New ones can be rather stiff. I thought if these were repaired properly, she’d have something comfortable to start with.”

Hagrid gave a small, thoughtful nod.

“Remember now,” Hagrid said. “Yeh brought ’em when I was loading that basket. I said I’d take ’em with the rest.”

“I should have put her name on them,” Neville replied. “I meant to write a note.”

“I should’ve remembered,” Hagrid said.

“You’ve remembered everything else,” Neville assured him. “And they’re here in plenty of time.”

Ron looked down at the crate. It contained small pots of ivy, several compact plants with silver leaves, and a bundle of wooden markers.

“Are these for the pub?” Ron asked.

“Most of them,” Neville said. “There are a few for here. I was just going to put them under cover.”

Harry looked towards the low shelter against the garden wall. The distance was only a few yards.

“We can help with that,” Harry said.



It was a small job, made smaller by four people.

Neville handed out the pots and showed them where to stand them beneath the shelter. Hagrid carried most of the crate in one hand. Ron took charge of the wooden markers and immediately asked whether any were liable to object to being put down.

“Only if you put them in the wrong pots,” Neville replied.

Ron paused.

“Was that a joke?” Ron asked.

Neville smiled. “Yes.”

“Good,” Ron said. “I like to check with plants.”

Harry lifted a pot of ivy. Its leaves were cool against his wrist, and a fine thread of water ran from the drainage hole onto his shoe. He set it beside two others, turning it so that the longest stem lay safely along the shelf.

Neville moved between them with quiet certainty. He no longer had the hurried manner Harry remembered from crowded classrooms. He looked at each plant, adjusted a leaf caught beneath a rim, and knew where everything would be happiest overnight.

“Do you do much of this?” Harry asked.

“A bit,” Neville replied. “Teaching takes most of my time. But people are more willing to ask questions here.”

“Than at school?” Harry said.

Neville nodded. “Some adults are embarrassed about not knowing things. They think everyone else has somehow learnt them already.”

Ron set down the markers.

“I’ve been assuming that about taxes for years,” Ron said.

“Plants are fairly forgiving,” Neville replied. “You can usually see what they need if you look often enough.”

Harry thought about the small patch of garden at home which he mostly left to itself. He knew where the children’s ball tended to lodge, and which step stayed damp after rain. Beyond that, he paid it less attention than it deserved.

He carried another pot across the path.

“What would you put somewhere shady?” Harry asked.

Neville’s face brightened, and for the next few minutes Harry learnt rather more about ferns than he had expected to learn that evening.



Once the plants were sheltered, Neville covered the open bag of compost and gathered the tools.

Harry reached for the trowel at the same moment as Ron. Their hands knocked together, and Ron withdrew his with exaggerated dignity.

“Years of friendship,” Ron said, “and now we’re competing for a trowel.”

“You can have it,” Harry replied.

“I don’t want it now,” Ron said. “It’s become awkward.”

Neville took it from between them and put it in the bucket.

“There,” Neville said. “Nobody’s won.”

Hagrid chuckled, a low sound that seemed to settle into the garden wall.

They checked the bed before leaving. Neville pressed the unplanted bulbs into a shallow tray and slid it beneath the bench, away from any curious birds.

“Will those be all right till morning?” Harry asked.

“Quite all right,” Neville replied. “I only stopped because I’d run out of light. There’s no hurry.”

Harry found he liked hearing that said aloud.

Hagrid lowered the lantern and extinguished it with a careful movement of his wand. The garden slipped into the softer light from the lane. Its shapes remained: the pale bench slat, the line of the wall, the dark leaves under their shelter.

Neville tucked the repaired gloves back into the paper.

“I’d better give these to Rosmerta,” Neville said. “She’s been saying she’ll use the oven gloves if necessary.”

“Don’t let her,” Ron replied. “They’ll be in the shepherd’s pie by Sunday.”

Hagrid took one last look at the barrow strap, evidently satisfied with its position, and they walked back towards the main street.

Harry fell into step beside Neville. Their shoulders brushed once in the narrow lane, and both moved aside at the same time, making Ron laugh behind them.



The pub had grown quieter.

Two tables were empty, and the scarf by the fire had dried enough to fold itself into a loose square. Rosmerta was polishing glasses behind the bar.

“Found them a home?” Rosmerta asked as they came in.

Neville placed the parcel on the counter.

“Yes,” Neville said. “They’re yours.”

Rosmerta put down the glass.

“Well,” Rosmerta said. “I ought to have recognised them.”

“You only tried them once,” Neville replied. “And the cuffs look different now.”

Rosmerta unfolded the paper and slipped her hands into the gloves. She opened and closed her fingers, then held both hands out in front of her.

“Oh, that’s comfortable,” Rosmerta said.

Hagrid watched with open pleasure.

“Thumb all right?” Hagrid asked.

Rosmerta bent it. “Much better.”

Harry saw her run one gloved fingertip over the patch in the opposite palm. The movement was small and approving.

“I’d forgotten how light they were,” Rosmerta said. “I expected gardening gloves to feel like something you wore for carrying hot bricks.”

“Some do,” Neville replied. “Those have had plenty of practice.”

Ron leaned against the bar.

“We walked all the way down the lane to discover we should have stayed here,” Ron said.

“You found Neville,” Rosmerta pointed out.

“And we moved some plants,” Harry added.

“And I learnt something about shade,” Ron said. “Apparently it isn’t a complete excuse.”

Rosmerta laughed and drew a fresh round of drinks. Neville tried to protest, but she waved him towards their table.

“For the gloves,” Rosmerta said. “And for getting me started.”



They sat together for another half-hour.

Neville explained what would go into the pub’s planting boxes: ivy to trail over the edges, silver foliage that would hold its colour, and bulbs hidden beneath the surface for spring.

Rosmerta listened while she folded the brown paper.

“So they’ll look finished tomorrow,” Rosmerta said, “and then something else will happen later?”

“Exactly,” Neville replied.

“That seems a sensible arrangement,” Rosmerta said.

She laid the gloves on a shelf behind the bar, where she would see them in the morning.

Harry watched her put them there. The shelf held an order book, a tin of spare tap fittings, and a small vase with a single late flower in it. The gloves looked at home among them.

Hagrid began asking Neville about something eating the lower leaves of his cabbages. Ron, who had initially appeared willing to follow this discussion, gradually became absorbed in peeling the label from an empty bottle.

Harry let the conversation flow round him. He had no contribution to make about cabbages, and nobody expected one.

A fresh log settled in the fire. From upstairs came the muffled tread of a guest crossing a room. Rosmerta went to answer a quiet call from the far table, leaving the folded paper beside Neville’s elbow.

“Worth mending those,” Hagrid said at last.

Neville nodded. “I thought so.”

Harry remembered the curve of the leather fingers and the new thread round the thumb. Tomorrow, Rosmerta would pull the gloves on, perhaps a little self-consciously at first, and fill the boxes outside her own back door.

He found himself looking forward to seeing them.



Hagrid was the first to leave.

He had an early start, and the walk to his cottage would take longer if he stopped to inspect every damp movement in the verge, as Ron suspected he might.

“Just worms, probably,” Hagrid said.

“That’s what I’m afraid of,” Ron replied. “You’ll want to make sure they’re all comfortable.”

Hagrid gave Ron’s shoulder an affectionate squeeze that lowered him slightly in his chair.

“See yeh tomorrow, Harry,” Hagrid said. “Come down if you’ve time after your meeting.”

“I will,” Harry promised.

Neville left with Hagrid, carrying the empty crate. At the door, Neville turned back.

“Ten o’clock, Rosmerta?” Neville asked.

“I’ll be ready,” Rosmerta replied. “With the proper gloves.”

When the door closed behind them, Ron stretched his legs beneath the table.

“Good evening,” Ron said.

Harry looked at him.

“It was,” Harry agreed.

“Even with the gardening,” Ron added.

They lingered over the last of their drinks. Ron spoke about catching the train home the next day, then remembered an order he had meant to check and wrote himself a note on the back of an old receipt.

Harry resisted the impulse to take out his meeting papers. There would be time enough in the morning. He already knew what he thought about most of them.

Eventually Ron stood and buttoned his coat.

“I’d better go before George decides I’ve joined the branch permanently,” Ron said.

Harry walked him to the door. The village beyond was almost still, with pools of light beneath the lamps and a little mist gathering where the road dipped.

Ron lifted a hand, then set off towards the place where he could Apparate. Harry watched until his friend had passed the last shop window, his long shadow folding across the pavement.



Upstairs, Harry’s room was warm.

A fire had been laid in the small grate, and his bag stood on a wooden chair. Beside the bed, a jug of water wore a knitted cover that stirred faintly whenever the room cooled.

Harry shut the door and stood for a moment without moving.

The quiet here had a different texture from the quiet downstairs. There was no conversation running beneath it, only the occasional click from the fire and the soft sound of water passing through an old pipe.

He took off his coat and hung it on a peg.

The folder of papers lay where he had left it. Harry moved it to the table, squared its edges, and put his glasses beside it. Then he changed into his nightclothes and drew back the bedcover.

Through the window he could see a narrow piece of the street. The pub sign moved slightly in the night air. Beyond it, the roofs climbed towards darkness, and somewhere past them lay the lane and its little garden.

Harry pictured the plants standing close together under the shelter. He remembered Neville turning a pot so a stem would not be crushed, and Hagrid looking pleased at the fit of a repaired strap. Rosmerta’s gloves waited on their shelf.

In the morning, Harry thought, he might ask to borrow a trowel before going up to the castle. There would be room for one more person beside the planting boxes, and he was fairly sure he could manage ivy.

He lay down and felt the mattress give beneath him. The linen was cool at first, then warmed along his legs.

Downstairs, a chair moved across the floor. A door clicked shut. Harry could no longer distinguish the words of the last conversation at the bar, only its low, companionable rise and fall.

His thoughts drifted towards home, towards familiar rooms and faces, then loosened into impressions of lamplight, soft leather and the smell of earth.

The fire settled gently in the grate.

Before the room had quite fallen silent, Harry was asleep.


A Shelf for Small Things

By four o’clock, Neville Longbottom had moved the same ball of string to five different places.

It had begun beside the seed ledger, where it prevented the book from opening properly. He had transferred it to the windowsill, where it rolled into a damp patch, then to the drawer beneath the potting bench, where it vanished among labels and old receipts. Now it sat in his coat pocket, making a small, determined lump against his hip.

The greenhouse potting room was quiet around him. Rain whispered on the roof, and the late October light lay pale upon the scrubbed benches. Beyond the connecting door, a self-watering can moved slowly along a row of ferns, tipping itself at each pot with the solemn care of a waiter.

Neville took the string out of his pocket.

“What I’d like to know,” he said, “is where you ought to live.”

“That depends,” Luna Lovegood replied from the doorway, “on how often you’re needed.”

Neville turned, smiling before he had quite finished turning. Luna stood with rain shining on her blue coat and a small overnight bag hanging from one shoulder. She had written to say she would arrive after lunch. Neville had put the letter somewhere sensible and then forgotten that sensible place entirely.

“Luna. I thought you were coming tomorrow.”

Luna unfastened her coat. “Tomorrow is when I’m leaving.”

“Yes. Of course it is.” Neville put the string on the bench again. “You’ve caught me organising.”

“I thought you looked unusually surrounded.”

They had known one another long enough for Neville to hear the affection in that. He took her coat and hung it beside his own. Her visit had been arranged for weeks, a quiet Friday between journeys, with supper at Hogwarts and a guest room upstairs. She wanted to see the greenhouses in their autumn condition. He wanted to hear where she had been, and to show her the little copper kettle he had finally persuaded to stop whistling during lessons.

Instead, she had arrived at the precise moment he was considering whether string could reasonably be stored inside a teapot.

Luna looked about the room. It was narrow and warmly untidy, with a stone sink, a deep cupboard, two benches and a window overlooking the kitchen garden. On one wall, between the door and Neville’s desk, three pale rectangles showed where notices had once hung.

“There used to be a shelf there,” Neville said, following her gaze. “It came down when we had the wall repaired. I meant to put one back.”

Luna placed her bag beneath the desk. “Perhaps that’s where your string lives.”

“Perhaps. Though I don’t want another shelf that becomes a place for everything nobody knows what to do with.”

Luna considered the ball on the bench.

“Then we’ll have to work out what belongs on it.”

Neville had imagined spending her visit showing her things. It was pleasant, unexpectedly, to be offered help with something he had not managed to begin.

He went to fetch the shelf.

It was waiting behind a cupboard, wrapped in a towel which had once been white. Neville drew it out and laid it across the bench. The wood was honey-coloured oak, with a rounded front edge and two plain brackets. Someone had written POT ROOM underneath in pencil.

“I found it in the stores in September,” Neville explained. “It’s the right size. I’ve been waiting until I knew what to put on it.”

Luna ran a finger along the rounded edge.

“That may be why it’s been waiting too.”

Neville laughed. He could be patient with things that took years to flower, but he sometimes needed a friend to point out that putting up a shelf did not require the same preparation.

They cleared a space on the bench. Luna found a cloth, Neville brought a bowl of warm water from the sink, and together they washed away the dust. The oak darkened beneath their hands, releasing a faint, clean smell.

Outside, rain gathered at the corners of the panes. A robin landed on the garden wall, shook itself and disappeared into the ivy.

Luna worked slowly along one bracket.

“Who will use it?” she asked.

“Me. The students. Hagrid, sometimes. Anyone helping out in here.”

“And what do they usually need?”

Neville glanced towards the deep cupboard.

“That’s rather the problem. They ask for all sorts of things.”

He opened the cupboard to demonstrate.

There were stacks of terracotta pots, a sack of clean sand, watering attachments, folded aprons, several empty tins and a large basket containing objects that had accumulated over the term. Neville lifted the basket out and set it between them.

A wooden label fell through a gap in the wicker.

“Oh, good,” he said. “I was looking for that.”

Luna picked it up. It read PLEASE LEAVE THE LID ON.

“Which lid?” she asked.

“I can’t remember.”

Luna laid the label aside. “We may have to leave that one until we do.”

They began with the string.

Neville explained that he used it for tying stems, securing bundles of labels and fastening sacks that no longer trusted their own drawcords. Students borrowed it almost every day.

“Definitely the shelf,” Luna said.

Next came a small pair of brass scissors, three pencils, a tin of plant labels, two smooth stones, a spoon, a broken hinge and a narrow wooden dibber.

Neville put the hinge directly into a box beneath the bench.

“Mending,” he said firmly.

Luna examined the spoon.

“Tea?” she asked.

“Compost measurements.”

“Then it shouldn’t look so inviting.”

Neville found a pencil and wrote COMPOST on a label, then tied it through the hole in the spoon’s handle. Luna gave an approving nod.

For the first time that afternoon, the collection before them began to seem manageable.

They discovered that sorting was easy until Neville knew the history of a thing.

The smooth stones, for instance, were useful for weighting open the seed ledger. One had come from the edge of the lake; the other had been given to him by a second-year who had thought it resembled a kneazle asleep. It did, slightly, if the light came from the left.

The little tin contained perfectly ordinary labels, but the tin itself had belonged to Professor Sprout. Neville still remembered her setting it down on a bench and explaining, without looking up, that a label written later was a label never written.

“I use that every morning,” Neville said.

“Then it sounds very at home here,” Luna replied.

The dibber proved more complicated. It was too small for most of Neville’s work, though useful when first-years planted beans. He turned it over in his hands.

“It might go on the shelf.”

Luna looked at the growing line of objects. “How often?”

“Twice a year.”

“Perhaps it could live near the beans.”

Neville returned it to a shallow drawer, where the bean trays were stored. The drawer closed without objection.

“That’s better,” he admitted.

Luna had found a little bell beneath a folded apron. She held it up by its loop.

“What does this do?”

“Rings,” Neville said.

Luna smiled at him.

“Yes, sorry. I mean, it’s for when someone’s working at the far end of the greenhouses. The doors carry the sound along. You ring if you need another pair of hands.”

She rang it once.

A clear, modest note travelled through the room. In the adjoining greenhouse, the watering can paused, turned its spout towards the sound, and then resumed its work.

“That seems useful,” Luna said.

“It is, when I can find it.”

They placed it beside the string.

Gradually they assembled a promising group: scissors, pencils, labels, bell, string, a folded cloth and the two stones for holding pages open. The shelf would take them easily, with space to spare.

Neville looked pleased for almost half a minute.

Then he opened the desk drawer in search of a ruler and found the woollen gloves.

They were a small pair, green, with one thumb darned in yellow. A student had left them after a cold outdoor lesson the previous week. Neville had meant to take them up to the castle.

“Those shouldn’t go on the shelf,” he said.

Luna touched the yellow thumb. “Because they belong to someone?”

“Yes. But people do leave things. Gloves, hair ribbons, handkerchiefs. I ought to have somewhere for them.”

She looked at the shelf, then at the window.

“Perhaps somewhere else. Otherwise someone looking for scissors might spend the afternoon wearing a stranger’s gloves.”

Neville could imagine that happening with uncomfortable clarity.

He put the gloves on top of Luna’s bag so he would remember them at supper. Then, because his hand was already in the drawer, he took out a packet of biscuits.

“I knew there was a reason I kept this drawer.”

The kettle was on a little iron stand beside the sink. Neville tapped it with his wand and filled the teapot while Luna washed two cups.

The kettle gave one soft, satisfied puff.

“You’ve changed it,” Luna observed.

“It used to whistle whenever anyone said ‘steep’. That was awkward in Herbology.”

“I imagine there are quite a lot of steep slopes in conversations about roots.”

“More than you’d think.”

They carried their tea to the window bench, where the glass was warm from the room and cool near the frame. Neville opened the biscuits. They were ginger ones, a little softer than they ought to have been.

Luna accepted one without comment.

The rain had thinned. On the far side of the garden, someone’s abandoned wheelbarrow was filling with a collection of bright leaves. A gust sent two more turning down into it.

Neville rested his cup against his knee.

“I thought I’d have this room properly arranged by now.”

“How long have you been teaching here?” Luna asked.

“Long enough to stop using the word ‘temporary’ about that basket.”

Luna looked towards it. “You’ve done rather a lot of other things.”

“Yes, but this is such a small thing.”

“Small things can take a long time when you only remember them while you’re looking for something else.”

Neville smiled into his tea. That was exactly what happened. He would need a pencil, open three drawers, make a private promise to sort everything out on Saturday, and then discover that Saturday contained marking, pruning, a staff meeting and somebody’s pumpkin behaving unexpectedly.

“It would be nice,” he said, “to reach for something and find it.”

Luna nodded. “That seems a good reason for a shelf.”

They sat for a while, eating their biscuits. Luna told him about a boarding house where the landlady kept all the teaspoons in a vase because she disliked opening drawers. Neville told Luna about the first-year who had carefully labelled every pot in a tray, including an empty one marked NOTHING YET.

“He said it was the most accurate label,” Neville added.

“I like him,” Luna said.

By the time they finished their tea, the rain had stopped.

Neville rose and picked up his wand.

“Shall we see whether it fits?”

The shelf lifted from the bench and floated towards the wall. Luna stood a little way back while Neville held it in place.

“Higher?” he asked.

“A little lower,” Luna replied. “For the first-years.”

Neville lowered it.

“Too low for Hagrid, though.”

Luna tilted her head. “Most things are.”

They settled on a height Neville could reach comfortably and a first-year could manage without standing on anything. With a few careful movements of his wand, he fixed the brackets into the stone. The shelf gave a tiny creak, then became still.

He tested it with his hand before letting go.

“There.”

The bare board looked surprisingly hopeful against the old wall.

They placed the tin of labels at one end, then the pencils in a jam jar. Neville arranged the scissors beside them, laid down the cloth, and set the stones towards the back. Luna put the bell near the front edge where it could be seen.

The string rolled off.

Neville caught it against his chest.

“I might have expected that.”

Luna looked around for a suitable container. The tins were too shallow or too large. A flowerpot held the string well enough, but its rough drainage hole caught the end when Neville tried to pull some out.

“We need something smooth,” he said.

“And heavy enough to stay,” Luna added.

Neville turned the pot in his hands. “I’ll find something.”

They left the string on the bench for the moment.

Then Neville stood back again, and something in his expression made Luna glance at him.

“What is it?” she asked.

“Hagrid.”

Luna waited.

“He comes in for labels and string sometimes. He asked whether I’d put the shelf up yet.” Neville rubbed the side of his nose. “I said I’d tell him when I did. I think he’s got something for it.”

“Then we’d better leave a space.”

Neville looked along the board. Everything had fitted so neatly that he had spread the objects out, giving the tin and jar an air of considerable importance.

“How much space?”

Luna glanced towards the doorway.

“With Hagrid, perhaps a generous amount.”

They moved the labels and pencils closer together.

Neville wrote a short note asking Hagrid to call in before supper, folded it into a square and touched it with his wand. The paper gave a small twitch, lifted, and slipped out through the open upper window.

Luna watched it cross the garden.

“Does that always find him?”

“Usually. Once it found his coat. He’d left it over a fence.”

“Was the coat able to help?”

“It waited until he came back.”

While they waited, Neville suggested a walk through the greenhouses. They had cleared the bench, the shelf was sound, and the air outside the potting room had that rich, leafy warmth Luna had always liked.

They went slowly between the rows. In the first greenhouse, seedlings stood beneath glass covers, each carrying its own tiny weather. One tray held a fine mist; another enjoyed a clear golden glow. Neville adjusted a cover with his fingertips.

“Too much sun in that one,” he said.

Luna bent to read a label. “Does it know it’s October?”

“I’m trying to persuade it.”

They passed broad leaves that folded politely away from the path and a row of young plants whose stems hummed when the watering can went by. Neville stopped once to free a tendril from a support it had chosen unwisely. Luna held his cup while he worked, though it had been empty for some time.

At the far door, they stepped into the garden.

The evening was clearing. Water shone in the seams between the stones, and the hills beyond the grounds had softened to blue. From the castle came the distant movement of people gathering before supper.

Luna slipped her hands into her cardigan pockets.

“Do you still go up to the Great Hall every evening?” she asked.

“Most evenings. Sometimes I take a tray back to my rooms if I’ve got work.”

“Do you do the work?”

Neville gave her a sideways look.

“Sometimes.”

Luna’s smile was small and untroubled.

They followed the path along the greenhouse wall. Beneath the window, Neville noticed a student’s pencil lying in the gravel. He picked it up, wiped it on his sleeve, and put it in his pocket.

“A beginning for the jar,” Luna said.

“It’ll probably be borrowed by Monday.”

“That sounds right.”

Neville looked back through the window. From outside, the new shelf was a pale line against the darker wall. He could see the bell, the tin and the jar. They looked almost as though they had always been there.

He found that he was looking forward to Monday.

Hagrid arrived carrying something wrapped in a brown checked cloth.

He filled the doorway with the cool smell of outdoors, his beard dotted with rainwater shaken from the trees. Neville moved the stool away from the bench to give him room.

“Got yer note,” Hagrid said. “Looks good, that.”

He nodded at the shelf.

“We’re still deciding a few things,” Neville replied. “Luna’s been helping.”

“Hello, Hagrid,” Luna said. “We’ve left you a space.”

Hagrid looked at the gap with an expression of pleased embarrassment.

“Didn’t want ter get in the way. Just thought it might be useful.”

He unwrapped the cloth.

Inside was a shallow wooden tray, scarcely longer than Neville’s hand. It had raised sides, a sloping front and a little round cup fixed into one corner. The wood was dark and rubbed smooth. Along one edge, a row of small pegs stood upright like blunt teeth.

Neville lifted it carefully.

“Did you make this?”

Hagrid nodded.

“Bit of pear wood. Been seasonin’ in the shed for ages. Thought yer could put yer string in the cup. Feed it through there, see?”

He pointed to a smooth notch in the rim.

Neville placed the ball inside and drew the end through. It moved freely.

“Oh, that’s excellent.”

“Pegs are fer loops,” Hagrid explained. “Short bits worth keepin’. Always end up in a knot if yer put ’em all together.”

Luna ran a finger over the polished corner.

“What’s the tray for?”

“Whatever yer usin’.” Hagrid’s large hand hovered above it. “Labels, perhaps. Or somethin’ yer need to leave out till tomorrow. Stops it gettin’ swept off with the earth.”

Neville set the tray in the space they had left.

It fitted, but only just. The back corner pressed against the jam jar, and the bell had to move.

Hagrid noticed at once.

“Could make it smaller.”

“No,” Neville said. “No, it’s the right size.”

He lifted the two stones off the shelf and laid them on the desk, beside the ledger they were meant to hold open. Then he moved the folded cloth to a hook beneath the sink.

The tray settled squarely on the oak.

“There,” Neville said.

Luna placed the scissors in the shallow section, handles facing out. Neville put two short lengths of string over the pegs and drew a fresh length from the cup.

Nothing rolled. Nothing caught.

Hagrid watched with quiet satisfaction.

“Rubbed that notch down three times,” he said. “Didn’t want it frayin’.”

“You’ve thought of everything,” Neville replied.

Hagrid looked pleased, though he bent to fold the checked cloth with great attention.

They tried the shelf as though a lesson were about to begin.

Neville fetched a small empty pot and set it on the bench. Luna reached for a label and a pencil. Hagrid drew off a piece of string, holding the ball steady with one finger until Neville pointed out that he did not need to.

“Habit,” Hagrid said, withdrawing his hand.

Neville cut the string. Luna wrote PRACTICE on the label in her fine, rather floating handwriting, and they tied it loosely around the pot.

For a moment, all three regarded their work.

“Very well labelled,” Luna said.

“Won’t mistake it for anything else,” Hagrid agreed.

Neville laughed, and Hagrid’s broad face opened into a smile.

Then Luna lifted the empty pot away.

“What happens to the things afterwards?” she asked.

Neville looked at the label and the short piece of string on the bench.

The question was so ordinary that he almost answered without thinking. Instead, he unwound the string, hung it over one of Hagrid’s pegs, and slipped the label into the front of the tray.

“We use them again.”

“And the scissors?” Luna asked.

Neville returned them to the tray.

The little arrangement looked ready.

Hagrid leaned closer to inspect the bell.

“Found that, then?”

“Bottom of a basket,” Neville admitted.

“Thought I hadn’t heard it lately.”

Neville rang it softly. The note travelled through the open door, and somewhere in the next greenhouse the watering can paused again.

“Useful sound,” Hagrid said.

Luna glanced at the remaining strip of empty oak beside the tin.

“Shall we leave that bit?”

Neville considered it. There was room for another jar, perhaps, or a little box. He could already think of several things that might go there.

Then he looked at Hagrid’s tray, holding just the few objects they had needed.

“Yes,” Neville said. “Let’s leave it.”

Hagrid stayed while Neville put the spare tins away. Luna brushed the bench, gathering the dry crumbs of soil into a dustpan. The room seemed larger now, although none of the furniture had moved.

Before they left, Neville found a small basket for lost belongings and placed it beneath the coat hooks. The green gloves went inside, beside a ribbon Luna had discovered behind the cupboard.

Neville took them out again.

“Supper,” he reminded himself.

He put the gloves in his pocket.

Hagrid held the door while they collected their coats. At the threshold, Neville turned for one last look.

The shelf was modest enough: a jar, a tin, a bell, and the wooden tray with its ball of string. Beneath it, the bench was clear.

He extinguished the lamps.

They walked up to the castle together beneath a sky the colour of clean slate.

Hagrid took the slope slowly so that they could remain abreast. Luna carried her overnight bag, and Neville carried the empty cups, having remembered them just before locking the greenhouse door. Warm windows shone ahead of them.

At the entrance hall, Hagrid made his farewells. He had supper waiting at his own hearth and wanted to be home before the evening grew colder.

“Tell me if the pegs want movin’,” Hagrid said.

“I will,” Neville promised. “Thank you.”

Luna touched Hagrid’s sleeve.

“The pear wood is lovely,” she said.

Hagrid looked down at her. “Thought you’d notice.”

He went back down the steps, his great coat moving in the evening breeze.

In the Great Hall, Neville found the owner of the gloves before the soup arrived. The girl accepted them with obvious relief and immediately put them on, despite the warmth.

“Yellow thumb,” she explained. “My grandmother did it.”

“I thought you’d want them back,” Neville said.

When he returned to his place, Luna was looking up at the enchanted ceiling. The clouds had parted above the floating candles, showing a broad, quiet stretch of stars.

“It’s a good ceiling for coming indoors,” she said.

Neville sat beside her. “I sometimes forget to look.”

They ate leek soup, bread still warm in the middle, and a dish of potatoes with rosemary. Around them, the familiar sounds of supper rose and settled: cutlery, conversation, the scrape of a bench, someone laughing several tables away.

Luna asked about Neville’s classes, and he told her about the students who had begun the term afraid of getting soil on their sleeves and now argued cheerfully over whose cuttings had rooted first. He asked about her travels. She described a quiet valley where the mornings smelled of wild mint, and an innkeeper who kept a chair by the fire for anyone who arrived with wet shoes.

By the time the dishes cleared, Neville felt as though the day had lengthened pleasantly without becoming tiring.

He showed Luna to her guest room, where a lamp had already been lit. She left her bag by the bed and came back to the doorway.

“What will you put in the tray tomorrow?” she asked.

Neville thought of the practice label.

“Something I’m in the middle of.”

“That sounds useful,” Luna replied.

He wished her goodnight and continued along the corridor to his own rooms.

Neville’s sitting room was warm, with a low fire behind the grate and a reading lamp beside the armchair. His slippers waited under the chair, one upright and one lying on its side.

He set them straight with his foot, hung up his coat, and took the pencil from his pocket.

For a moment he considered putting it on the mantelpiece.

Instead, he laid it beside his keys. He would see it there in the morning and take it down to the greenhouse.

There was a little marking to do. Neville looked at the pile on his desk, then at the clock. Nothing was needed before Monday. He placed a sheet of paper over the essays to keep them tidy and closed the ink bottle.

At the window, he paused.

The greenhouses were dark shapes below the castle, their roofs holding a faint reflection of the sky. He could just distinguish the path they had taken and the pale wall of the kitchen garden.

Tomorrow he would need labels for the winter seedlings. He would reach for the tin. There would be a pencil beside it, scissors in the tray, and string that came smoothly through the polished notch.

He smiled, drew the curtains, and went to wash.

The water was pleasantly hot. He changed into his pyjamas, folded his clothes over the chair, and turned down the bed. The sheets felt cool against his feet. Beyond the bedroom door, the fire gave a small settling sound.

Neville switched off the lamp.

For a little while, he lay listening to the familiar quiet of his rooms. He thought of Luna in the guest room down the corridor, of Hagrid home by his hearth, and of the shelf waiting in the potting room.

There was still that little space beside the tin.

He was glad they had left it.

His breathing slowed. The warmth beneath the blankets reached his toes, and his hand relaxed upon the sheet. Before the last faint sounds of the castle had faded, Neville was asleep.


The Quiet Table

Ron first noticed the little table because someone had put a folded blanket beneath one of its legs.

It stood near the window of a sitting room on the third floor of Hogwarts, where the November afternoon had gathered into a soft grey light. There were five chairs around it, none of them alike. One had a high, straight back; another was upholstered in faded green; a third looked as though it had spent most of its life beside a kitchen fire. The remaining two had been pushed so close together that their arms overlapped.

Professor McGonagall stood beside the hearth with a small sheaf of parchment.

“I was assured this room would be suitable for an informal supper,” McGonagall said. “I should perhaps have been more specific about the furniture.”

Hermione set her overnight bag beside the sofa.

“It’s a lovely room,” Hermione said.

It was. A fire had been laid, the shelves held books that appeared to have been read, and the window seat was deep enough for two people who knew each other well. Beyond the glass, the lawns sloped towards the lake. Ron could see a gardener’s wheelbarrow standing beneath a bare tree.

“The room’s fine,” Ron said. He touched the table with two fingers. It tipped towards him, then settled back on its blanket. “The question is whether we can all eat without wearing most of it.”

“That,” McGonagall replied, “is what I hoped you might help me establish.”

Ron had come prepared for a weekend of being fed. He considered the table again.

“How long have we got?”

“Neville and Luna will join us at half past six,” McGonagall said. “It is now a quarter to four.”

“Plenty of time,” Ron said.

Hermione looked at him with the affectionate caution she generally reserved for those words.

Their invitation had arrived a fortnight earlier: one quiet Saturday at Hogwarts, supper with a few old friends, and a guest room for the night. Ron and Hermione’s children were staying with his parents, who had accepted the arrangement before either child had finished explaining it. Hermione had brought a book. Ron had brought a second pair of socks and a firm intention not to be useful until Monday.

Yet there was something agreeable about this particular problem. It required no journey, no committee and, so far as he could see, no one telling him that his answer must be submitted in duplicate.

McGonagall laid her parchment on the mantelpiece.

“The larger rooms are in use,” McGonagall explained. “I thought we might enjoy somewhere less formal. Neville suggested this one, since it is close to the guest rooms.”

“Very considerate of him,” Ron said, examining the two chairs whose arms had become entangled. “Especially if he knew about these.”

Hermione drew one chair away. The other came with it.

For a moment they both stood holding the furniture.

“Perhaps,” Hermione said, “we should begin by separating them.”

Once apart, the chairs proved to be a narrow wooden armchair and a broad one covered in blue wool. The broad chair had surprisingly short legs. Ron sat down in it and found the table edge level with his chest.

“No,” Ron said.

McGonagall’s mouth moved at one corner.

“An unusually decisive contribution, Mister Weasley.”

“Ron,” Hermione said gently, as she had several times since they had arrived.

“I am aware,” McGonagall replied. “Old habits are persistent.”

Ron got up again. He had thought returning to Hogwarts as an adult would make the castle feel smaller. Instead, certain things remained precisely the same size: the windows, the staircases, and Professor McGonagall’s capacity to make him stand a little straighter.

He took off his coat.

“Right,” Ron said. “Let’s see what we’ve actually got.”

The table was made of dark wood, with a narrow drawer at one end and a leaf folded down on either side. Opened fully, it might accommodate five people, though not if they wanted much supper. One corner was rounded from years of use. A small pale circle marked the place where a hot cup had once stood.

Hermione knelt beside the leg with the blanket beneath it.

“The floor slopes,” Hermione said.

“So does the table,” Ron replied.

“We should find out how much of each.”

McGonagall watched Hermione remove the blanket, which proved to be a rather good travelling rug folded six times. The table dropped noticeably.

Ron caught the teapot before it slid.

There was a short silence.

“I had not realised there was a teapot on it,” McGonagall said.

“Neither had I,” Ron replied, placing it safely on the mantelpiece. “That’s something we’ve learnt.”

The teapot was empty. It had a chipped lid and a little knitted cover shaped like an ordinary brown hen. Hermione turned it once in her hands before setting it down.

“Shall we move the table away from the window?” Hermione suggested. “The boards may be more even.”

They shifted it towards the hearth. Ron lifted one end while McGonagall guided the other with her wand. Hermione watched the legs. A faint rectangular outline emerged on the carpet where the table had stood.

In its new position it rocked less, but one foot still hovered above the floor.

Ron crouched.

“There’s something missing here.”

The short leg ended in a small brass socket. The other legs had wooden feet, each screwed into a similar fitting. One foot had been turned higher than the others.

Hermione leaned down beside him.

“They’re adjustable,” Hermione said.

Ron tried the nearest foot. It moved with a dry little squeak.

“Well,” Ron said, “that’s clever.”

“Until someone loses one,” McGonagall observed.

The drawer seemed the obvious place to look.

Inside, Ron found three candle ends, a bundle of plain string, a folded square of green baize and a paper packet labelled FELT. There was also a drawer handle, which was puzzling, since the drawer already possessed one.

There was no wooden foot.

Hermione lifted out the baize and shook it gently. A brass screw rolled into Ron’s palm.

“Promising,” Hermione said.

Ron looked from the screw to the table.

“Only if we’re planning to grow the rest.”

McGonagall took the packet of felt.

“These will at least prevent us from scratching the floor while we experiment,” McGonagall said.

They put a felt square under each leg and set the travelling rug across the sofa. Then Hermione stood back, her wand held loosely at her side.

“We could repair the missing foot,” Hermione said. “If we had it.”

“We could make another,” Ron replied.

“Yes. Although I’d rather understand how the table is meant to stand before we alter it.”

Ron approved of that. He had spent enough time helping George with shop fittings to know that a charm could leave a cupboard looking beautifully straight while every drawer inside it remained impossible to open.

He lifted one of the folded leaves.

A wooden support swung out underneath, stopped halfway and refused to go any further.

“Oh,” Ron said. “There’s another bit.”

McGonagall set her parchment down again.

“I shall ask for tea,” McGonagall said.

By the time the tea arrived, they had discovered that one leaf was supported properly and the other was not. The obstructed support had been turned the wrong way on its hinge. It had probably happened when the table was folded.

Ron eased it back, lifted the leaf slightly, and swung the support underneath.

The wood settled with a sound of quiet agreement.

Hermione placed a cup on the opened leaf. It stayed where it was.

“That’s much better,” Hermione said.

Ron sat in the kitchen chair and tested the space beneath.

His knee struck the support.

He shifted sideways.

His other knee struck a crosspiece.

“Better for the cup,” Ron said.

They drank their tea from the sofa and the window seat, leaving the table unoccupied in the middle of the room.

Outside, the light had thinned. A few students crossed the lawn with their scarves pulled up, taking the diagonal route towards the entrance. Somewhere below, a door closed and a burst of laughter travelled briefly along the corridor.

Ron held his cup between both hands.

“How many chairs does Hogwarts have?” Ron asked.

“I have never commissioned a count,” McGonagall replied.

“Thousands, surely.”

“Very likely.”

“And we’ve managed to find the five that don’t go together.”

Hermione looked over her cup.

“We don’t necessarily need them to match.”

“I’d settle for them all reaching the table.”

McGonagall stirred her tea once.

“I could have a suitable set brought up,” McGonagall said. “This need not become an undertaking.”

Ron glanced at the little table. With one leaf raised and the other hanging, it looked halfway through getting dressed.

“No, it’s all right,” Ron said. “I want to see if we can make it work.”

Hermione’s expression softened. She knew that particular inclination in him: a minor difficulty, a little time, and suddenly he would be unable to leave the thing alone.

“Then perhaps we should think about the people,” Hermione said.

Ron nodded towards the blue chair.

“That one’s for someone with much shorter legs.”

“Or,” Hermione replied, “it belongs somewhere other than a dining table.”

They carried the blue chair to the fire. It looked immediately at home there.

The narrow armchair went beside it. Without the broad chair pressing against its side, it seemed quite a decent chair.

“That leaves three,” Ron said.

“And two people who haven’t arrived,” Hermione replied.

McGonagall set down her cup.

“There is a small furniture store at the end of this corridor,” McGonagall said. “We might inspect it before we resort to transfiguring the coal scuttle.”

The store was less a room than a deep cupboard, lit by a window no wider than Ron’s hand. Several framed landscapes leaned against the wall. A folding screen stood behind a rolled carpet. There were two hat stands, a washstand, a chest of draughts pieces and three chairs.

Ron brightened.

Then he saw that one chair had no seat.

“Useful if we invite a ghost,” Ron said.

Hermione examined the other two. One was a plain dining chair with a loose cushion. The second was a small stool, its surface polished smooth at the edges.

McGonagall rested a hand on the dining chair.

“This should do.”

Ron picked up the stool.

Something clicked inside it.

He stopped.

Hermione glanced over.

“What was that?”

“Either it’s meant to do that,” Ron said, “or I’ve just made it worse.”

The stool had a shallow compartment beneath its seat. A wooden catch, nearly flush with the edge, held it closed. Ron pressed it and lifted the lid.

Inside lay a little dusting brush and a dark wooden foot with a brass screw at one end.

Ron held it up.

Hermione laughed, a brief, pleased sound that made the narrow cupboard seem warmer.

“Well done,” Hermione said.

“I haven’t done anything yet,” Ron replied. “It might belong to a completely different troublesome table.”

But the grain matched, as did the brass thread, and the foot fitted neatly into the empty socket.

Ron screwed it into place while Hermione steadied the table. McGonagall turned the other feet until all four stood firmly on their felt squares.

No blanket was required.

Ron put both palms on the top and leaned a little.

The table remained still.

“There,” Ron said.

McGonagall regarded it with satisfaction.

“A considerable improvement.”

They brought in the dining chair and left the stool by the sofa, where it could support a tray. There were now four chairs around the table, and two comfortable ones by the fire.

For a moment, the room appeared finished.

Then Hermione laid five folded napkins on the tabletop.

Ron studied the available space.

“Ah.”

The fifth person would have to sit at the end with the drawer. Unfortunately, the drawer projected far enough beneath the top to make that position uncomfortable. The opposite end had a crossbar. With both leaves raised, there was room for plates, but very little room for the people who intended to use them.

Hermione put down the last napkin.

“We’ve made a stable table,” Hermione said. “We haven’t made a supper table.”

Ron pulled out the kitchen chair again and sat down. He tried turning his knees to the side.

“No,” Ron said. “We haven’t.”

At five o’clock, Neville arrived carrying a shallow basket covered with a clean cloth.

“I thought these might be useful,” Neville said.

He lifted the cloth to reveal several small pots of thyme, each tucked into a paper sleeve.

“For the table?” Hermione asked.

“If there’s room,” Neville said. Then he looked properly at the table. “Perhaps the windowsill.”

“That’s becoming the difficulty,” Ron replied.

Neville put the basket down and listened while they explained. He tried the high-backed chair, stood up, and shifted it six inches to the left.

“The leg’s in the way there,” Neville said.

“It’s in the way almost everywhere,” Ron replied.

Neville considered the table without any sign of urgency. His jumper had a little soil on one cuff, and he brushed it away with his thumb.

“I use one rather like this in the greenhouse office,” Neville said. “Only one side’s open.”

“For eating?” Ron asked.

“For putting things down while I’m working.”

Ron looked at the folded leaf, then at the window seat.

Hermione was already moving the napkins.

“What if we only opened one side?” Hermione said.

“Then it’s smaller,” Ron replied.

“Yes,” Hermione said, “but we might be able to sit around the open side without the chairs colliding.”

They tried it. McGonagall folded down the leaf nearest the hearth, and Neville helped Ron turn the table. The flat edge went towards the window seat, with the raised leaf facing into the room.

It looked possible.

Ron sat on the window seat.

The table was too far away.

They moved it closer.

Now he could reach, but he could not get out.

Hermione folded her arms and studied him.

“You could remain there until breakfast,” Hermione said.

“As long as someone brings it.”

Neville laughed, then glanced at the broad blue chair by the fire.

“Could we eat from our laps?”

McGonagall looked mildly doubtful.

“I had imagined plates.”

“Plates can go on laps,” Ron said. “It’s one of their lesser-known qualities.”

Hermione sat beside him on the window seat, testing the distance.

“Soup would be awkward,” Hermione said.

They all looked at McGonagall.

“There is no soup,” McGonagall said.

Ron felt they had made more progress in the last thirty seconds than in the previous hour.

Supper, it turned out, was to be a pie with mushrooms and leeks, roast potatoes, carrots, bread and a warm apple pudding.

“All very manageable,” Ron said.

“With gravy,” McGonagall added.

“Still manageable.”

Hermione raised an eyebrow.

“Mostly,” Ron conceded.

Luna arrived while they were trying to arrange the chairs in a loose circle.

She had come from Hogsmeade on foot, and a fine sprinkling of rain glittered on the shoulders of her coat. Her hair was fastened with a blue ribbon. She carried a paper bag, which she handed to Hermione.

“Pears,” Luna said. “The woman in the shop said they would be good tomorrow, but I tried one and it was already good.”

“Thank you,” Hermione said. “We can have them with breakfast.”

Luna took in the room, the open leaf and the scattering of chairs.

“Are you choosing places?” Luna asked.

“We’re trying to,” Ron replied. “It’s proving surprisingly technical.”

Luna removed her coat and hung it beside the door.

“May I sit in that one?” Luna asked, pointing to the green upholstered chair.

“Please do,” McGonagall said.

Luna settled into it. She put both feet on the floor and rested her hands on the arms.

“It’s comfortable,” Luna said. “Except that I can’t see Neville.”

Neville moved the high-backed chair a little.

“Now?” Neville asked.

“Now I can see your elbow,” Luna replied.

Ron stepped back and looked at their arrangement.

They had placed the largest backs towards the middle of the room. Anyone sitting in them would have to turn to speak to anyone else. The window seat was half hidden. The blue chair faced the door.

He had been thinking about how to fit chairs around a table. He had barely considered where anyone would be looking.

“Hang on,” Ron said.

He moved the high-backed chair to one side of the fireplace, angled towards the window. The green chair went opposite it, with enough space between them to see across. He put the plain dining chair nearer the window seat and drew the kitchen chair back towards the hearth.

Luna watched with interest.

“That’s nicer,” Luna said.

Hermione sat on the window seat again. This time there was nothing in front of her knees.

“Yes,” Hermione said. “It is.”

The table stood slightly apart from everyone, one leaf raised, looking useful at last.

Ron folded his arms.

“What if we stop trying to sit at it?”

McGonagall considered the suggestion.

“A serving table?”

“Put the food there,” Ron said. “Pass the plates round. Everyone sits where they’re comfortable.”

Neville glanced at the basket on the windowsill.

“And there’ll be room for one of these.”

“There will,” Hermione agreed.

They tried the seats properly before deciding.

McGonagall preferred the high-backed chair, which supported her shoulders and allowed her to reach the small side table by the hearth. Luna kept the green chair. Neville took the kitchen chair after discovering that the plain dining chair was slightly too low for him. Hermione chose the window seat, leaving room for Ron beside her.

The remaining dining chair could hold a tray of used dishes, and the blue armchair waited near the fire for anyone who wished to move later.

“It’s a great deal of furniture for five people,” Neville said.

“We’ve used almost all of it,” Ron replied proudly.

There was still the matter of the serving table’s position. Too near the window seat, it blocked Hermione’s way. Too near the hearth, it would require everyone to reach across the hot dishes.

Ron and Neville carried it to the wall between the bookcase and the window. With the leaf open, it offered ample room for supper; with the other folded, it left a clear path to every chair.

Hermione opened the little drawer. It slid out without meeting anyone’s knees.

“Napkins,” Hermione said, putting them inside.

McGonagall placed the teapot at one end.

Luna chose a pot of thyme and set it at the other.

Ron took the travelling rug from the sofa and folded it over the back of the blue chair.

The room was ready.

They had forty minutes until supper.

For the first time that afternoon, no one appeared inclined to improve anything.

Neville lit the fire with a quiet movement of his wand. The kindling caught, then the logs, and a light warmth began to reach across the carpet. Hermione drew the curtains when the window became more reflective than transparent, leaving a narrow gap through which the first lights on the grounds could be seen.

Ron sat beside her with his empty hands resting on his knees.

He had expected to feel hungry by now. He did feel hungry, but beneath that was the pleasant satisfaction of having finished something small enough to see all at once.

The table stood level. The chairs faced one another. Nobody would have to apologise whenever they wanted to get up.

Hermione touched his sleeve.

“You enjoyed that,” Hermione said quietly.

Ron looked towards the table.

“More than I expected.”

“I noticed.”

“It’s nice when something can be sorted out before dinner.”

Hermione smiled.

“Yes,” Hermione said. “It is.”

McGonagall was asking Neville about a classroom windowsill that had become crowded with seedlings. Neville explained that the students were supposed to take their plants away on Fridays, but several had begun leaving notes requesting that he keep an eye on them over the weekend.

“One note included a watering schedule,” Neville said.

“Was it correct?” McGonagall asked.

“Very nearly.”

“Then I should be inclined to reward the planning.”

Luna leaned forward in her green chair.

“Perhaps the plants enjoy staying together,” Luna said.

Neville considered this with the seriousness he gave most questions about plants.

“They do seem to be doing well,” Neville replied.

Supper arrived beneath silver covers, accompanied by a stack of warmed plates. McGonagall arranged the dishes while Ron fetched the napkins from the drawer.

He opened it, stopped, and then laughed.

Hermione turned towards him.

“What is it?”

“There’s plenty of room for the napkins,” Ron said. “We spent ages trying to put someone’s legs here.”

Hermione accepted hers with a small smile.

“We needed somewhere to begin.”

Ron passed a plate to Luna, then to McGonagall. Neville took charge of the potatoes. Hermione held the bread basket while everyone helped themselves.

There was one brief difficulty with the gravy boat, whose handle faced the wall. Ron turned it round.

After that, everything went quite easily.

The pie had a crisp, golden top and a filling fragrant with thyme. Steam rose when McGonagall cut into it. The potatoes broke softly beneath their browned edges, and the bread was still warm enough to soften butter.

Ron balanced his plate on a folded napkin and took a careful mouthful.

“Well?” Hermione asked.

Ron swallowed.

“I think the furniture was worth it.”

From the high-backed chair, McGonagall gave him a dry look.

“I shall pass your assessment to the kitchens.”

Conversation moved in the easy, uneven way it did when nobody needed to catch a train. Neville described a student who had tried to keep a weekly height record for a particularly fast-growing vine and had run out of wall before Wednesday. Hermione told them about a woman at work whose filing charm had begun sorting everything according to how urgently she wished to avoid it.

“Sounds useful,” Ron said.

“It put every document in the same cupboard,” Hermione replied.

“Still sounds useful.”

Luna had been travelling along the coast and spoke about a village where the harbour lights were covered each autumn to keep migrating birds from circling them.

“You could still see the boats,” Luna said. “Only the light stayed low, close to the water.”

Neville asked about the birds. McGonagall asked whether the harbour had a lighthouse. Ron listened while he finished his pie, the fire warm against one side of his face.

No one had to raise their voice. From the window seat, he could see everyone.

When Hermione wanted more carrots, Ron could reach the dish without standing in anybody’s way. When Neville went to fetch water, his chair moved freely. Luna rested her empty plate on the stool, which had been placed conveniently beside her.

The table bore its dishes without a tremor.

Later, as they passed round the apple pudding, McGonagall glanced towards it.

“I believe we may leave the room arranged this way,” McGonagall said.

“For the next gathering?” Hermione asked.

“For anyone who wants to use it.”

Ron looked at the chairs, each occupied according to comfort rather than appearance.

“Someone will probably move them all round tomorrow,” Ron said.

“Almost certainly,” McGonagall replied.

The thought did not trouble him. Whoever came tomorrow might want to write letters, or play cards, or sit alone by the window. They could move a chair.

For this evening, everything was where it needed to be.

After supper, Neville helped clear the plates while Hermione wrapped the remaining bread. Luna moved to the blue chair and drew the travelling rug over her knees. McGonagall poured tea from the brown hen teapot, which kept the second cup as warm as the first.

Ron put one of the pears beside the bread for breakfast.

By nine o’clock, the conversation had slowed. There were longer pauses in which the logs settled and someone turned a cup in its saucer. Neville yawned, apologised, and admitted that he had been in the greenhouses before breakfast.

Luna folded the rug carefully before they left together.

“I’m glad we didn’t all sit at the table,” Luna said at the door. “I should have spent the evening looking at the back of that chair.”

“So should Neville,” Ron replied. “And he was sitting in it.”

Neville smiled and wished them goodnight.

McGonagall remained long enough to check the fire.

“Thank you for your assistance,” McGonagall said.

Ron glanced at Hermione.

“We found the foot in a stool,” Ron said. “I’m not sure it counts as expert assistance.”

“It counts as a comfortable evening,” McGonagall replied.

Their guest room was a short walk down the corridor. Hermione carried her book, which she had not opened. Ron carried the paper bag of pears.

The bed had been turned down, and a shaded lamp stood on either side. At the foot lay a thick quilt. Rain touched the window in small, intermittent taps.

Ron placed the pears on the chest of drawers.

“Breakfast is organised,” Ron said.

Hermione sat on the edge of the bed to take off her shoes.

“Very efficient.”

Ron drew the curtains while Hermione washed. When she returned, he went in, leaving his slippers neatly beside the bed without quite intending to.

By the time he came back, Hermione was beneath the quilt, her book open against her knees.

Ron climbed in beside her. The sheets were cool for a moment, then comfortable. He stretched his legs and felt the day loosen through his shoulders.

“Enough room?” Hermione asked.

Ron turned his head on the pillow.

“For my knees? Luxury.”

Hermione laughed softly and returned to her page.

He listened to the rain. Somewhere beyond the room, footsteps crossed the corridor and faded. The castle was settling into its night sounds, familiar enough that he did not need to attend to them.

Ron thought briefly of the little wooden foot turning into its socket, of the drawer opening, of five plates passed from hand to hand. He had meant to tell Hermione that they ought to have Neville and Luna to supper at home before Christmas.

He could tell her in the morning.

Hermione read another page, then looked down. Ron’s eyes were closed, one hand resting loosely on the quilt between them.

She lowered the lamp beside him.

Ron felt the light diminish through his eyelids. He shifted a little closer when Hermione lay down, and her hand found his.

Warm beneath the quilt, with the quiet rain at the window, Ron fell asleep.


The Last Page of the Day

Harry had meant to leave Hogwarts after tea. By half past four, however, the rain had stopped, Neville had brought out a second plate of biscuits, and the sheet of parchment between them still contained only three lines.

At the top Harry had written, in his neatest handwriting, A Short Walk Through the Grounds.

Beneath that he had written, Begin at the oak front doors.

The third line had been crossed out twice.

“It really shouldn’t be difficult,” Harry said.

Neville, who was sitting opposite him in the little office beside Greenhouse Three, looked at the parchment with the patience he usually reserved for seedlings.

“It isn’t difficult to walk it,” Neville said. “Writing it down turns out to be another matter.”

Outside the open window, water slipped from the greenhouse gutters into a row of deep stone jars. The late September afternoon smelled of damp earth and the last warmth of summer. On Neville’s desk, a small ceramic pot was producing one blue flower at a time. Each flower opened, considered the room briefly, and folded itself away.

Harry had spent the afternoon helping Neville move young plants into larger pots. It had been pleasant work, requiring enough attention to keep him occupied and very little conversation unless either of them felt like it. Afterwards, Neville had mentioned the visitors’ guide.

There were often people at Hogwarts who had an hour to fill: visiting lecturers, parents waiting for appointments, former pupils who had come back to see a friend. They asked whether there was somewhere nearby they might take a short walk.

Neville usually explained the same route.

“Along the terrace, round the little lawn, down to the beech tree, and back by the greenhouses,” Neville said. “But people know different parts of the grounds. What seems obvious to me isn’t always obvious to them.”

“And you thought I could write it?”

“I thought you could tell me which bits don’t make sense,” Neville replied. “You’ve been away long enough to notice things.”

Harry looked at his crossed-out sentence. He had discovered, with some embarrassment, that he could picture the entire walk and could not reliably describe its first turn.



Neville poured the last of the tea. The office was comfortable in a way that had nothing to do with matching furniture. One chair had a green cushion; the other had been repaired with a length of stout garden twine. There were clean mugs on a shelf above books with soil-darkened corners, and a cardigan hung from a peg beside three pairs of gloves.

Harry had taken off his glasses to rub his eyes when an owl tapped at the window.

Neville let it in and untied a small envelope from its leg. After reading the note, he smiled.

“Luna’s staying tonight as well,” Neville said. “She’s finished in the library.”

Harry put his glasses back on.

“I didn’t know she was here.”

“She came to look at some old drawings of Hebridean moths. There’s a collection upstairs that hasn’t been properly sorted.”

Neville wrote a reply, explaining where they were, and offered the owl a piece of biscuit before sending it out.

“McGonagall said you could have a room, by the way,” Neville added. “If you’d rather go tomorrow.”

Harry glanced out at the grounds. The wet paths were beginning to brighten as the clouds thinned. He had no work until the following afternoon, and no reason to hurry home to an empty house; Ginny was away with the children until tomorrow morning. He had left himself a sensible margin for the visit and had been preparing to spend it travelling.

“All right,” Harry said. “Thanks. I’ll stay.”

Neville nodded towards the parchment.

“Then perhaps we can finish that before dinner.”

Harry drew a fresh sheet towards him. Having decided to stay, he found he was no longer inclined to make the walk sound shorter than it was.



Luna arrived carrying a narrow folder under one arm. Her hair was tied back loosely with a blue ribbon, and there was a pale smear of library dust on the sleeve of her coat.

“Hello, Harry,” Luna said. “Neville says you’re making a walk.”

“Trying to put one on paper,” Harry replied.

Luna laid her folder on the windowsill and read the three lines. She did not appear troubled by the crossings-out.

“Have you walked it yet?” Luna asked.

“We’ve both walked it hundreds of times,” Harry said.

“Recently?”

Harry considered this.

“Not while trying to follow those.”

Luna pulled up a low stool. Neville moved the biscuit plate within her reach.

“What do you want someone to feel when they’ve finished?” Luna asked.

Harry had been thinking about paths and turns. The question made him look again at the title.

“That they’ve had a pleasant half-hour,” Harry said. “And that they knew where they were going.”

“Without having to ask whether they’ve gone wrong,” Neville added.

“Then we should try it,” Luna said. “I can read the directions. You can see what I do.”

Neville looked at the clock. He still had several plants to check before evening, but there was time for all three of them to walk the route once.

Harry tucked the unfinished pages into a firm-backed notebook. They agreed to begin at the front doors, exactly where a visitor would begin, rather than making a convenient start outside Neville’s office.

On the way up to the castle, Harry noticed that he was already choosing words. He tried a sentence about the greenhouses in his head, then let it go. They had not reached the front doors yet. There was no need to write the whole walk before taking its first step.



The oak doors stood open to the afternoon. Beyond them, the entrance hall held its familiar mingling of stone, wax polish and distant cooking. A few pupils were passing through on their way back from the grounds. Their conversation rose towards the ceiling and faded along a corridor.

Harry stopped at the top of the broad steps.

“Begin at the oak front doors,” Luna read from his page.

“So far, so good,” Neville said.

“Go left,” Harry added.

Luna turned to face the doors.

“From here?” Luna asked.

Harry laughed quietly.

“No. Facing out.”

He opened the notebook and wrote that down.

They descended the steps. At the bottom, the broad gravel approach met the narrower terrace path, which ran beside the castle wall. Harry had intended that visitors should turn there, where the walking was easy and the stonework held the warmth of the afternoon.

“Keep the castle on your left,” Neville suggested.

Harry wrote it beneath the first sentence. That was better than another left turn. It gave the walker something steady to follow.

Luna looked along the terrace.

“Will they know this is the path you mean?” Luna asked.

Harry followed her gaze. From where they stood, the gravel approach seemed the more natural way to go. The terrace was quieter, partly sheltered by a low wall.

“The narrower path beside the castle,” Harry said.

Luna took three unhurried steps along it, then looked back.

“Yes,” Luna said. “That gets me here.”

Harry read the new beginning aloud. It sounded almost excessively plain, until he imagined standing there for the first time, holding the paper with an unfamiliar castle behind him.

He left every word in.



The terrace curved gently with the building. Rainwater shone in the seams between its paving stones, and the low wall beside it was patched with small, flat cushions of moss. Beyond the wall, the lawn sloped towards the lake.

Neville paused to brush a fallen twig from the path with the toe of his shoe.

“This is where I usually tell people to go towards the lake,” Neville said.

Harry could see the difficulty at once. Almost everything in front of them was, in some sense, towards the lake.

“And then they choose their own route down,” Harry said.

“Sometimes.”

They continued until the terrace widened beside a small lawn. In its centre stood a round bed planted with white and pale yellow flowers. The bed was still full, though some of the taller stems had begun to lean.

“Turn by the flower bed,” Harry said, testing the words aloud.

Neville shook his head.

“It isn’t always flowers. In winter it’s mostly stems, and I don’t know what we’ll put there next spring.”

“The bed itself is round,” Luna observed.

Harry looked at the low stone rim. It would be recognisable in any season.

“At the round stone-edged bed, take the path that slopes gently down,” Harry said.

Luna followed it without hesitation.

Neville smiled.

“That’s what I should have been saying.”

Harry made a small mark in the margin to remind himself that the slope mattered. A visitor might be tired, or wearing shoes chosen for an office rather than a walk. He had imagined a guide that simply showed the way. It was beginning to seem useful to say what the way would ask of someone.



Below the little lawn, the path narrowed and became fine gravel. Harry felt the change through the soles of his shoes.

The air was cooler here. The castle sheltered them from the breeze, but the grass still held the rain, and every so often a drop fell from the leaves above with a soft, separate tap.

Luna walked a few paces ahead with the notebook.

“Continue to the beech tree,” Luna read.

There were five trees in view.

Harry knew the one he meant: broad, copper-leaved, with a generous crown and a bench beneath it. He had never needed to identify it to anyone.

“The large beech tree,” Harry amended.

“They might not know a beech,” Neville said.

Harry looked at him.

“You do realise this is going to become a book.”

Neville grinned.

“Only if we put in all five trees.”

Luna stopped where the path divided around a long patch of grass.

“There’s a bench,” Luna said. “That’s easier to recognise.”

Harry joined her. From there, the bench was plainly visible, its back a pale line beneath the leaves.

“Follow the gravel path down to the wooden bench beneath the broad tree,” Harry said.

“That will do,” Neville replied.

They took the right-hand curve round the grass and reached the bench. Its seat was damp at one end, where water had blown beneath the branches. Neville dried the whole length with a small movement of his wand.

Harry added another note. The bench would make a useful stopping place, particularly for someone who wanted a few minutes outside rather than a full circuit.

He had called the guide a short walk. He began to think it ought to offer a shorter one as well.



They sat with the notebook open across Harry’s knees.

From the bench, the lake appeared between two sloping shoulders of lawn. The water was grey near the shore and silver farther out, where the clouds were opening. A few yellow leaves lay on the gravel at their feet, each holding a small dark patch of rain.

Neville stretched his legs.

“People can turn back here,” Neville said. “It’s a good view.”

Harry nodded and wrote, If you would like a shorter walk, return by the same path.

He read the sentence again, considering whether it needed anything else.

“Tell them the castle stays in sight,” Luna suggested.

“From here?”

“Nearly all the way. That’s reassuring if you’re somewhere new.”

Harry looked back. The castle rose clearly beyond the lawn, its windows catching the late light. He had spent so much of his life recognising Hogwarts from every angle that he had forgotten how much there was to take in at a first visit.

He wrote, The castle remains in view along this part of the route.

Neville leaned over to read.

“Would you put how long it takes?”

“Twenty minutes?” Harry said.

“If you keep walking,” Neville replied.

Luna was watching a leaf turn slowly on the surface of a shallow puddle.

“Perhaps half an hour, with time to sit,” Luna said.

Harry crossed out twenty and wrote about half an hour.

That seemed a better promise. There would be no reason to hurry past the best place to rest merely to complete the walk within the time printed at the top.

For a little while, none of them spoke. Harry rested the quill across the page and let his shoulders settle against the bench.



The next part of the path ran almost level across the slope. It did not go close to the lake, but gave a wider view of it before turning gently towards the greenhouses.

Harry had described it as the lower path. Reading that now, he could see a faint track farther down the grass which might equally answer the description.

Luna glanced from one to the other.

“This gravel path?” Luna asked.

“Yes,” Harry said. “We stay on this one.”

“Then say that.”

Harry wrote, Stay on the gravel path as it curves to the right. There is no need to leave it to see the lake.

Neville bent to examine the edge of the gravel, where rain had washed a little earth across it.

“This bit can be soft after wet weather,” Neville said. “I’ll ask for some fresh gravel.”

Harry tested the ground with his foot. It was firm enough, though the outer edge was muddy.

“For now, we can mention sturdy shoes,” Harry said.

“Near the beginning,” Luna replied. “It’s rather late to learn that here.”

They stopped while Harry marked the top of the page. He was accumulating useful sentences in quite the wrong order.

On the far side of the lake, the light spread beneath the clouds. Harry watched it broaden across the water while he waited for the ink to dry. The view did not need an instruction. Anyone following the path would find it.

He left a space on the page and went on walking.



The greenhouses came gradually into view beyond a hedge. Their glass roofs reflected the sky in uneven squares, some bright, some pearly with moisture. A door stood open, and the warm smell of plants drifted out across the path.

Neville checked his watch.

“I’d better see to the last pots,” Neville said. “Will you two manage the return?”

“Unless there are another five trees to choose from,” Harry replied.

“Only the doors,” Neville said. “But I’ll explain those before I go.”

They stood where the path divided. One branch led directly between the greenhouses. The other curved uphill beside them, returning to the terrace.

Harry’s original notes said, Return past the greenhouses.

Luna read it aloud and looked down both branches.

“I could do either,” Luna said.

Neville pointed to the uphill path.

“That one. If you go between the glasshouses, you end up by the working doors. There are usually wheelbarrows.”

Harry wrote, At the greenhouses, take the uphill path, keeping the glass buildings on your right.

Neville considered the sentence.

“Yes. That’s clear.”

He promised to meet them at dinner, then went towards his office, pausing on the way to move an empty pot from a doorstep.

Harry watched his friend disappear into the warm green light. Neville had sounded mildly apologetic about the guide when he first brought it out, as though he ought to have completed it months ago. Yet the route depended on knowledge he used without thinking: which path stayed firm, where the bench could be seen, which doors belonged to visitors and which to people carrying trays.

Harry was glad they had come outside.



The return slope was gentle but longer than Harry remembered. He and Luna walked without speaking until they reached a small landing beside the greenhouse wall.

Someone had set two large terracotta pots there, planted with rosemary. The rain had sharpened its scent.

“This is a good place to pause,” Luna said.

Harry looked back down the path. From here, the greenhouses stood between them and most of the grounds, making the route feel briefly separate from the castle.

“Do we need to say that?” Harry asked.

Luna considered the question.

“Perhaps only that the path goes uphill. People will see the place to stop.”

Harry nodded. The guide could become so attentive that it interrupted the walk every few paces.

He crossed out a sentence he had begun about resting beside the pots. Then he added a simpler note near the beginning: The return includes a gentle uphill stretch.

Luna leaned against the low wall while he wrote.

“I like directions that tell you just enough,” Luna said. “Then you can look about.”

“That’s the difficult part,” Harry replied. “Knowing what enough is.”

“You’ll know when you don’t need to keep looking at the paper.”

They resumed walking. Harry tucked the notebook under his arm and tried to notice where he would want to check it.

At the top of the slope, the terrace appeared again, with the castle wall beyond. There was only one sensible turning. He would need a final sentence to confirm it, but no more.

For the first time, the whole guide seemed possible on one page.



Back at the front steps, Luna handed him the notebook.

“You’ve got us home,” Luna said.

Harry looked at the scattered additions, arrows and crossings-out.

“On paper, we may still be by the round flower bed.”

They stood aside as two pupils came down the steps carrying a chessboard between them. One piece was complaining about being tilted. The pupils settled on the broad wall beside the entrance, absorbed in arranging their game.

Harry turned to a clean page and began putting the directions in order.

Luna waited on the bottom step, her folder beside her. The air had grown cooler, but there was still daylight enough for another circuit.

Harry read the new version aloud. It began with the length and the ground underfoot, then moved from the oak doors to the terrace, the stone-edged bed, the bench and the curve towards the greenhouses.

When he finished, Luna nodded.

“I think I could follow that,” Luna said.

“You’ve just walked it.”

“That does give me an advantage.”

Harry smiled at the page. He could either spend another hour wondering whether it worked or follow it once more.

“Will you read it again?” Harry asked. “This time, we’ll do exactly what it says.”

Luna picked up her folder.

“Yes,” Luna said. “Though I might sit on the bench for a little longer.”

Harry added a small line beneath the title: Allow about half an hour, or longer if you wish to pause.

Then he handed her the notebook, and they began again.



The second walk felt different almost at once.

Harry had expected to spend it correcting things. Instead, the first instructions took them neatly from the steps to the terrace, leaving him free to notice the evening arriving across the grounds.

A strip of clear sky had opened in the west. The stone beside the path was drying in patches. Somewhere behind a window above them, someone was practising a tune on a flute, taking the same few bars slowly until they joined.

At the round bed, Luna read the next sentence and turned down the sloping gravel path.

“No hesitation,” Harry said.

“It’s a very definite round bed,” Luna replied.

Halfway to the bench, they met Professor McGonagall coming uphill with a folded tartan shawl over one arm. She stopped when she saw them.

“Potter. Miss Lovegood,” McGonagall said. “I understand you are both joining us for dinner.”

“Yes, Professor,” Harry replied.

“And Professor Longbottom tells me you have taken charge of his walking directions.”

“Only the handwriting,” Harry said. “Most of it’s his.”

McGonagall glanced at the notebook Luna held.

“A useful undertaking,” McGonagall said. “I have directed three visitors to that bench this month. One returned having inspected the outside of every greenhouse.”

“We’ve tried to prevent that,” Harry replied.

“Then I shall be pleased to see the finished page,” McGonagall said.

She continued up the path at a brisk, measured pace. Harry watched her turn correctly at the round bed without consulting anything.

Luna looked down at their instructions.

“I expect she’d be rather difficult to use as a test,” Luna said.

Harry laughed, and they went on.



At the bench, Luna closed the notebook.

The sky had softened to pale gold above the hills. There was very little wind. Across the lake, the reflected clouds seemed to rest just beneath the surface, disturbed now and then by widening circles.

Harry sat at the dry end of the bench. Luna settled beside him and placed her folder on her lap.

“What did you find in the library?” Harry asked.

“Several very good drawings,” Luna replied. “And some notes about the plants the moths prefer. The drawings showed them with their wings open, but the notes were mostly about where they sat.”

“Was that useful?”

“Very. You spend a great deal of time looking at closed wings.”

Harry smiled. He could imagine her waiting quite happily beside a patch of heather while something small declined to display itself.

Luna opened the folder and showed him a copied drawing. The moth had softly marked wings in brown and cream, with a fine fringe along their edges. In one corner, Luna had drawn the leaf on which it rested.

Harry looked from the drawing to the grounds.

“I think I was trying to describe everything,” Harry said.

“In the guide?”

“Yes. As though someone might miss the walk if I left something out.”

Luna slid the drawing back into its folder.

“They’ll have their own walk,” Luna said. “They might notice things we haven’t.”

Harry sat with that for a moment. A bird moved through the beech above them, shaking a few drops onto the gravel. He looked up but could see only leaves.

When they rose, he did not add the bird, the colour of the sky or the sound of the water to his page.



They reached the turn by the greenhouses without needing to consult the notebook again.

Luna read the uphill instruction before they took it. Harry checked the wording against the scene: glass buildings on the right, the slope ahead, the terrace at the top.

It was clear.

Beside the rosemary pots, they paused long enough for Harry to look back. The bench was hidden now, but he could trace the course of the path through the grounds. It seemed modest from here, a small loop taking in a piece of lawn, a tree, some water and a row of working greenhouses.

That was enough for the purpose Neville had described.

At the terrace, Luna read the last direction.

“Turn left and follow the castle wall back to the front steps.”

They did. The oak doors stood open ahead, and warm light was beginning to gather in the entrance hall.

Harry took back the notebook.

“I think that’s it,” Harry said.

Luna looked at the page once more.

“There’s one thing,” Luna said.

Harry waited, quill ready.

“You’ve put ‘walk steadily’ near the beginning. Do they need to?”

He read the sentence. He had written it while calculating the time.

“No,” Harry said.

He crossed out the two words.

The final page would have no crossings-out, but he thought he might keep the working sheets. They had become a record of an afternoon spent discovering how much could be removed once the useful parts were right.



Dinner was already being laid when they entered the Great Hall.

The enchanted ceiling held the clear evening sky, with a few high clouds still lit along their edges. Harry and Luna joined Neville near the end of the staff table. Neville had washed and changed his jumper, though a small green leaf remained caught against his cuff.

“How did it go?” Neville asked.

“We returned to the intended doors,” Luna replied.

“Both times,” Harry added.

Neville looked pleased.

Over a supper of vegetable pie, potatoes and broad beans, Harry told him what they had changed. Neville listened closely to the directions about the bench and the greenhouse turn.

“I ought to have thought of that,” Neville said.

“You knew where you meant,” Harry replied. “That was the trouble.”

Neville pulled the leaf from his cuff and set it beside his plate.

“I suppose I do that in lessons as well.”

“Probably less often,” Harry said. “The plants let you know.”

Neville smiled.

“Usually quite firmly.”

They spoke of easier things after that. Luna described the library drawings. Neville explained that the little blue-flowered plant in his office opened each blossom for only a few seconds and was consequently a poor choice for anyone who wanted to sketch it. Harry asked whether it ever exhausted itself.

“Only if you overwater it,” Neville said. “Then it sulks for a week.”

By the time pudding arrived, the tall windows had darkened. Harry felt the comfortable tiredness of an afternoon outdoors. There was still the fair copy to make, but the work of deciding was finished.



They returned to Neville’s office after dinner.

The greenhouse jars had stopped dripping. Inside, Neville lit a lamp and cleared a space on the desk. He found a sheet of good parchment, a ruler and a bottle of dark blue ink.

Harry washed his hands again to make certain there was no trace of soil on his fingers. Then he sat down.

The room was quiet while he wrote. Luna settled by the window with her folder, checking the labels on her copied drawings. Neville made notes for the following morning, occasionally looking towards a plant on the shelf as though remembering something it would need.

Harry gave the title a line to itself. Under it he wrote the time, the advice about shoes and the gentle slope on the return. He left comfortable spaces between the directions. The page looked welcoming before it was half finished.

When he reached the bench, he included the shorter option. Then came the level path above the lake, the turn at the greenhouses, and the return beside the castle.

He read each sentence before moving on.

At the bottom there was room for a final note. Harry considered it for a while, then wrote that the route was intended for daylight hours.

He put down the quill.

Neville came round the desk. Luna joined them, and the three of them read the page in silence.

“It sounds like the walk,” Neville said at last.

Harry looked over it once more. The route began somewhere definite, gave the walker choices where they were useful, and brought them back.

“Yes,” Harry said. “I think it does.”



Neville waited for the ink to dry before making several copies with a careful charm. Harry watched the words appear, line by line, on the fresh sheets.

One copy would go in the entrance hall for visitors who asked. Another could be left in the guest rooms. Neville kept the original on his desk until he could give it to McGonagall in the morning.

“There,” Neville said. “That’s been on my list since June.”

Harry leaned back in his chair.

“Mine since half past four.”

“It was a very busy half past four,” Luna observed.

They tidied the desk together. Neville put away the ink, Luna gathered her drawings, and Harry stacked the working pages with their arrows and amendments. He folded them once and slipped them into his bag.

Outside, the greenhouse glass reflected their lamplight. Beyond it, the grounds had become shapes: the low line of the hedge, the darker slope of the lawn, the castle windows above.

They left the office and walked back uphill. Neville locked the door behind them. At the top, Harry could just make out where their route joined the terrace.

Tomorrow someone might follow it with the page in one hand. They would find their own pace, stop where they pleased, and return in time for whatever had brought them to Hogwarts.

Harry had no urge to go over the wording again.



His guest room was in a quiet corridor on the second floor.

Neville showed him the door and wished him goodnight. Luna’s room was farther along, near the turn in the passage.

“Goodnight, Harry,” Luna said. “I’m glad you stayed.”

“So am I,” Harry replied.

Inside, a small fire was burning behind a brass guard. There was a deep chair, a washstand and a bed with a folded woollen blanket across its foot. Someone had set a jug of water and a clean glass beside the lamp.

Harry put his bag on the chair. The room looked out over part of the grounds, though the window reflected more of the fire than the night beyond it.

He washed, changed and laid his clothes neatly over the chair back. In the pocket of his robes he found a small piece of gravel. He set it on the windowsill to take outside in the morning.

Then he drew the curtains.

The bed was cool at first. Harry pulled the blanket over the quilt and settled against the pillows with a quiet sigh. There was nothing left to finish downstairs. Neville had his guide; Luna had her drawings; the grounds would keep until morning.

He took off his glasses and placed them beside the water glass.

For a little while he watched the firelight move across the ceiling. He thought of the bench beneath the beech, then of Neville bending over the finished page, pleased to have a small task done well.

Harry reached for the lamp and extinguished it.

The fire settled softly. His breathing grew slower, and before its light had faded from the ceiling, he was asleep.
