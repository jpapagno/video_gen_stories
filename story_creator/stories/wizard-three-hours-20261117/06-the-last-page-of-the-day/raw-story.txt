The Last Page of the Day

Harry had meant to leave Hogwarts after tea. By half past four, however, the rain had stopped, Neville had brought out a second plate of biscuits, and the sheet of parchment between them still contained only three lines.

At the top Harry had written, in his neatest handwriting, A Short Walk Through the Grounds.

Beneath that he had written, Begin at the oak front doors.

The third line had been crossed out twice.

“It really shouldn’t be difficult,” Harry said.

Neville, who was sitting opposite him in the little office beside Greenhouse Three, looked at the parchment with the patience he usually reserved for seedlings.

“It isn’t difficult to walk it,” Neville said. “Writing it down turns out to be another matter.”

Outside the open window, water slipped from the greenhouse gutters into a row of deep stone jars. The late September afternoon smelled of damp earth and the last warmth of summer. On Neville’s desk, a small ceramic pot was producing one blue flower at a time. Each flower opened, considered the room briefly, and folded itself away.

Harry had spent the afternoon helping Neville move young plants into larger pots. It had been pleasant work, requiring enough attention to keep him occupied and very little conversation unless either of them felt like it. Afterwards, Neville had mentioned the visitors’ guide.

There were often people at Hogwarts who had an hour to fill: visiting lecturers, parents waiting for appointments, former pupils who had come back to see a friend. They asked whether there was somewhere nearby they might take a short walk.

Neville usually explained the same route.

“Along the terrace, round the little lawn, down to the beech tree, and back by the greenhouses,” Neville said. “But people know different parts of the grounds. What seems obvious to me isn’t always obvious to them.”

“And you thought I could write it?”

“I thought you could tell me which bits don’t make sense,” Neville replied. “You’ve been away long enough to notice things.”

Harry looked at his crossed-out sentence. He had discovered, with some embarrassment, that he could picture the entire walk and could not reliably describe its first turn.

***

Neville poured the last of the tea. The office was comfortable in a way that had nothing to do with matching furniture. One chair had a green cushion; the other had been repaired with a length of stout garden twine. There were clean mugs on a shelf above books with soil-darkened corners, and a cardigan hung from a peg beside three pairs of gloves.

Harry had taken off his glasses to rub his eyes when an owl tapped at the window.

Neville let it in and untied a small envelope from its leg. After reading the note, he smiled.

“Luna’s staying tonight as well,” Neville said. “She’s finished in the library.”

Harry put his glasses back on.

“I didn’t know she was here.”

“She came to look at some old drawings of Hebridean moths. There’s a collection upstairs that hasn’t been properly sorted.”

Neville wrote a reply, explaining where they were, and offered the owl a piece of biscuit before sending it out.

“McGonagall said you could have a room, by the way,” Neville added. “If you’d rather go tomorrow.”

Harry glanced out at the grounds. The wet paths were beginning to brighten as the clouds thinned. He had no work until the following afternoon, and no reason to hurry home to an empty house; Ginny was away with the children until tomorrow morning. He had left himself a sensible margin for the visit and had been preparing to spend it travelling.

“All right,” Harry said. “Thanks. I’ll stay.”

Neville nodded towards the parchment.

“Then perhaps we can finish that before dinner.”

Harry drew a fresh sheet towards him. Having decided to stay, he found he was no longer inclined to make the walk sound shorter than it was.

***

Luna arrived carrying a narrow folder under one arm. Her hair was tied back loosely with a blue ribbon, and there was a pale smear of library dust on the sleeve of her coat.

“Hello, Harry,” Luna said. “Neville says you’re making a walk.”

“Trying to put one on paper,” Harry replied.

Luna laid her folder on the windowsill and read the three lines. She did not appear troubled by the crossings-out.

“Have you walked it yet?” Luna asked.

“We’ve both walked it hundreds of times,” Harry said.

“Recently?”

Harry considered this.

“Not while trying to follow those.”

Luna pulled up a low stool. Neville moved the biscuit plate within her reach.

“What do you want someone to feel when they’ve finished?” Luna asked.

Harry had been thinking about paths and turns. The question made him look again at the title.

“That they’ve had a pleasant half-hour,” Harry said. “And that they knew where they were going.”

“Without having to ask whether they’ve gone wrong,” Neville added.

“Then we should try it,” Luna said. “I can read the directions. You can see what I do.”

Neville looked at the clock. He still had several plants to check before evening, but there was time for all three of them to walk the route once.

Harry tucked the unfinished pages into a firm-backed notebook. They agreed to begin at the front doors, exactly where a visitor would begin, rather than making a convenient start outside Neville’s office.

On the way up to the castle, Harry noticed that he was already choosing words. He tried a sentence about the greenhouses in his head, then let it go. They had not reached the front doors yet. There was no need to write the whole walk before taking its first step.

***

The oak doors stood open to the afternoon. Beyond them, the entrance hall held its familiar mingling of stone, wax polish and distant cooking. A few pupils were passing through on their way back from the grounds. Their conversation rose towards the ceiling and faded along a corridor.

Harry stopped at the top of the broad steps.

“Begin at the oak front doors,” Luna read from his page.

“So far, so good,” Neville said.

“Go left,” Harry added.

Luna turned to face the doors.

“From here?” Luna asked.

Harry laughed quietly.

“No. Facing out.”

He opened the notebook and wrote that down.

They descended the steps. At the bottom, the broad gravel approach met the narrower terrace path, which ran beside the castle wall. Harry had intended that visitors should turn there, where the walking was easy and the stonework held the warmth of the afternoon.

“Keep the castle on your left,” Neville suggested.

Harry wrote it beneath the first sentence. That was better than another left turn. It gave the walker something steady to follow.

Luna looked along the terrace.

“Will they know this is the path you mean?” Luna asked.

Harry followed her gaze. From where they stood, the gravel approach seemed the more natural way to go. The terrace was quieter, partly sheltered by a low wall.

“The narrower path beside the castle,” Harry said.

Luna took three unhurried steps along it, then looked back.

“Yes,” Luna said. “That gets me here.”

Harry read the new beginning aloud. It sounded almost excessively plain, until he imagined standing there for the first time, holding the paper with an unfamiliar castle behind him.

He left every word in.

***

The terrace curved gently with the building. Rainwater shone in the seams between its paving stones, and the low wall beside it was patched with small, flat cushions of moss. Beyond the wall, the lawn sloped towards the lake.

Neville paused to brush a fallen twig from the path with the toe of his shoe.

“This is where I usually tell people to go towards the lake,” Neville said.

Harry could see the difficulty at once. Almost everything in front of them was, in some sense, towards the lake.

“And then they choose their own route down,” Harry said.

“Sometimes.”

They continued until the terrace widened beside a small lawn. In its centre stood a round bed planted with white and pale yellow flowers. The bed was still full, though some of the taller stems had begun to lean.

“Turn by the flower bed,” Harry said, testing the words aloud.

Neville shook his head.

“It isn’t always flowers. In winter it’s mostly stems, and I don’t know what we’ll put there next spring.”

“The bed itself is round,” Luna observed.

Harry looked at the low stone rim. It would be recognisable in any season.

“At the round stone-edged bed, take the path that slopes gently down,” Harry said.

Luna followed it without hesitation.

Neville smiled.

“That’s what I should have been saying.”

Harry made a small mark in the margin to remind himself that the slope mattered. A visitor might be tired, or wearing shoes chosen for an office rather than a walk. He had imagined a guide that simply showed the way. It was beginning to seem useful to say what the way would ask of someone.

***

Below the little lawn, the path narrowed and became fine gravel. Harry felt the change through the soles of his shoes.

The air was cooler here. The castle sheltered them from the breeze, but the grass still held the rain, and every so often a drop fell from the leaves above with a soft, separate tap.

Luna walked a few paces ahead with the notebook.

“Continue to the beech tree,” Luna read.

There were five trees in view.

Harry knew the one he meant: broad, copper-leaved, with a generous crown and a bench beneath it. He had never needed to identify it to anyone.

“The large beech tree,” Harry amended.

“They might not know a beech,” Neville said.

Harry looked at him.

“You do realise this is going to become a book.”

Neville grinned.

“Only if we put in all five trees.”

Luna stopped where the path divided around a long patch of grass.

“There’s a bench,” Luna said. “That’s easier to recognise.”

Harry joined her. From there, the bench was plainly visible, its back a pale line beneath the leaves.

“Follow the gravel path down to the wooden bench beneath the broad tree,” Harry said.

“That will do,” Neville replied.

They took the right-hand curve round the grass and reached the bench. Its seat was damp at one end, where water had blown beneath the branches. Neville dried the whole length with a small movement of his wand.

Harry added another note. The bench would make a useful stopping place, particularly for someone who wanted a few minutes outside rather than a full circuit.

He had called the guide a short walk. He began to think it ought to offer a shorter one as well.

***

They sat with the notebook open across Harry’s knees.

From the bench, the lake appeared between two sloping shoulders of lawn. The water was grey near the shore and silver farther out, where the clouds were opening. A few yellow leaves lay on the gravel at their feet, each holding a small dark patch of rain.

Neville stretched his legs.

“People can turn back here,” Neville said. “It’s a good view.”

Harry nodded and wrote, If you would like a shorter walk, return by the same path.

He read the sentence again, considering whether it needed anything else.

“Tell them the castle stays in sight,” Luna suggested.

“From here?”

“Nearly all the way. That’s reassuring if you’re somewhere new.”

Harry looked back. The castle rose clearly beyond the lawn, its windows catching the late light. He had spent so much of his life recognising Hogwarts from every angle that he had forgotten how much there was to take in at a first visit.

He wrote, The castle remains in view along this part of the route.

Neville leaned over to read.

“Would you put how long it takes?”

“Twenty minutes?” Harry said.

“If you keep walking,” Neville replied.

Luna was watching a leaf turn slowly on the surface of a shallow puddle.

“Perhaps half an hour, with time to sit,” Luna said.

Harry crossed out twenty and wrote about half an hour.

That seemed a better promise. There would be no reason to hurry past the best place to rest merely to complete the walk within the time printed at the top.

For a little while, none of them spoke. Harry rested the quill across the page and let his shoulders settle against the bench.

***

The next part of the path ran almost level across the slope. It did not go close to the lake, but gave a wider view of it before turning gently towards the greenhouses.

Harry had described it as the lower path. Reading that now, he could see a faint track farther down the grass which might equally answer the description.

Luna glanced from one to the other.

“This gravel path?” Luna asked.

“Yes,” Harry said. “We stay on this one.”

“Then say that.”

Harry wrote, Stay on the gravel path as it curves to the right. There is no need to leave it to see the lake.

Neville bent to examine the edge of the gravel, where rain had washed a little earth across it.

“This bit can be soft after wet weather,” Neville said. “I’ll ask for some fresh gravel.”

Harry tested the ground with his foot. It was firm enough, though the outer edge was muddy.

“For now, we can mention sturdy shoes,” Harry said.

“Near the beginning,” Luna replied. “It’s rather late to learn that here.”

They stopped while Harry marked the top of the page. He was accumulating useful sentences in quite the wrong order.

On the far side of the lake, the light spread beneath the clouds. Harry watched it broaden across the water while he waited for the ink to dry. The view did not need an instruction. Anyone following the path would find it.

He left a space on the page and went on walking.

***

The greenhouses came gradually into view beyond a hedge. Their glass roofs reflected the sky in uneven squares, some bright, some pearly with moisture. A door stood open, and the warm smell of plants drifted out across the path.

Neville checked his watch.

“I’d better see to the last pots,” Neville said. “Will you two manage the return?”

“Unless there are another five trees to choose from,” Harry replied.

“Only the doors,” Neville said. “But I’ll explain those before I go.”

They stood where the path divided. One branch led directly between the greenhouses. The other curved uphill beside them, returning to the terrace.

Harry’s original notes said, Return past the greenhouses.

Luna read it aloud and looked down both branches.

“I could do either,” Luna said.

Neville pointed to the uphill path.

“That one. If you go between the glasshouses, you end up by the working doors. There are usually wheelbarrows.”

Harry wrote, At the greenhouses, take the uphill path, keeping the glass buildings on your right.

Neville considered the sentence.

“Yes. That’s clear.”

He promised to meet them at dinner, then went towards his office, pausing on the way to move an empty pot from a doorstep.

Harry watched his friend disappear into the warm green light. Neville had sounded mildly apologetic about the guide when he first brought it out, as though he ought to have completed it months ago. Yet the route depended on knowledge he used without thinking: which path stayed firm, where the bench could be seen, which doors belonged to visitors and which to people carrying trays.

Harry was glad they had come outside.

***

The return slope was gentle but longer than Harry remembered. He and Luna walked without speaking until they reached a small landing beside the greenhouse wall.

Someone had set two large terracotta pots there, planted with rosemary. The rain had sharpened its scent.

“This is a good place to pause,” Luna said.

Harry looked back down the path. From here, the greenhouses stood between them and most of the grounds, making the route feel briefly separate from the castle.

“Do we need to say that?” Harry asked.

Luna considered the question.

“Perhaps only that the path goes uphill. People will see the place to stop.”

Harry nodded. The guide could become so attentive that it interrupted the walk every few paces.

He crossed out a sentence he had begun about resting beside the pots. Then he added a simpler note near the beginning: The return includes a gentle uphill stretch.

Luna leaned against the low wall while he wrote.

“I like directions that tell you just enough,” Luna said. “Then you can look about.”

“That’s the difficult part,” Harry replied. “Knowing what enough is.”

“You’ll know when you don’t need to keep looking at the paper.”

They resumed walking. Harry tucked the notebook under his arm and tried to notice where he would want to check it.

At the top of the slope, the terrace appeared again, with the castle wall beyond. There was only one sensible turning. He would need a final sentence to confirm it, but no more.

For the first time, the whole guide seemed possible on one page.

***

Back at the front steps, Luna handed him the notebook.

“You’ve got us home,” Luna said.

Harry looked at the scattered additions, arrows and crossings-out.

“On paper, we may still be by the round flower bed.”

They stood aside as two pupils came down the steps carrying a chessboard between them. One piece was complaining about being tilted. The pupils settled on the broad wall beside the entrance, absorbed in arranging their game.

Harry turned to a clean page and began putting the directions in order.

Luna waited on the bottom step, her folder beside her. The air had grown cooler, but there was still daylight enough for another circuit.

Harry read the new version aloud. It began with the length and the ground underfoot, then moved from the oak doors to the terrace, the stone-edged bed, the bench and the curve towards the greenhouses.

When he finished, Luna nodded.

“I think I could follow that,” Luna said.

“You’ve just walked it.”

“That does give me an advantage.”

Harry smiled at the page. He could either spend another hour wondering whether it worked or follow it once more.

“Will you read it again?” Harry asked. “This time, we’ll do exactly what it says.”

Luna picked up her folder.

“Yes,” Luna said. “Though I might sit on the bench for a little longer.”

Harry added a small line beneath the title: Allow about half an hour, or longer if you wish to pause.

Then he handed her the notebook, and they began again.

***

The second walk felt different almost at once.

Harry had expected to spend it correcting things. Instead, the first instructions took them neatly from the steps to the terrace, leaving him free to notice the evening arriving across the grounds.

A strip of clear sky had opened in the west. The stone beside the path was drying in patches. Somewhere behind a window above them, someone was practising a tune on a flute, taking the same few bars slowly until they joined.

At the round bed, Luna read the next sentence and turned down the sloping gravel path.

“No hesitation,” Harry said.

“It’s a very definite round bed,” Luna replied.

Halfway to the bench, they met Professor McGonagall coming uphill with a folded tartan shawl over one arm. She stopped when she saw them.

“Potter. Miss Lovegood,” McGonagall said. “I understand you are both joining us for dinner.”

“Yes, Professor,” Harry replied.

“And Professor Longbottom tells me you have taken charge of his walking directions.”

“Only the handwriting,” Harry said. “Most of it’s his.”

McGonagall glanced at the notebook Luna held.

“A useful undertaking,” McGonagall said. “I have directed three visitors to that bench this month. One returned having inspected the outside of every greenhouse.”

“We’ve tried to prevent that,” Harry replied.

“Then I shall be pleased to see the finished page,” McGonagall said.

She continued up the path at a brisk, measured pace. Harry watched her turn correctly at the round bed without consulting anything.

Luna looked down at their instructions.

“I expect she’d be rather difficult to use as a test,” Luna said.

Harry laughed, and they went on.

***

At the bench, Luna closed the notebook.

The sky had softened to pale gold above the hills. There was very little wind. Across the lake, the reflected clouds seemed to rest just beneath the surface, disturbed now and then by widening circles.

Harry sat at the dry end of the bench. Luna settled beside him and placed her folder on her lap.

“What did you find in the library?” Harry asked.

“Several very good drawings,” Luna replied. “And some notes about the plants the moths prefer. The drawings showed them with their wings open, but the notes were mostly about where they sat.”

“Was that useful?”

“Very. You spend a great deal of time looking at closed wings.”

Harry smiled. He could imagine her waiting quite happily beside a patch of heather while something small declined to display itself.

Luna opened the folder and showed him a copied drawing. The moth had softly marked wings in brown and cream, with a fine fringe along their edges. In one corner, Luna had drawn the leaf on which it rested.

Harry looked from the drawing to the grounds.

“I think I was trying to describe everything,” Harry said.

“In the guide?”

“Yes. As though someone might miss the walk if I left something out.”

Luna slid the drawing back into its folder.

“They’ll have their own walk,” Luna said. “They might notice things we haven’t.”

Harry sat with that for a moment. A bird moved through the beech above them, shaking a few drops onto the gravel. He looked up but could see only leaves.

When they rose, he did not add the bird, the colour of the sky or the sound of the water to his page.

***

They reached the turn by the greenhouses without needing to consult the notebook again.

Luna read the uphill instruction before they took it. Harry checked the wording against the scene: glass buildings on the right, the slope ahead, the terrace at the top.

It was clear.

Beside the rosemary pots, they paused long enough for Harry to look back. The bench was hidden now, but he could trace the course of the path through the grounds. It seemed modest from here, a small loop taking in a piece of lawn, a tree, some water and a row of working greenhouses.

That was enough for the purpose Neville had described.

At the terrace, Luna read the last direction.

“Turn left and follow the castle wall back to the front steps.”

They did. The oak doors stood open ahead, and warm light was beginning to gather in the entrance hall.

Harry took back the notebook.

“I think that’s it,” Harry said.

Luna looked at the page once more.

“There’s one thing,” Luna said.

Harry waited, quill ready.

“You’ve put ‘walk steadily’ near the beginning. Do they need to?”

He read the sentence. He had written it while calculating the time.

“No,” Harry said.

He crossed out the two words.

The final page would have no crossings-out, but he thought he might keep the working sheets. They had become a record of an afternoon spent discovering how much could be removed once the useful parts were right.

***

Dinner was already being laid when they entered the Great Hall.

The enchanted ceiling held the clear evening sky, with a few high clouds still lit along their edges. Harry and Luna joined Neville near the end of the staff table. Neville had washed and changed his jumper, though a small green leaf remained caught against his cuff.

“How did it go?” Neville asked.

“We returned to the intended doors,” Luna replied.

“Both times,” Harry added.

Neville looked pleased.

Over a supper of vegetable pie, potatoes and broad beans, Harry told him what they had changed. Neville listened closely to the directions about the bench and the greenhouse turn.

“I ought to have thought of that,” Neville said.

“You knew where you meant,” Harry replied. “That was the trouble.”

Neville pulled the leaf from his cuff and set it beside his plate.

“I suppose I do that in lessons as well.”

“Probably less often,” Harry said. “The plants let you know.”

Neville smiled.

“Usually quite firmly.”

They spoke of easier things after that. Luna described the library drawings. Neville explained that the little blue-flowered plant in his office opened each blossom for only a few seconds and was consequently a poor choice for anyone who wanted to sketch it. Harry asked whether it ever exhausted itself.

“Only if you overwater it,” Neville said. “Then it sulks for a week.”

By the time pudding arrived, the tall windows had darkened. Harry felt the comfortable tiredness of an afternoon outdoors. There was still the fair copy to make, but the work of deciding was finished.

***

They returned to Neville’s office after dinner.

The greenhouse jars had stopped dripping. Inside, Neville lit a lamp and cleared a space on the desk. He found a sheet of good parchment, a ruler and a bottle of dark blue ink.

Harry washed his hands again to make certain there was no trace of soil on his fingers. Then he sat down.

The room was quiet while he wrote. Luna settled by the window with her folder, checking the labels on her copied drawings. Neville made notes for the following morning, occasionally looking towards a plant on the shelf as though remembering something it would need.

Harry gave the title a line to itself. Under it he wrote the time, the advice about shoes and the gentle slope on the return. He left comfortable spaces between the directions. The page looked welcoming before it was half finished.

When he reached the bench, he included the shorter option. Then came the level path above the lake, the turn at the greenhouses, and the return beside the castle.

He read each sentence before moving on.

At the bottom there was room for a final note. Harry considered it for a while, then wrote that the route was intended for daylight hours.

He put down the quill.

Neville came round the desk. Luna joined them, and the three of them read the page in silence.

“It sounds like the walk,” Neville said at last.

Harry looked over it once more. The route began somewhere definite, gave the walker choices where they were useful, and brought them back.

“Yes,” Harry said. “I think it does.”

***

Neville waited for the ink to dry before making several copies with a careful charm. Harry watched the words appear, line by line, on the fresh sheets.

One copy would go in the entrance hall for visitors who asked. Another could be left in the guest rooms. Neville kept the original on his desk until he could give it to McGonagall in the morning.

“There,” Neville said. “That’s been on my list since June.”

Harry leaned back in his chair.

“Mine since half past four.”

“It was a very busy half past four,” Luna observed.

They tidied the desk together. Neville put away the ink, Luna gathered her drawings, and Harry stacked the working pages with their arrows and amendments. He folded them once and slipped them into his bag.

Outside, the greenhouse glass reflected their lamplight. Beyond it, the grounds had become shapes: the low line of the hedge, the darker slope of the lawn, the castle windows above.

They left the office and walked back uphill. Neville locked the door behind them. At the top, Harry could just make out where their route joined the terrace.

Tomorrow someone might follow it with the page in one hand. They would find their own pace, stop where they pleased, and return in time for whatever had brought them to Hogwarts.

Harry had no urge to go over the wording again.

***

His guest room was in a quiet corridor on the second floor.

Neville showed him the door and wished him goodnight. Luna’s room was farther along, near the turn in the passage.

“Goodnight, Harry,” Luna said. “I’m glad you stayed.”

“So am I,” Harry replied.

Inside, a small fire was burning behind a brass guard. There was a deep chair, a washstand and a bed with a folded woollen blanket across its foot. Someone had set a jug of water and a clean glass beside the lamp.

Harry put his bag on the chair. The room looked out over part of the grounds, though the window reflected more of the fire than the night beyond it.

He washed, changed and laid his clothes neatly over the chair back. In the pocket of his robes he found a small piece of gravel. He set it on the windowsill to take outside in the morning.

Then he drew the curtains.

The bed was cool at first. Harry pulled the blanket over the quilt and settled against the pillows with a quiet sigh. There was nothing left to finish downstairs. Neville had his guide; Luna had her drawings; the grounds would keep until morning.

He took off his glasses and placed them beside the water glass.

For a little while he watched the firelight move across the ceiling. He thought of the bench beneath the beech, then of Neville bending over the finished page, pleased to have a small task done well.

Harry reached for the lamp and extinguished it.

The fire settled softly. His breathing grew slower, and before its light had faded from the ceiling, he was asleep.
