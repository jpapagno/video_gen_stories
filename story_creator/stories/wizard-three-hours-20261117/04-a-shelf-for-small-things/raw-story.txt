A Shelf for Small Things

By four o’clock, Neville Longbottom had moved the same ball of string to five different places.

It had begun beside the seed ledger, where it prevented the book from opening properly. He had transferred it to the windowsill, where it rolled into a damp patch, then to the drawer beneath the potting bench, where it vanished among labels and old receipts. Now it sat in his coat pocket, making a small, determined lump against his hip.

The greenhouse potting room was quiet around him. Rain whispered on the roof, and the late October light lay pale upon the scrubbed benches. Beyond the connecting door, a self-watering can moved slowly along a row of ferns, tipping itself at each pot with the solemn care of a waiter.

Neville took the string out of his pocket.

“What I’d like to know,” he said, “is where you ought to live.”

“That depends,” Luna Lovegood replied from the doorway, “on how often you’re needed.”

Neville turned, smiling before he had quite finished turning. Luna stood with rain shining on her blue coat and a small overnight bag hanging from one shoulder. She had written to say she would arrive after lunch. Neville had put the letter somewhere sensible and then forgotten that sensible place entirely.

“Luna. I thought you were coming tomorrow.”

Luna unfastened her coat. “Tomorrow is when I’m leaving.”

“Yes. Of course it is.” Neville put the string on the bench again. “You’ve caught me organising.”

“I thought you looked unusually surrounded.”

They had known one another long enough for Neville to hear the affection in that. He took her coat and hung it beside his own. Her visit had been arranged for weeks, a quiet Friday between journeys, with supper at Hogwarts and a guest room upstairs. She wanted to see the greenhouses in their autumn condition. He wanted to hear where she had been, and to show her the little copper kettle he had finally persuaded to stop whistling during lessons.

Instead, she had arrived at the precise moment he was considering whether string could reasonably be stored inside a teapot.

Luna looked about the room. It was narrow and warmly untidy, with a stone sink, a deep cupboard, two benches and a window overlooking the kitchen garden. On one wall, between the door and Neville’s desk, three pale rectangles showed where notices had once hung.

“There used to be a shelf there,” Neville said, following her gaze. “It came down when we had the wall repaired. I meant to put one back.”

Luna placed her bag beneath the desk. “Perhaps that’s where your string lives.”

“Perhaps. Though I don’t want another shelf that becomes a place for everything nobody knows what to do with.”

Luna considered the ball on the bench.

“Then we’ll have to work out what belongs on it.”

Neville had imagined spending her visit showing her things. It was pleasant, unexpectedly, to be offered help with something he had not managed to begin.

He went to fetch the shelf.

It was waiting behind a cupboard, wrapped in a towel which had once been white. Neville drew it out and laid it across the bench. The wood was honey-coloured oak, with a rounded front edge and two plain brackets. Someone had written POT ROOM underneath in pencil.

“I found it in the stores in September,” Neville explained. “It’s the right size. I’ve been waiting until I knew what to put on it.”

Luna ran a finger along the rounded edge.

“That may be why it’s been waiting too.”

Neville laughed. He could be patient with things that took years to flower, but he sometimes needed a friend to point out that putting up a shelf did not require the same preparation.

They cleared a space on the bench. Luna found a cloth, Neville brought a bowl of warm water from the sink, and together they washed away the dust. The oak darkened beneath their hands, releasing a faint, clean smell.

Outside, rain gathered at the corners of the panes. A robin landed on the garden wall, shook itself and disappeared into the ivy.

Luna worked slowly along one bracket.

“Who will use it?” she asked.

“Me. The students. Hagrid, sometimes. Anyone helping out in here.”

“And what do they usually need?”

Neville glanced towards the deep cupboard.

“That’s rather the problem. They ask for all sorts of things.”

He opened the cupboard to demonstrate.

There were stacks of terracotta pots, a sack of clean sand, watering attachments, folded aprons, several empty tins and a large basket containing objects that had accumulated over the term. Neville lifted the basket out and set it between them.

A wooden label fell through a gap in the wicker.

“Oh, good,” he said. “I was looking for that.”

Luna picked it up. It read PLEASE LEAVE THE LID ON.

“Which lid?” she asked.

“I can’t remember.”

Luna laid the label aside. “We may have to leave that one until we do.”

They began with the string.

Neville explained that he used it for tying stems, securing bundles of labels and fastening sacks that no longer trusted their own drawcords. Students borrowed it almost every day.

“Definitely the shelf,” Luna said.

Next came a small pair of brass scissors, three pencils, a tin of plant labels, two smooth stones, a spoon, a broken hinge and a narrow wooden dibber.

Neville put the hinge directly into a box beneath the bench.

“Mending,” he said firmly.

Luna examined the spoon.

“Tea?” she asked.

“Compost measurements.”

“Then it shouldn’t look so inviting.”

Neville found a pencil and wrote COMPOST on a label, then tied it through the hole in the spoon’s handle. Luna gave an approving nod.

For the first time that afternoon, the collection before them began to seem manageable.

They discovered that sorting was easy until Neville knew the history of a thing.

The smooth stones, for instance, were useful for weighting open the seed ledger. One had come from the edge of the lake; the other had been given to him by a second-year who had thought it resembled a kneazle asleep. It did, slightly, if the light came from the left.

The little tin contained perfectly ordinary labels, but the tin itself had belonged to Professor Sprout. Neville still remembered her setting it down on a bench and explaining, without looking up, that a label written later was a label never written.

“I use that every morning,” Neville said.

“Then it sounds very at home here,” Luna replied.

The dibber proved more complicated. It was too small for most of Neville’s work, though useful when first-years planted beans. He turned it over in his hands.

“It might go on the shelf.”

Luna looked at the growing line of objects. “How often?”

“Twice a year.”

“Perhaps it could live near the beans.”

Neville returned it to a shallow drawer, where the bean trays were stored. The drawer closed without objection.

“That’s better,” he admitted.

Luna had found a little bell beneath a folded apron. She held it up by its loop.

“What does this do?”

“Rings,” Neville said.

Luna smiled at him.

“Yes, sorry. I mean, it’s for when someone’s working at the far end of the greenhouses. The doors carry the sound along. You ring if you need another pair of hands.”

She rang it once.

A clear, modest note travelled through the room. In the adjoining greenhouse, the watering can paused, turned its spout towards the sound, and then resumed its work.

“That seems useful,” Luna said.

“It is, when I can find it.”

They placed it beside the string.

Gradually they assembled a promising group: scissors, pencils, labels, bell, string, a folded cloth and the two stones for holding pages open. The shelf would take them easily, with space to spare.

Neville looked pleased for almost half a minute.

Then he opened the desk drawer in search of a ruler and found the woollen gloves.

They were a small pair, green, with one thumb darned in yellow. A student had left them after a cold outdoor lesson the previous week. Neville had meant to take them up to the castle.

“Those shouldn’t go on the shelf,” he said.

Luna touched the yellow thumb. “Because they belong to someone?”

“Yes. But people do leave things. Gloves, hair ribbons, handkerchiefs. I ought to have somewhere for them.”

She looked at the shelf, then at the window.

“Perhaps somewhere else. Otherwise someone looking for scissors might spend the afternoon wearing a stranger’s gloves.”

Neville could imagine that happening with uncomfortable clarity.

He put the gloves on top of Luna’s bag so he would remember them at supper. Then, because his hand was already in the drawer, he took out a packet of biscuits.

“I knew there was a reason I kept this drawer.”

The kettle was on a little iron stand beside the sink. Neville tapped it with his wand and filled the teapot while Luna washed two cups.

The kettle gave one soft, satisfied puff.

“You’ve changed it,” Luna observed.

“It used to whistle whenever anyone said ‘steep’. That was awkward in Herbology.”

“I imagine there are quite a lot of steep slopes in conversations about roots.”

“More than you’d think.”

They carried their tea to the window bench, where the glass was warm from the room and cool near the frame. Neville opened the biscuits. They were ginger ones, a little softer than they ought to have been.

Luna accepted one without comment.

The rain had thinned. On the far side of the garden, someone’s abandoned wheelbarrow was filling with a collection of bright leaves. A gust sent two more turning down into it.

Neville rested his cup against his knee.

“I thought I’d have this room properly arranged by now.”

“How long have you been teaching here?” Luna asked.

“Long enough to stop using the word ‘temporary’ about that basket.”

Luna looked towards it. “You’ve done rather a lot of other things.”

“Yes, but this is such a small thing.”

“Small things can take a long time when you only remember them while you’re looking for something else.”

Neville smiled into his tea. That was exactly what happened. He would need a pencil, open three drawers, make a private promise to sort everything out on Saturday, and then discover that Saturday contained marking, pruning, a staff meeting and somebody’s pumpkin behaving unexpectedly.

“It would be nice,” he said, “to reach for something and find it.”

Luna nodded. “That seems a good reason for a shelf.”

They sat for a while, eating their biscuits. Luna told him about a boarding house where the landlady kept all the teaspoons in a vase because she disliked opening drawers. Neville told Luna about the first-year who had carefully labelled every pot in a tray, including an empty one marked NOTHING YET.

“He said it was the most accurate label,” Neville added.

“I like him,” Luna said.

By the time they finished their tea, the rain had stopped.

Neville rose and picked up his wand.

“Shall we see whether it fits?”

The shelf lifted from the bench and floated towards the wall. Luna stood a little way back while Neville held it in place.

“Higher?” he asked.

“A little lower,” Luna replied. “For the first-years.”

Neville lowered it.

“Too low for Hagrid, though.”

Luna tilted her head. “Most things are.”

They settled on a height Neville could reach comfortably and a first-year could manage without standing on anything. With a few careful movements of his wand, he fixed the brackets into the stone. The shelf gave a tiny creak, then became still.

He tested it with his hand before letting go.

“There.”

The bare board looked surprisingly hopeful against the old wall.

They placed the tin of labels at one end, then the pencils in a jam jar. Neville arranged the scissors beside them, laid down the cloth, and set the stones towards the back. Luna put the bell near the front edge where it could be seen.

The string rolled off.

Neville caught it against his chest.

“I might have expected that.”

Luna looked around for a suitable container. The tins were too shallow or too large. A flowerpot held the string well enough, but its rough drainage hole caught the end when Neville tried to pull some out.

“We need something smooth,” he said.

“And heavy enough to stay,” Luna added.

Neville turned the pot in his hands. “I’ll find something.”

They left the string on the bench for the moment.

Then Neville stood back again, and something in his expression made Luna glance at him.

“What is it?” she asked.

“Hagrid.”

Luna waited.

“He comes in for labels and string sometimes. He asked whether I’d put the shelf up yet.” Neville rubbed the side of his nose. “I said I’d tell him when I did. I think he’s got something for it.”

“Then we’d better leave a space.”

Neville looked along the board. Everything had fitted so neatly that he had spread the objects out, giving the tin and jar an air of considerable importance.

“How much space?”

Luna glanced towards the doorway.

“With Hagrid, perhaps a generous amount.”

They moved the labels and pencils closer together.

Neville wrote a short note asking Hagrid to call in before supper, folded it into a square and touched it with his wand. The paper gave a small twitch, lifted, and slipped out through the open upper window.

Luna watched it cross the garden.

“Does that always find him?”

“Usually. Once it found his coat. He’d left it over a fence.”

“Was the coat able to help?”

“It waited until he came back.”

While they waited, Neville suggested a walk through the greenhouses. They had cleared the bench, the shelf was sound, and the air outside the potting room had that rich, leafy warmth Luna had always liked.

They went slowly between the rows. In the first greenhouse, seedlings stood beneath glass covers, each carrying its own tiny weather. One tray held a fine mist; another enjoyed a clear golden glow. Neville adjusted a cover with his fingertips.

“Too much sun in that one,” he said.

Luna bent to read a label. “Does it know it’s October?”

“I’m trying to persuade it.”

They passed broad leaves that folded politely away from the path and a row of young plants whose stems hummed when the watering can went by. Neville stopped once to free a tendril from a support it had chosen unwisely. Luna held his cup while he worked, though it had been empty for some time.

At the far door, they stepped into the garden.

The evening was clearing. Water shone in the seams between the stones, and the hills beyond the grounds had softened to blue. From the castle came the distant movement of people gathering before supper.

Luna slipped her hands into her cardigan pockets.

“Do you still go up to the Great Hall every evening?” she asked.

“Most evenings. Sometimes I take a tray back to my rooms if I’ve got work.”

“Do you do the work?”

Neville gave her a sideways look.

“Sometimes.”

Luna’s smile was small and untroubled.

They followed the path along the greenhouse wall. Beneath the window, Neville noticed a student’s pencil lying in the gravel. He picked it up, wiped it on his sleeve, and put it in his pocket.

“A beginning for the jar,” Luna said.

“It’ll probably be borrowed by Monday.”

“That sounds right.”

Neville looked back through the window. From outside, the new shelf was a pale line against the darker wall. He could see the bell, the tin and the jar. They looked almost as though they had always been there.

He found that he was looking forward to Monday.

Hagrid arrived carrying something wrapped in a brown checked cloth.

He filled the doorway with the cool smell of outdoors, his beard dotted with rainwater shaken from the trees. Neville moved the stool away from the bench to give him room.

“Got yer note,” Hagrid said. “Looks good, that.”

He nodded at the shelf.

“We’re still deciding a few things,” Neville replied. “Luna’s been helping.”

“Hello, Hagrid,” Luna said. “We’ve left you a space.”

Hagrid looked at the gap with an expression of pleased embarrassment.

“Didn’t want ter get in the way. Just thought it might be useful.”

He unwrapped the cloth.

Inside was a shallow wooden tray, scarcely longer than Neville’s hand. It had raised sides, a sloping front and a little round cup fixed into one corner. The wood was dark and rubbed smooth. Along one edge, a row of small pegs stood upright like blunt teeth.

Neville lifted it carefully.

“Did you make this?”

Hagrid nodded.

“Bit of pear wood. Been seasonin’ in the shed for ages. Thought yer could put yer string in the cup. Feed it through there, see?”

He pointed to a smooth notch in the rim.

Neville placed the ball inside and drew the end through. It moved freely.

“Oh, that’s excellent.”

“Pegs are fer loops,” Hagrid explained. “Short bits worth keepin’. Always end up in a knot if yer put ’em all together.”

Luna ran a finger over the polished corner.

“What’s the tray for?”

“Whatever yer usin’.” Hagrid’s large hand hovered above it. “Labels, perhaps. Or somethin’ yer need to leave out till tomorrow. Stops it gettin’ swept off with the earth.”

Neville set the tray in the space they had left.

It fitted, but only just. The back corner pressed against the jam jar, and the bell had to move.

Hagrid noticed at once.

“Could make it smaller.”

“No,” Neville said. “No, it’s the right size.”

He lifted the two stones off the shelf and laid them on the desk, beside the ledger they were meant to hold open. Then he moved the folded cloth to a hook beneath the sink.

The tray settled squarely on the oak.

“There,” Neville said.

Luna placed the scissors in the shallow section, handles facing out. Neville put two short lengths of string over the pegs and drew a fresh length from the cup.

Nothing rolled. Nothing caught.

Hagrid watched with quiet satisfaction.

“Rubbed that notch down three times,” he said. “Didn’t want it frayin’.”

“You’ve thought of everything,” Neville replied.

Hagrid looked pleased, though he bent to fold the checked cloth with great attention.

They tried the shelf as though a lesson were about to begin.

Neville fetched a small empty pot and set it on the bench. Luna reached for a label and a pencil. Hagrid drew off a piece of string, holding the ball steady with one finger until Neville pointed out that he did not need to.

“Habit,” Hagrid said, withdrawing his hand.

Neville cut the string. Luna wrote PRACTICE on the label in her fine, rather floating handwriting, and they tied it loosely around the pot.

For a moment, all three regarded their work.

“Very well labelled,” Luna said.

“Won’t mistake it for anything else,” Hagrid agreed.

Neville laughed, and Hagrid’s broad face opened into a smile.

Then Luna lifted the empty pot away.

“What happens to the things afterwards?” she asked.

Neville looked at the label and the short piece of string on the bench.

The question was so ordinary that he almost answered without thinking. Instead, he unwound the string, hung it over one of Hagrid’s pegs, and slipped the label into the front of the tray.

“We use them again.”

“And the scissors?” Luna asked.

Neville returned them to the tray.

The little arrangement looked ready.

Hagrid leaned closer to inspect the bell.

“Found that, then?”

“Bottom of a basket,” Neville admitted.

“Thought I hadn’t heard it lately.”

Neville rang it softly. The note travelled through the open door, and somewhere in the next greenhouse the watering can paused again.

“Useful sound,” Hagrid said.

Luna glanced at the remaining strip of empty oak beside the tin.

“Shall we leave that bit?”

Neville considered it. There was room for another jar, perhaps, or a little box. He could already think of several things that might go there.

Then he looked at Hagrid’s tray, holding just the few objects they had needed.

“Yes,” Neville said. “Let’s leave it.”

Hagrid stayed while Neville put the spare tins away. Luna brushed the bench, gathering the dry crumbs of soil into a dustpan. The room seemed larger now, although none of the furniture had moved.

Before they left, Neville found a small basket for lost belongings and placed it beneath the coat hooks. The green gloves went inside, beside a ribbon Luna had discovered behind the cupboard.

Neville took them out again.

“Supper,” he reminded himself.

He put the gloves in his pocket.

Hagrid held the door while they collected their coats. At the threshold, Neville turned for one last look.

The shelf was modest enough: a jar, a tin, a bell, and the wooden tray with its ball of string. Beneath it, the bench was clear.

He extinguished the lamps.

They walked up to the castle together beneath a sky the colour of clean slate.

Hagrid took the slope slowly so that they could remain abreast. Luna carried her overnight bag, and Neville carried the empty cups, having remembered them just before locking the greenhouse door. Warm windows shone ahead of them.

At the entrance hall, Hagrid made his farewells. He had supper waiting at his own hearth and wanted to be home before the evening grew colder.

“Tell me if the pegs want movin’,” Hagrid said.

“I will,” Neville promised. “Thank you.”

Luna touched Hagrid’s sleeve.

“The pear wood is lovely,” she said.

Hagrid looked down at her. “Thought you’d notice.”

He went back down the steps, his great coat moving in the evening breeze.

In the Great Hall, Neville found the owner of the gloves before the soup arrived. The girl accepted them with obvious relief and immediately put them on, despite the warmth.

“Yellow thumb,” she explained. “My grandmother did it.”

“I thought you’d want them back,” Neville said.

When he returned to his place, Luna was looking up at the enchanted ceiling. The clouds had parted above the floating candles, showing a broad, quiet stretch of stars.

“It’s a good ceiling for coming indoors,” she said.

Neville sat beside her. “I sometimes forget to look.”

They ate leek soup, bread still warm in the middle, and a dish of potatoes with rosemary. Around them, the familiar sounds of supper rose and settled: cutlery, conversation, the scrape of a bench, someone laughing several tables away.

Luna asked about Neville’s classes, and he told her about the students who had begun the term afraid of getting soil on their sleeves and now argued cheerfully over whose cuttings had rooted first. He asked about her travels. She described a quiet valley where the mornings smelled of wild mint, and an innkeeper who kept a chair by the fire for anyone who arrived with wet shoes.

By the time the dishes cleared, Neville felt as though the day had lengthened pleasantly without becoming tiring.

He showed Luna to her guest room, where a lamp had already been lit. She left her bag by the bed and came back to the doorway.

“What will you put in the tray tomorrow?” she asked.

Neville thought of the practice label.

“Something I’m in the middle of.”

“That sounds useful,” Luna replied.

He wished her goodnight and continued along the corridor to his own rooms.

Neville’s sitting room was warm, with a low fire behind the grate and a reading lamp beside the armchair. His slippers waited under the chair, one upright and one lying on its side.

He set them straight with his foot, hung up his coat, and took the pencil from his pocket.

For a moment he considered putting it on the mantelpiece.

Instead, he laid it beside his keys. He would see it there in the morning and take it down to the greenhouse.

There was a little marking to do. Neville looked at the pile on his desk, then at the clock. Nothing was needed before Monday. He placed a sheet of paper over the essays to keep them tidy and closed the ink bottle.

At the window, he paused.

The greenhouses were dark shapes below the castle, their roofs holding a faint reflection of the sky. He could just distinguish the path they had taken and the pale wall of the kitchen garden.

Tomorrow he would need labels for the winter seedlings. He would reach for the tin. There would be a pencil beside it, scissors in the tray, and string that came smoothly through the polished notch.

He smiled, drew the curtains, and went to wash.

The water was pleasantly hot. He changed into his pyjamas, folded his clothes over the chair, and turned down the bed. The sheets felt cool against his feet. Beyond the bedroom door, the fire gave a small settling sound.

Neville switched off the lamp.

For a little while, he lay listening to the familiar quiet of his rooms. He thought of Luna in the guest room down the corridor, of Hagrid home by his hearth, and of the shelf waiting in the potting room.

There was still that little space beside the tin.

He was glad they had left it.

His breathing slowed. The warmth beneath the blankets reached his toes, and his hand relaxed upon the sheet. Before the last faint sounds of the castle had faded, Neville was asleep.
