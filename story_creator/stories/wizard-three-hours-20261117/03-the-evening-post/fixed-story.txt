The Evening Post

By the time Harry reached Hogsmeade, the lamps were coming on above the shop doors, and the last of the afternoon lay in pale strips along the wet pavement. Rain had passed through an hour earlier. It had left the village smelling of stone, chimney smoke and the damp wool of people returning home.

Ron was waiting outside the Three Broomsticks with his hands in his coat pockets.

“You’re late,” Ron said, although he sounded pleased to see him.

“Five minutes,” Harry replied.

“I’ve been using them responsibly,” Ron said. “I’ve established that there’s shepherd’s pie.”

Harry had come north for a meeting at Hogwarts the following morning. There was nothing urgent about it: a discussion with McGonagall about arrangements for Ministry visitors, followed by lunch if the discussion remained civil. Ron, who had been delivering an order from the joke shop to a Hogsmeade stockist, had arranged to stay for supper.

For once, neither of them needed to hurry home. Their families knew where they were, Harry had a room upstairs, and the evening stretched ahead without anything written across it.

Then Hagrid came round the corner carrying a brown-paper parcel.

He held it flat on one enormous palm, with the care he might have given a sleeping creature. A length of green string crossed the paper. Tucked beneath it was a small cream card.

“Evenin’,” Hagrid said. “Glad I’ve caught yeh. Wondered if either of you might recognise these.”

He lifted a corner of the wrapping. Inside lay a pair of gardening gloves, their brown leather rubbed dark at the fingertips.

Ron looked from the gloves to Hagrid.

“Have they done something?” Ron asked.

“Been mended,” Hagrid replied. “Very neatly, an’ all. Trouble is, I can’t remember who they’re for.”



They went inside, where the warmth met them at the door.

The Three Broomsticks was comfortably busy. A few villagers occupied the tables nearest the fire, and someone had draped a wet scarf over a chair back. The scarf was turning itself at intervals, presenting each side to the heat.

Madam Rosmerta pointed them towards a table by the window.

“I’ll come over in a moment,” Rosmerta said. “And keep that parcel off the wet bit, Hagrid. Someone’s just upset a glass.”

Hagrid put it safely in the middle of the table and settled onto the broad chair that was kept for him near the hearth.

“Picked up a basket from the mender this afternoon,” Hagrid explained. “There was a strap for one of the school barrows, a buckle, couple of things for the greenhouses. An’ these. I remember takin’ them down, only it was a fortnight ago, and I’ve had a fair few things to remember since.”

Harry drew the cream card out from under the string. In small, precise handwriting, it said: Right thumb reinforced. Cuffs finished. Paid.

There was no name.

“Could the mender tell you?” Harry asked.

“Gone to visit her sister till Monday,” Hagrid said. “Left the basket next door for me. I could keep ’em till she gets back, o’ course. Only she said there was something in it wanted for tomorrow.”

Ron pulled the parcel closer.

“Could be the barrow strap,” Ron said.

“Could be,” Hagrid agreed. “But I’d sooner get everything where it belongs.”

Harry understood that. He had once imagined that a peaceful life would consist chiefly of having very little to do. Increasingly, he found he liked having one small thing to put right, provided it could be done before supper went cold.

“Let’s have a look,” Harry said.

Hagrid undid the string. The gloves were adult-sized, with broad cuffs of faded green cloth. Fresh stitching ran round the right thumb, and a small patch had been fitted so carefully into one palm that Harry noticed it only when he tilted the leather towards the lamp.

“They’re certainly not yours,” Ron told Hagrid.

“Got mine here,” Hagrid said, patting a pocket large enough to contain a loaf of bread.

He brought out one glove. It was roughly the size of the parcel.



Rosmerta arrived with three tankards and the news that there was indeed shepherd’s pie.

“Three, please,” Ron said at once.

“Make mine a large one,” Hagrid added.

Rosmerta glanced at the open parcel.

“Those came out well,” Rosmerta said.

Hagrid brightened. “Yeh know whose they are?”

“I remember seeing them before they went in,” Rosmerta replied. “The cuff was hanging off one. You were sitting just there, trying to keep a bag of bulbs from rolling under the table.”

“That sounds like me,” Hagrid said.

Harry waited while Rosmerta thought.

“It was the afternoon we had all that wind,” Rosmerta continued. “People kept coming in with leaves stuck to them. I couldn’t tell you who brought the gloves, though.”

“Anyone gardening?” Ron asked.

“Half the village, at this time of year,” Rosmerta said. “Getting things in before the ground turns difficult. I’ve still got two empty boxes round the back that I keep meaning to do something with.”

She took away an abandoned glass from the neighbouring table.

“You might ask at the little garden by the lane,” Rosmerta added. “There’s been somebody working there most afternoons.”

When she had gone, Harry turned one glove over. There was a dusting of fine grit in a fold of the cuff, and a small white mark near the wrist.

“Neville?” Harry suggested.

Hagrid stroked his beard.

“Could well be,” Hagrid said. “He brings things down with mine sometimes. But I saw him yesterday, and he didn’t mention ’em.”

“He probably has more than one pair,” Harry said.

“He has more than one of most gardening things,” Ron replied. “I went into his greenhouse once looking for a chair. There were six things that looked like chairs, and apparently none of them were.”

A brief smile moved through Hagrid’s beard.

Harry set the gloves side by side. Whoever owned them had worn the leather soft, then thought them worth repairing. The fingers had kept the slight curve of a hand resting round a tool.

“We could walk down after we’ve eaten,” Harry said. “See if anyone’s there.”

Ron looked towards the kitchen door.

“After,” Ron agreed firmly.



Supper arrived under a faint haze of steam.

For a while, the parcel rested between them while they ate and caught up with one another. Ron described a display stand at the shop which had developed the habit of applauding customers who decided against a purchase.

“George thinks it’s encouraging,” Ron said. “Hermione says it makes people feel watched.”

“What do you think?” Harry asked.

“I think it applauded a man for putting back a nose-biting teacup, and he bought three. So I’m reserving judgement.”

Hagrid had been repairing the fence beside his pumpkin patch. One stretch leaned, though the posts appeared sound.

“Ground settles,” Hagrid said. “Things grow. Yeh can have it straight as anything in spring, an’ by autumn it’s found a way of its own.”

Harry ate slowly, feeling the day loosen its hold on him. He had spent most of the afternoon travelling, with a folder of papers pressing against his knee. Now the folder was upstairs, and rainwater shone on the windows, reflecting the room in soft pieces.

Outside, a witch guided a laden shopping basket past the pub. It floated obediently at elbow height, except when it drifted towards a bakery window.

Ron followed Harry’s glance.

“Mine does that near Honeydukes,” Ron said. “Hermione thinks I’ve charmed it.”

“Have you?” Harry asked.

“No,” Ron replied. “But I admire its judgement.”

Hagrid finished first and sat back, cupping his tankard in both hands.

“Nice having you two here,” Hagrid said.

The simple way he said it quietened them for a moment.

“It’s nice being here,” Harry replied.

He meant the pub, and the village, and this particular table. He meant, too, that he could sit with Ron and Hagrid without waiting for a message to arrive or wondering how soon they would have to leave.

When Rosmerta collected the plates, she noticed Harry gathering up the string.

“If you find the owner, tell them that’s good work,” Rosmerta said. “Most people would have thrown those away.”

“Perhaps they’re comfortable,” Harry replied.

Rosmerta nodded. “That’s usually the reason.”



They stepped out into an evening that had cleared.

Above the roofs, the sky was a deep, even blue. A few stars showed between the chimney pots. Harry could see the warm square of his upstairs window, where Rosmerta had left a lamp burning.

Hagrid carried the parcel, and Ron took the side of the pavement nearest the road, where he could walk without brushing against Hagrid’s coat.

The little garden lay beside a narrow lane behind the shops. Harry had passed it before without giving it much thought. It occupied an awkward triangle of ground, too small for a building, with a low wall along one edge and a wooden bench beneath a rowan tree.

A lantern hung from the tree. Beneath it stood several pots, a folded sack, and a wheelbarrow with one handle wrapped in fresh leather.

“Well, there’s the strap,” Ron said.

Hagrid looked pleased.

“Dropped that off earlier,” Hagrid said. “Least I remembered where that went.”

The garden was empty, but someone had been working there recently. The soil in the nearest bed was newly turned, and a narrow board lay across it to keep boots from pressing it down. A trowel stood in a bucket.

Harry bent to look at a small wooden label. It bore a neat line of writing: White crocuses, three inches apart.

“That looks like Neville’s writing,” Harry said.

Ron peered over his shoulder.

“It’s legible,” Ron replied. “That narrows things down considerably from school.”

Hagrid lifted the lantern a little higher on its hook. Light spread across the bed, showing a scattering of pale bulbs arranged on the soil, ready to go in.

There was something restful about the unfinished work. Nobody had abandoned it in haste. The tools were together, the sack had been folded against the damp, and a cover waited beside the barrow. Whoever had stopped expected to return.

Harry noticed a pair of muddy kneeling pads beneath the bench.

“Perhaps he’s gone for something,” Harry said.

“Or he’s had enough sense to stop for supper,” Ron replied.



They stood for a few minutes, letting their eyes become accustomed to the garden.

A trickle of water moved somewhere behind the wall. The rowan’s remaining leaves stirred, making a dry, papery sound above them. Harry could smell the newly opened soil.

Hagrid examined the board across the bed.

“Good way of doing it,” Hagrid said. “Keeps the earth loose. Professor Sprout always used to tell me off for standing where she’d just dug.”

“You do make rather a lot of footprint,” Ron observed.

“Can’t help that,” Hagrid replied mildly.

Harry walked to the far end of the triangle. Three shallow stone bowls had been set beside the path, each filled with gravel and water. A label explained that they were for birds, and asked visitors to leave the stones in place.

Beside them, an old terracotta pot had been repaired with three shining bands.

It reminded Harry of the gloves. The garden seemed to contain a number of things that had been made useful again: the pot, the barrow, even the bench, whose newest slat was still lighter than the others.

He put his hand on the bench back. The wood was smooth and cold.

“Rosmerta said people had been working here most afternoons,” Harry said. “Does Neville come down often?”

“Once or twice a week, lately,” Hagrid replied. “Said he wanted somewhere folk could try a bit o’ gardening without worrying about getting everything wrong.”

Ron looked at the carefully spaced bulbs.

“I’d manage to put one in upside down,” Ron said.

“Neville says most of ’em work it out,” Hagrid replied.

Harry smiled. It sounded like something Neville would say with complete conviction.

From the mouth of the lane came the sound of a door closing. A figure crossed the lamplight at the far end, carrying a crate.

Harry recognised the familiar, slightly forward-leaning walk before he saw the face.

“Neville,” Harry called.



Neville turned, then came towards them with a broad smile.

“Harry! Ron! I didn’t know you were up here.”

“Neither did we, quite,” Ron said. “It’s become an investigation.”

Hagrid held out the parcel.

“Brought these back from the mender,” Hagrid explained. “Can’t place who gave ’em to me.”

Neville set his crate down by the bench and looked inside the paper.

“Oh, those,” Neville said warmly. “I wondered whether they’d be ready.”

Hagrid’s shoulders relaxed.

“Yours, then?”

“They used to be,” Neville replied. “I was having them done for someone else.”

Harry laughed softly.

“We’d got as far as your handwriting,” Harry said.

Neville glanced at the bulb label.

“I’m afraid I’ve labelled rather a lot,” Neville admitted. “People kept asking where they could stand.”

“That’s useful,” Ron said. “I generally discover where I shouldn’t have stood afterwards.”

Neville picked up the right glove, flexing the repaired thumb between his fingers.

“That’s much better,” Neville said. “It had started catching whenever you closed your hand. I asked for the cuffs to be made a little shorter as well.”

“Who are they for?” Harry asked.

“Madam Rosmerta,” Neville replied. “She’s going to help with the planters tomorrow morning.”

The answer was so close to where they had started that all four of them were quiet for a moment.

Then Ron grinned.

“She admired the stitching,” Ron said. “I think that’s promising.”



Neville sat on the edge of the bench and explained.

Rosmerta had mentioned her empty planting boxes one afternoon while Neville was having lunch. He had offered to help her fill them. That had led to a conversation about the little garden, and then to Rosmerta saying that she had never really known where to begin.

“She said she could keep a pub running through a Saturday in December,” Neville told them, “but a tray of seedlings made her feel she ought to fetch someone qualified.”

Harry could imagine her saying it.

“So you gave her your gloves,” Harry said.

“I was going to,” Neville replied. “She tried them on, and they fitted across the hand, but the cuffs got in the way. Then the thumb tore. They’d been doing that for ages.”

Neville turned the glove palm upwards.

“She liked how soft they were,” Neville continued. “New ones can be rather stiff. I thought if these were repaired properly, she’d have something comfortable to start with.”

Hagrid gave a small, thoughtful nod.

“Remember now,” Hagrid said. “Yeh brought ’em when I was loading that basket. I said I’d take ’em with the rest.”

“I should have put her name on them,” Neville replied. “I meant to write a note.”

“I should’ve remembered,” Hagrid said.

“You’ve remembered everything else,” Neville assured him. “And they’re here in plenty of time.”

Ron looked down at the crate. It contained small pots of ivy, several compact plants with silver leaves, and a bundle of wooden markers.

“Are these for the pub?” Ron asked.

“Most of them,” Neville said. “There are a few for here. I was just going to put them under cover.”

Harry looked towards the low shelter against the garden wall. The distance was only a few yards.

“We can help with that,” Harry said.



It was a small job, made smaller by four people.

Neville handed out the pots and showed them where to stand them beneath the shelter. Hagrid carried most of the crate in one hand. Ron took charge of the wooden markers and immediately asked whether any were liable to object to being put down.

“Only if you put them in the wrong pots,” Neville replied.

Ron paused.

“Was that a joke?” Ron asked.

Neville smiled. “Yes.”

“Good,” Ron said. “I like to check with plants.”

Harry lifted a pot of ivy. Its leaves were cool against his wrist, and a fine thread of water ran from the drainage hole onto his shoe. He set it beside two others, turning it so that the longest stem lay safely along the shelf.

Neville moved between them with quiet certainty. He no longer had the hurried manner Harry remembered from crowded classrooms. He looked at each plant, adjusted a leaf caught beneath a rim, and knew where everything would be happiest overnight.

“Do you do much of this?” Harry asked.

“A bit,” Neville replied. “Teaching takes most of my time. But people are more willing to ask questions here.”

“Than at school?” Harry said.

Neville nodded. “Some adults are embarrassed about not knowing things. They think everyone else has somehow learnt them already.”

Ron set down the markers.

“I’ve been assuming that about taxes for years,” Ron said.

“Plants are fairly forgiving,” Neville replied. “You can usually see what they need if you look often enough.”

Harry thought about the small patch of garden at home which he mostly left to itself. He knew where the children’s ball tended to lodge, and which step stayed damp after rain. Beyond that, he paid it less attention than it deserved.

He carried another pot across the path.

“What would you put somewhere shady?” Harry asked.

Neville’s face brightened, and for the next few minutes Harry learnt rather more about ferns than he had expected to learn that evening.



Once the plants were sheltered, Neville covered the open bag of compost and gathered the tools.

Harry reached for the trowel at the same moment as Ron. Their hands knocked together, and Ron withdrew his with exaggerated dignity.

“Years of friendship,” Ron said, “and now we’re competing for a trowel.”

“You can have it,” Harry replied.

“I don’t want it now,” Ron said. “It’s become awkward.”

Neville took it from between them and put it in the bucket.

“There,” Neville said. “Nobody’s won.”

Hagrid chuckled, a low sound that seemed to settle into the garden wall.

They checked the bed before leaving. Neville pressed the unplanted bulbs into a shallow tray and slid it beneath the bench, away from any curious birds.

“Will those be all right till morning?” Harry asked.

“Quite all right,” Neville replied. “I only stopped because I’d run out of light. There’s no hurry.”

Harry found he liked hearing that said aloud.

Hagrid lowered the lantern and extinguished it with a careful movement of his wand. The garden slipped into the softer light from the lane. Its shapes remained: the pale bench slat, the line of the wall, the dark leaves under their shelter.

Neville tucked the repaired gloves back into the paper.

“I’d better give these to Rosmerta,” Neville said. “She’s been saying she’ll use the oven gloves if necessary.”

“Don’t let her,” Ron replied. “They’ll be in the shepherd’s pie by Sunday.”

Hagrid took one last look at the barrow strap, evidently satisfied with its position, and they walked back towards the main street.

Harry fell into step beside Neville. Their shoulders brushed once in the narrow lane, and both moved aside at the same time, making Ron laugh behind them.



The pub had grown quieter.

Two tables were empty, and the scarf by the fire had dried enough to fold itself into a loose square. Rosmerta was polishing glasses behind the bar.

“Found them a home?” Rosmerta asked as they came in.

Neville placed the parcel on the counter.

“Yes,” Neville said. “They’re yours.”

Rosmerta put down the glass.

“Well,” Rosmerta said. “I ought to have recognised them.”

“You only tried them once,” Neville replied. “And the cuffs look different now.”

Rosmerta unfolded the paper and slipped her hands into the gloves. She opened and closed her fingers, then held both hands out in front of her.

“Oh, that’s comfortable,” Rosmerta said.

Hagrid watched with open pleasure.

“Thumb all right?” Hagrid asked.

Rosmerta bent it. “Much better.”

Harry saw her run one gloved fingertip over the patch in the opposite palm. The movement was small and approving.

“I’d forgotten how light they were,” Rosmerta said. “I expected gardening gloves to feel like something you wore for carrying hot bricks.”

“Some do,” Neville replied. “Those have had plenty of practice.”

Ron leaned against the bar.

“We walked all the way down the lane to discover we should have stayed here,” Ron said.

“You found Neville,” Rosmerta pointed out.

“And we moved some plants,” Harry added.

“And I learnt something about shade,” Ron said. “Apparently it isn’t a complete excuse.”

Rosmerta laughed and drew a fresh round of drinks. Neville tried to protest, but she waved him towards their table.

“For the gloves,” Rosmerta said. “And for getting me started.”



They sat together for another half-hour.

Neville explained what would go into the pub’s planting boxes: ivy to trail over the edges, silver foliage that would hold its colour, and bulbs hidden beneath the surface for spring.

Rosmerta listened while she folded the brown paper.

“So they’ll look finished tomorrow,” Rosmerta said, “and then something else will happen later?”

“Exactly,” Neville replied.

“That seems a sensible arrangement,” Rosmerta said.

She laid the gloves on a shelf behind the bar, where she would see them in the morning.

Harry watched her put them there. The shelf held an order book, a tin of spare tap fittings, and a small vase with a single late flower in it. The gloves looked at home among them.

Hagrid began asking Neville about something eating the lower leaves of his cabbages. Ron, who had initially appeared willing to follow this discussion, gradually became absorbed in peeling the label from an empty bottle.

Harry let the conversation flow round him. He had no contribution to make about cabbages, and nobody expected one.

A fresh log settled in the fire. From upstairs came the muffled tread of a guest crossing a room. Rosmerta went to answer a quiet call from the far table, leaving the folded paper beside Neville’s elbow.

“Worth mending those,” Hagrid said at last.

Neville nodded. “I thought so.”

Harry remembered the curve of the leather fingers and the new thread round the thumb. Tomorrow, Rosmerta would pull the gloves on, perhaps a little self-consciously at first, and fill the boxes outside her own back door.

He found himself looking forward to seeing them.



Hagrid was the first to leave.

He had an early start, and the walk to his cottage would take longer if he stopped to inspect every damp movement in the verge, as Ron suspected he might.

“Just worms, probably,” Hagrid said.

“That’s what I’m afraid of,” Ron replied. “You’ll want to make sure they’re all comfortable.”

Hagrid gave Ron’s shoulder an affectionate squeeze that lowered him slightly in his chair.

“See yeh tomorrow, Harry,” Hagrid said. “Come down if you’ve time after your meeting.”

“I will,” Harry promised.

Neville left with Hagrid, carrying the empty crate. At the door, Neville turned back.

“Ten o’clock, Rosmerta?” Neville asked.

“I’ll be ready,” Rosmerta replied. “With the proper gloves.”

When the door closed behind them, Ron stretched his legs beneath the table.

“Good evening,” Ron said.

Harry looked at him.

“It was,” Harry agreed.

“Even with the gardening,” Ron added.

They lingered over the last of their drinks. Ron spoke about catching the train home the next day, then remembered an order he had meant to check and wrote himself a note on the back of an old receipt.

Harry resisted the impulse to take out his meeting papers. There would be time enough in the morning. He already knew what he thought about most of them.

Eventually Ron stood and buttoned his coat.

“I’d better go before George decides I’ve joined the branch permanently,” Ron said.

Harry walked him to the door. The village beyond was almost still, with pools of light beneath the lamps and a little mist gathering where the road dipped.

Ron lifted a hand, then set off towards the place where he could Apparate. Harry watched until his friend had passed the last shop window, his long shadow folding across the pavement.



Upstairs, Harry’s room was warm.

A fire had been laid in the small grate, and his bag stood on a wooden chair. Beside the bed, a jug of water wore a knitted cover that stirred faintly whenever the room cooled.

Harry shut the door and stood for a moment without moving.

The quiet here had a different texture from the quiet downstairs. There was no conversation running beneath it, only the occasional click from the fire and the soft sound of water passing through an old pipe.

He took off his coat and hung it on a peg.

The folder of papers lay where he had left it. Harry moved it to the table, squared its edges, and put his glasses beside it. Then he changed into his nightclothes and drew back the bedcover.

Through the window he could see a narrow piece of the street. The pub sign moved slightly in the night air. Beyond it, the roofs climbed towards darkness, and somewhere past them lay the lane and its little garden.

Harry pictured the plants standing close together under the shelter. He remembered Neville turning a pot so a stem would not be crushed, and Hagrid looking pleased at the fit of a repaired strap. Rosmerta’s gloves waited on their shelf.

In the morning, Harry thought, he might ask to borrow a trowel before going up to the castle. There would be room for one more person beside the planting boxes, and he was fairly sure he could manage ivy.

He lay down and felt the mattress give beneath him. The linen was cool at first, then warmed along his legs.

Downstairs, a chair moved across the floor. A door clicked shut. Harry could no longer distinguish the words of the last conversation at the bar, only its low, companionable rise and fall.

His thoughts drifted towards home, towards familiar rooms and faces, then loosened into impressions of lamplight, soft leather and the smell of earth.

The fire settled gently in the grate.

Before the room had quite fallen silent, Harry was asleep.
