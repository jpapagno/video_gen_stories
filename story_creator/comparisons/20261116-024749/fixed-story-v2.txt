The Last Light at Alderwick

On the first Thursday of November, Mara Bell found a small brass key in a packet of lavender.

She was unpacking the linen cupboard at Alderwick School of Practical Enchantment, where she had recently taken the post of keeper of household spells. The packet had slipped behind a stack of pillowcases. Its paper was soft with age, and someone had written on it, in brown ink:

For the lamp that has forgotten its window.

Mara turned the key over in her palm.

It had a round bow, a short stem and three uneven teeth. A scrap of blue wool was tied through it. There was nothing else in the packet except lavender stems, dry enough to break at a touch.

Outside the cupboard, the school was settling into its evening habits. Slippers travelled in pairs towards the dormitories. A tea trolley waited patiently beside a closed classroom door. Somewhere below, the great kitchen clock struck seven and then, after consideration, struck once more.

Mara put the packet in her cardigan pocket.

She had spent most of her working life mending enchantments in other people’s houses. Now, at fifty-eight, she had a room of her own beneath the school’s western eaves and responsibility for everything from the warming stones in the bathrooms to the charm that persuaded the library curtains to close.

She liked the work. She liked knowing that a tap ran warm because she had attended to it, and that the bed linen smelled faintly of summer even in November.

But the school was old, and she was new. She still sometimes took the long way to breakfast because a passage had looked unfamiliar, or found herself thanking a portrait that had merely cleared its throat.

A small task might help her learn the place.

She would find the lamp, if it was still there, and discover which window it belonged in.



The next morning brought rain of a patient, soaking kind.

It silvered the leaded panes and gathered on the broad leaves outside the breakfast room. The school stood above the village of Lower Alder, with its oldest buildings arranged around a courtyard containing a well, a pear tree and a stone bench that remained dry in every weather.

Mara had porridge, stewed apples and two cups of tea. Then she took the key to the kitchen.

Tamsin Vale, the head cook, was making pastry at a long elm table. A bowl beside her turned slowly, folding butter into flour with a wooden spoon.

“A lamp?” she said. “We’ve plenty. Most of them know exactly where they belong, particularly if you try moving them.”

Mara showed her the note.

Tamsin wiped her fingers and brought it close to the light.

“That looks like Missus Wren’s writing. She kept the household spells before you. Lovely woman. Could get a stain out of anything, though she said some stains ought to stay. Particularly on aprons.”

“Did you know her well?”

“Well enough to miss her marmalade. She went to live with her daughter by the sea, twelve years ago. I think she meant to leave everything in order, but her daughter’s youngest was arriving earlier than expected.”

Tamsin returned the paper.

“Try the old workroom. We keep spare lamps there, behind the drying racks.”

The workroom lay beyond the laundry, where warm air carried the clean, mineral smell of wet wool and soap. Shirts hung motionless while small currents of air travelled through their sleeves.

Mara found the lamps on a shelf above a basket of curtain rings. There were reading lamps with green shades, a pair of silver candlesticks, three floating globes asleep in a bowl, and a squat lantern made of cloudy glass.

Behind them stood a lamp no taller than a teapot.

Its base was blue pottery. A brass door curved around the little chamber where a flame ought to have been. Above it, a milk-white shade was painted with sprigs of rosemary.

Mara tried the key.

It turned with a soft, well-oiled click.

Inside lay a folded piece of paper and a pale disc of something resembling beeswax. There was no wick.

The paper read:

Clean glass. A little warmth. A window with something to wait for.

Underneath, in smaller writing, someone had added:

Ask Elian about the colour.

Mara carried the lamp into the laundry light. A grey thumbprint marked the shade. Dust had gathered beneath the brass rim.

She washed it carefully with warm water, dried it on a soft cloth and polished the little door.

The wax-like disc grew faintly golden.

For a moment, the rosemary leaves on the shade looked freshly painted.



Elian Reed worked in the school greenhouse, where November was permitted to enter only as far as the porch.

Inside, the air smelled of damp earth, orange peel and the peppery leaves of winter stock. Rain tapped overhead. A row of seed trays shuffled sideways to give Mara room to set down her lamp.

Elian was tying a tomato vine to a cane. He was a narrow man in his seventies, with spectacles that enlarged his eyes and a habit of keeping string in every pocket.

“Missus Wren’s lamp,” he said at once.

“You remember it.”

“I remember being told not to put it in the greenhouse. Plants have quite enough opinions about daylight.”

Mara read him the second note.

“Ah. The colour.”

He finished his knot, tested it and came over.

“My mother called that sort of flame a hearthlight. They used to be common round here. Very economical. No oil, no smoke, and they don’t mind a draught.”

“How do you light one?”

“Well, warmth wakes it. But it needs to borrow its colour.”

“From what?”

“Something particular to the place it belongs. I don’t think it much matters what, provided somebody remembers why it’s there.”

Mara considered this.

“That sounds rather imprecise.”

“It is. Old domestic magic often is. The instructions were mostly passed between people who already knew what they were doing.”

He rubbed a little condensation from his spectacles.

“The one in my mother’s kitchen borrowed its colour from a copper kettle. My father polished it on Sundays. When the kettle finally wore through, the lamp carried on shining exactly the same.”

He held the lamp between his hands. Slowly, light gathered at its centre: a faint cream glow, scarcely brighter than the rainy morning.

Then it faded.

“It’s willing,” he said.

“Do you know which window Missus Wren used?”

“No. She was always moving things to wherever they were useful.”

He went to a shelf and brought back a small pot of rosemary.

“For company. There’s an old variety in there, the same as the painting.”

Mara accepted it.

On her way out, she paused beside a tray of tiny green shoots. Each wore a bead of water at its tip. Behind her, Elian began humming while he tied the next vine, and the rain continued its soft work on the glass.



Mara spent the afternoon attending to ordinary things.

A draught in the music room turned out to be coming from a ventilation charm that had misunderstood the word bracing. The bathwater on the third floor needed persuading that lukewarm was not a satisfactory ambition. In the west dormitory, a blanket had acquired the habit of tucking itself around the bedposts.

She mended what she could, made notes about the rest, and took the lamp with her.

At each window, she set it down briefly.

The music room looked across the playing lawn towards the hills. The third-floor landing offered a fine view of the village roofs. Her own room faced a row of chimneys and, beyond them, the dark line of an orchard.

The lamp remained unlit.

By four o’clock, Mara began to feel that she was asking it too often. She put it on her desk beside the rosemary and left it alone while she answered letters.

There were three: a request for new boot-drying charms, an invitation to the winter staff supper, and a note from her sister describing the determined progress of a climbing rose.

Mara wrote back about the school. She mentioned the kitchen clock, the obliging tea trolley and her room beneath the eaves.

She started a sentence about the lamp, then stopped.

Instead, she wrote, I am learning where everything goes.

After supper, she took her tea to the small staff sitting room.

Only the librarian was there. Ivo Pell sat by the fire, repairing the spine of an atlas. A spool of green thread hovered at his shoulder.

When Mara explained the lamp, he smiled.

“Have you tried the household ledgers?”

“I didn’t know there were any.”

“There are always ledgers. The difficulty is finding the one that admits to being useful.”

He laid down the atlas.

“Come tomorrow. We’ll see what Missus Wren thought worth recording.”



The library opened at nine, but Ivo let Mara in at half past eight.

Morning light rested on the long tables. The books were still quiet, apart from a dictionary settling an argument with a thesaurus in the reference section.

They found the household ledgers in a cupboard labelled Temporary Records, which contained volumes going back two hundred years.

Missus Wren had written in a neat, sloping hand.

Most entries were practical.

South stair carpets shortened by three inches. Cause unknown. Length restored.

Kitchen warming shelf now suitable for dough. Previously overenthusiastic.

Four dozen pillow comforts refreshed before examinations.

Mara turned pages while Ivo fetched coffee.

Then, between a receipt for beeswax and a list of replacement window catches, she found a brief entry.

November the sixth. Blue hearthlight returned to west passage. Borrowed the colour again from Fen’s bakery. Must remember to thank him.

Below it:

November the eighth. Ada says she could see it from the bend.

Mara read both lines twice.

“The west passage,” she said.

“There are three,” Ivo replied. “Depending on the period of construction, and how much the architect trusted his compass.”

“Who was Ada?”

“Let’s have a look.”

He consulted a register from the same year. His finger moved slowly down the page.

“Ada Sedge. Evening laundress. Lived in Lower Alder. Retired the following spring.”

“Would she have walked home?”

“Most people did. There was a footpath through the orchard then.”

“Isn’t there still?”

“Possibly. I haven’t had reason to use it for some time.”

He looked towards the window, where the orchard lay beyond the rain-dark lawn.

“You could ask in the village. Somebody will remember Ada.”

Mara copied the entries onto the back of an envelope.

As she closed the ledger, a pressed rosemary leaf slipped from between the pages and landed beside her hand.

She left it where it had rested, carefully tucked against the seam.



On Saturday, Mara walked down to Lower Alder.

The rain had passed, leaving the air mild and the roads dark. Water dripped from the hedges. Here and there, an autumn leaf lifted itself from a blocked drain and shuffled politely into the verge.

The village occupied a fold in the valley. Its houses were built of pale stone, with steep roofs and small front gardens. An enchanted broom swept the post office step. Outside the cobbler’s, a pair of newly mended boots practised walking on a low wooden platform.

Mara had been down twice before, for groceries and a new button. This time she took the lane slowly.

Fen’s bakery stood beside the bridge.

Its windows were misted at the edges, and the door opened with the sound of a small bell. Inside, the warmth smelled of yeast, browned butter and currants.

A broad-shouldered woman was setting loaves on a shelf.

“My father was Fen,” she said when Mara asked. “I’m Nessa. Though everyone still says Fen’s, and I’ve stopped trying to improve on it.”

Mara placed the lamp on the counter.

Nessa’s expression softened.

“Well, I haven’t seen that in years.”

“Missus Wren borrowed its colour from here.”

“Yes. From the oven, when we took the last bread out.”

“Would that be difficult to do?”

“Not at all. You’ll need to stay till half past twelve.”

Mara glanced at the clock. It was barely eleven.

Nessa pointed towards a little table by the window.

“There’s tea. And if you’re staying, you can tell me whether the school still serves those dreadful orange biscuits.”

“They do. I rather like them.”

“Then I shall give you something better to compare.”

She brought tea and a small bun split with butter.

While Mara ate, customers came and went. A man collected a pie wrapped in a cloth that would keep it warm. Two children chose a loaf for their grandmother after serious discussion of crusts. An elderly woman asked for yesterday’s bread and received a fresh loaf with a damaged corner at the same price.

At half past twelve, Nessa opened the oven.

The last batch stood inside: six round loaves, their tops the colour of hazelnuts.

“Set it there.”

Mara put the lamp on a stool facing the open oven.

Heat brushed her cheeks. Somewhere in the lamp, a tiny sound rang out, clear as a spoon touching the rim of a glass.

The pale disc inside became amber.

Light rose through it, filling the rosemary shade with a soft golden glow.

Nessa watched with folded arms.

“That’s it.”

The lamp shone steadily for several seconds. Then its light drew inward, leaving a small warm point at the centre.

“Has it gone out?”

“Saving it, I expect. My father said they were sensible things.”

Mara bought a loaf and thanked her.

“Do you know Ada Sedge?”

“Ada’s my aunt. She lives in the last cottage up Mill Lane. Blue gate. Take her one of these, will you?”

Nessa added a currant bun to the bag.



Ada’s cottage had a blue gate, a gravel path and a little porch crowded with pots.

She answered before Mara knocked twice.

“Nessa saw you coming?” she asked, looking at the bakery bag.

“Not exactly.”

“Then you’d better come in and explain.”

Ada was small and straight-backed, with white hair pinned above ears that appeared never to have missed anything worth hearing.

Her sitting room smelled of furniture polish and the faint sweetness of apples stored somewhere cool. A cat occupied an armchair beside the hearth. Ada removed a knitting basket from the other chair and waved Mara into it.

The lamp stood between them on a low table.

“I used to look for that,” Ada said.

“On your way home?”

“On my way up, mostly.”

She poured tea from a brown pot.

“In winter, I started after the afternoon lessons. I helped with the laundry and stayed till the last lot was folded. The orchard path was the quickest way from this end of the village.”

“Was the lamp there to light it?”

“A little. You couldn’t see much by it until you were nearly at the door. But once you came round the bend, you knew the kettle would be on.”

Ada smiled into her cup.

“Missus Wren took her break when I arrived. We had tea in the mending room. Ten minutes, usually. Sometimes fifteen if there was a good deal to tell.”

Mara looked down at the blue base.

“So it was her lamp.”

“I suppose it belonged to the school. But she looked after it.”

“Which window was it in?”

“The low one beside the orchard door. There was a rosemary bush underneath. I used to catch my apron on it.”

Mara knew the door. It was in an unused passage beyond the old mending room, where spare desks were stored. She had assumed the ground outside was simply part of the orchard.

“Do you still go up to the school?”

“Not often. My knees prefer the village now. But my granddaughter does. Nell. She started helping Elian three afternoons a week.”

“How does she get there?”

“Round by the main drive. A long way, if you ask me. She says the orchard gate is stuck.”

Mara made a quiet note of that.

Ada cut the bun in half and put one portion on Mara’s saucer.

“Are you happy up there?”

The question was kindly put, without ceremony.

“I think I shall be,” Mara said.

Ada nodded as though this were a perfectly satisfactory answer.



On Monday, Mara went to the orchard door.

The passage was chilly but dry. Old desks stood along one wall, and a tall cupboard held jars of buttons, lengths of braid and a hat that appeared to have been awaiting collection for several decades.

At the end was a low window, deep-set in the stone.

Beyond it, the orchard sloped towards a hedge. Most of the apples had been gathered. A few remained on the highest branches, bright against the grey sky.

Mara tried the door.

It opened readily enough. Fallen leaves had gathered on the step, and beneath the window a rosemary bush leaned away from the wall.

She followed a faint line through the grass.

The path was still there, surfaced with small flat stones. It passed between two apple trees and curved towards a gate. From the bend, she could see the window clearly.

The gate itself had dropped on its hinges.

Mara fetched her tool bag.

She was used to small repairs. First she cleared the earth beneath the bottom rail. Then she tightened the hinge screws and asked the wood, quietly, to remember its proper shape.

The gate rose half an inch.

She tried the latch. It clicked neatly.

By then her hands were cold, and there was mud on one cuff. She stood for a moment looking down the path towards the village, where smoke rose almost straight from the chimneys.

It was a good way to come to work.

Back inside, she washed the window. The glass needed doing twice. She cleared the sill, swept the floor and moved the nearest desks far enough back to make a comfortable passage.

Then she placed the lamp beside the pane.

Its little amber centre brightened.

Light reached the painted rosemary, hesitated there, and sank again.

Mara sat on a desk and considered the instructions.

Clean glass. A little warmth. A window with something to wait for.

She had the right window. She had the colour. The room itself was cold enough to explain the rest.

She fetched a warming stone from the workroom and set it beneath the sill.

The lamp glowed politely, but no more brightly than before.



Mara left it there for the afternoon.

She had work to do, including a troublesome cupboard in the infirmary that kept arranging bandages by length instead of width.

At four, she went to the kitchen for tea.

“Find your window?” Tamsin asked.

“Yes. Beside the orchard door. I’ve opened the path again.”

“Oh, good. Nell will be pleased. She comes in with half the weather on her after that walk round the drive.”

“Does she finish late?”

“Half past five today. Elian’s sorting the winter bulbs.”

Mara watched Tamsin put two thick slices of bread beside the stove to toast.

“Would there be a small kettle I could borrow?”

“Of course. What for?”

“The old mending room. I thought it might be nice to have somewhere to stop when people come in that way.”

Tamsin turned the bread.

“There’s a whole tea tray in the cupboard. Take the brown cups. They’re comfortable to hold.”

The mending room had a small fireplace, a table and two chairs. Mara opened the flue, laid a modest fire and dusted the mantelpiece. She found a third chair in the passage and a cushion in the linen store.

It was pleasant work. She put the kettle beside the hearth and arranged the cups.

At a quarter past five, Elian appeared in the doorway with soil on his knees.

“Tamsin said there might be tea.”

“There will be in a moment.”

“Nell’s just finishing the labels.”

He looked around.

“I’d forgotten this was a room.”

“So had everyone, I think.”

He settled into a chair and held his hands towards the fire.

Through the open doorway, Mara could see the lamp at the window. Its glow had strengthened a little.

She put three teaspoons of tea into the pot.

Outside, footsteps sounded on the path.

A young woman in a knitted hat came round the bend carrying an empty bulb basket. She paused when she saw the window, then lifted one hand.

The lamp filled with gold.

It happened so quietly that Mara almost missed the change. Light spread through the shade and rested along the sill, warm and steady.

Nell came in, bringing the scent of cold leaves.

“You’ve fixed the gate.”

“Yes,” Mara said. “Come and have some tea.”



After that, the room found its use gradually.

Nell stopped there before walking home. Elian left a pair of clean shoes beneath a chair. Tamsin came through one afternoon with a plate of biscuits and stayed long enough to complain comfortably about a delivery of undersized onions.

Mara kept the kettle filled.

She did not make a rule about it. Some evenings no one came, and she drank her tea alone, listening to the fire and watching the orchard darken.

The lamp shone just the same.

On Friday, Ada visited with Nessa, having accepted a lift in the baker’s little cart. Nell met them at the gate.

Mara saw the three of them from the window: Ada taking her granddaughter’s arm, Nessa carrying a covered plate, all of them pausing where the path curved.

By the time they reached the door, Mara had warmed the pot.

Ada stood a moment in the mending room.

“Well,” she said, taking off her gloves. “This is better than spare desks.”

They had tea and apple cake. Elian brought in a late chrysanthemum in a narrow jar. Ivo arrived with the household ledger, having decided that a useful restoration deserved an entry.

Mara wrote the date, then:

Blue hearthlight cleaned and returned to the orchard window. Gate repaired. Tea things put back in the mending room.

She paused before adding:

Colour borrowed from Fen’s bakery, with thanks.

Ada read it over her shoulder.

“That will do nicely.”



That night, Mara waited until the cups were washed and the fire had settled into a red glow.

The others had gone. She had watched Ada and Nessa down the path, their lantern swinging gently between them. Nell had waved from the gate before following.

Now the orchard lay still beyond the glass.

Mara touched the lamp’s brass door. It was warm beneath her fingers.

She left it burning.

Upstairs, the school had grown quiet. The tea trolley stood in its alcove. A folded blanket waited outside the linen cupboard, having apparently found its own way back. Mara picked it up and put it on the shelf.

In her room, she set the brass key beside the pot of rosemary.

She washed, drew the curtains and turned down the bed. For a little while she sat in her nightdress, writing to her sister.

I have found a nice place for tea, she wrote. You must come and see it when you visit.

There was more she could have said, but it would keep.

She laid down her pen.

The warming charm had taken the chill from the sheets. Mara slipped beneath them and settled her head into the pillow, which smelled faintly of lavender.

Somewhere below, a door closed softly. The old kitchen clock sounded the hour, counted correctly, and was still.

Mara listened until the last note had faded.

Then she turned onto her side, let the day loosen its hold, and fell asleep.
